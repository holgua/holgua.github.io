PayPal: The Bet Still Works

The Bottom Line

The bet on PayPal is intact. Three of its five conditions are clearly working — the payments network is still growing, the buyback programme is running at pace, and the consumer credit quality that raised alarm bells a year ago has improved markedly. Two conditions carry a yellow flag rather than a green one: operating margins dipped in the most recent quarter due to a shift in the product mix, and the metric that matters most for the long-run thesis — branded checkout share — went unreported in the latest filing, leaving that strand of the story temporarily unreadable. None of that crosses any of the pre-set lines for action. The share price is in a neutral zone, neither cheap enough to add nor expensive enough to trim. The one condition to watch is the branded checkout share, which a recently filed lawsuit has put a spotlight on.

About PayPal

PayPal runs a two-sided digital payments network: 439 million active accounts spread across roughly 200 countries, processing $1.79 trillion in payments during the 2025 fiscal year. For every transaction that flows through the network — a consumer paying for something online, a merchant receiving the funds — PayPal takes a small percentage. That percentage, earned across a staggering volume of transactions, is the engine of the business.

The network has two distinctive layers. The consumer side is what most people know: the PayPal wallet, Venmo (the peer-to-peer payments app common among younger users), and PayPal's buy-now-pay-later product. The merchant side is less visible but increasingly important: Braintree, the payment infrastructure that powers checkout for many large retailers and platforms, runs behind the scenes on a large and growing share of the total volume. Braintree's growth has been the dominant driver of recent volume expansion, though it earns a lower margin per dollar than PayPal's branded checkout products — a distinction that matters for understanding the quarterly results.

PayPal holds money-transmitter licences across all fifty US states, a credit-institution authorisation from Luxembourg (covering EU operations), and a Financial Conduct Authority authorisation in the United Kingdom. These licences took years to accumulate and cannot be replicated quickly; they are a genuine structural barrier to entry that Apple Pay, Google Pay, and even Stripe cannot sidestep by shipping software.

The business went through a difficult period from 2021 to 2023: over-ambitious expansion, margin compression, and three chief executive changes in five years. The current phase is deliberately narrower — focusing on profitable engagement over account growth, cost discipline, and returning cash to shareholders via buybacks. The results are visible in the numbers: operating profit margins have expanded from 13.9% in the 2022 fiscal year to 18.3% in 2025, and the share count has fallen by 21% over the same period as buybacks have accelerated.

The new chief executive, Enrique Lores, took the role in March 2026. He comes from Hewlett-Packard, where he ran a large global technology company, but he has no prior operating experience in payments or financial services. He has been a board member since 2021 and board chair since mid-2024, which means he is not arriving cold; whether that translates into a smooth continuation of the financial transformation already under way is the central execution question of the next year.

The Case for the Bet

The central idea is that PayPal is being priced as though its earnings will shrink for years, when the numbers suggest the opposite. The business generates more than five billion dollars a year in cash above its operating costs, is returning the bulk of it to shareholders at prices that imply the market thinks the franchise is in long-run decline, and is doing so through a structural disruption in how payments work at the consumer level. The bet is not that PayPal wins the contest for consumer checkout dominance — it is that even if that contest goes against it, the buyback engine at these prices compounds per-share value fast enough to produce a good return.

Five conditions must keep holding for that bet to remain valid:

  1. Branded checkout share loss must stay below two percentage points in any single fiscal year. A loss beyond that threshold would signal that consumers are structurally shifting away — not just drifting — and would indicate the moat is eroding faster than buybacks can compensate.

  2. No enforcement action from the US consumer finance regulator or the Department of Justice that results in a formal agreement restricting PayPal's core products. Regulatory scrutiny is normal and ongoing; a consent order with operational restrictions is a different matter.

  3. Consumer credit losses must stay below 10% on an annualised basis. PayPal extends credit to consumers through its buy-now-pay-later products; losses above that threshold, without a clear recovery path disclosed by management, would suggest the credit underwriting has broken down.

  4. Management must continue to return capital via buybacks and must not take on meaningful new debt. The threshold is a net debt level above 2.0 times annual operating profit before interest, depreciation, and amortisation. Taking on debt of that scale without a compelling strategic reason would suggest the buyback discipline has been abandoned and that something has shifted internally.

  5. Annual operating profit must not fall below $5.5 billion, and management must not publicly guide the following year's adjusted operating profit below $5.0 billion. These are the signals that the margin recovery has reversed — not softened, but structurally reversed.

The Rules

The rules translate the combination of thesis state and share price into a specific instruction. They were set in advance of any particular quarterly result:

  • If the thesis is fully intact and the share price is in a neutral zone (neither cheap nor expensive): hold. No action.

  • If any of the five conditions above begins to deteriorate — showing weakness but not yet breaching a named threshold: do not add. Wait for two consecutive quarters of confirming weakness before treating it as a trend rather than noise.

  • If any named exit condition fires: exit the full position within thirty days. No averaging down, no waiting for a recovery.

  • If the share price falls to $41 (the cheap edge of the valuation band) and the thesis is intact: add in tranches over one to two quarters.

  • If the share price rises to $60 (the expensive edge of the valuation band): trim a quarter to a third of the position to harvest the premium, while retaining the core holding.

  • If the share price rises to $78 (the extreme edge): exit the full position. At that level, the market is pricing in roughly eleven years of base-case earnings compounding instantly; the implied forward return falls below what equities should earn over the long run, and reallocation becomes the better choice.

The Conditions Today

Branded checkout volume. This condition is the main uncertainty right now. The most recent quarterly filing — covering the three months ending in March 2026 — does not separately disclose branded checkout payment volume. Total payment volume across the network grew 11% compared to the same quarter a year earlier, but that aggregate figure is dominated by Braintree's growth. The branded checkout metric that the thesis specifically tracks was absent from the filing, making it impossible to assess this condition directly from the latest regulatory document.

Total payment volume and network scale. The network is still growing. Total payment volume rose 11% in the first quarter of 2026, driven by Braintree, the main PayPal branded product, and Venmo. Payment transactions grew 7% to 6.5 billion. The premise that the network is processing more value — even if its growth rate has moderated — is holding.

Buyback discipline. This is the clearest positive. PayPal repurchased 34 million shares in the first quarter of 2026 at a cost of $1.5 billion, with an average price of $44.60 per share. That pace — roughly $6 billion a year at current prices — has reduced the share count by 21% since 2022. The remaining authorisation stands at $12.4 billion, enough to fund several more years of buybacks at current prices.

Operating margin. Mixed, but not alarming. The full 2025 fiscal year delivered operating profit of $6.1 billion — 14% above the year before, and well above the $5.5 billion exit threshold. The first quarter of 2026, however, saw operating profit slip to $1.49 billion (an 18% margin) from $1.53 billion (20%) a year earlier. The cause is product mix: Braintree transactions carry a higher expense rate than the branded checkout products, so as Braintree grows faster, the blended margin compresses. Management disclosed this dynamic explicitly, and the annualised run-rate from the first quarter still implies roughly $5.95 billion for the full year — above the threshold.

Consumer credit quality. The best piece of news in the latest filing. The net charge-off rate on consumer loans — the share of loans that go unrecovered in a given period, expressed as an annual rate — improved to 3.0% in the first quarter of 2026, down from 4.5% in the same quarter a year earlier. Net charge-offs in dollar terms fell to $74 million from $78 million. Both figures are well inside the 10% exit-condition threshold. The consumer loan portfolio of $5.4 billion (after accounting for sold participations) is 96.3% current.

Regulatory and legal status. The consumer finance regulator has issued two active investigative demands — one covering payment transfer regulations and one covering the PayPal Credit product. Neither has resulted in enforcement action or a consent order. A securities class action filed in February 2026 (the Darcy case) alleges that management failed to disclose impediments to the branded checkout growth strategy; the case is at the pleadings stage and does not constitute confirmed evidence of exit condition (1). A prior securities class action was dismissed with prejudice in March 2026. The net regulatory picture is mixed, but no named exit condition has been triggered.

The Share Price

At $42.49, PayPal's share price sits in the neutral zone — above the $41 buy-signal line and below the $60 trim-signal line. The valuation is based on owner earnings yield: how much cash the business generates for every dollar of market value. At the current price and using 2025 fiscal-year owner earnings of roughly $4.5 billion (after adjusting for stock-based compensation), the yield is just under 12% — meaningfully below the 12.5% level that arms the buy signal, and well above the 8.5% level that arms the trim signal.

The fair-value anchor is $48 — derived from a yield of 10.5%, the equilibrium level embedded in the thesis. The current price is 12% below that anchor, meaning the shares are modestly cheap by the thesis's own internal standard, but not cheap enough to trigger active buying.

For context on what the market is implying: at $42, the share price requires owner earnings to shrink by roughly 5% to 7% per year for a decade before stabilising, just to justify the current valuation on a zero-real-return basis. The business grew operating profit by 14% last year. The gap between what the market is pricing and what the numbers are showing is the source of the thesis's remaining margin of safety.

What's Wobbling

Two conditions are in a yellow state rather than a green one, and one carries an active legal signal worth watching.

Branded checkout share: unreadable this quarter. The most important single metric for the thesis — the share of PayPal-branded transactions at checkout, as opposed to Braintree back-end processing — was not separately disclosed in the March 2026 quarterly filing. The thesis calls for exiting if branded checkout share falls by more than two percentage points in a single fiscal year; that threshold cannot be assessed from the filing itself.

The reason this yellow flag carries more weight than a simple data gap: a securities class action filed in February 2026 alleges that management knew of "impediments to its branded checkout growth strategy" that it did not disclose publicly. The lawsuit is at the pleadings stage and proves nothing on its own, but it is a signpost. The disclosure of branded checkout metrics in the next quarterly report — and in the earnings call accompanying it — will be the decisive read. If the data shows branded checkout share loss exceeding two percentage points on a full-year basis, the exit condition fires. If the data shows stability or recovery, the yellow flag clears.

Operating margin: dipped but not broken. The first-quarter 2026 operating margin came in at 18%, two percentage points below the same quarter a year earlier. The cause — a higher share of Braintree's lower-margin volume in the mix — is disclosed and explicable. The full-year 2025 figure of 18.3% is above the critical $5.5 billion operating profit threshold, and the first-quarter 2026 annualised run-rate of roughly $5.95 billion is also above it. This is a condition to watch rather than a condition in trouble.

Active account growth: plateauing. The network has 439 million active accounts, up just 1% from the year before. Transactions per active account declined slightly, to 58.7 from 59.4. Neither figure triggers an exit condition — the thesis does not require active account growth, only that the existing base keeps generating enough cash per share through buybacks. But a plateau turning into a decline would become relevant over two or three further quarters.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Branded checkout becomes structurally obsolete. If Apple Pay, device-level defaults, or AI-agent-mediated purchasing gradually make PayPal's consumer-facing checkout irrelevant — not just under pressure but actually routed around — the network's most valuable surface could erode without appearing as a dramatic single event. The timeline for this risk is multi-year and the current numbers do not confirm it, but the direction of travel is visible.

  • A regulator changes the product rules. The consumer finance regulator has two active investigations into PayPal's practices. A change of administration or a legal settlement that requires PayPal to restructure how it handles payment disputes, consumer credit, or transfer rules could increase costs and reduce the product's appeal significantly. The current regulatory environment is relatively favourable; that could change.

  • The CEO transition unsettles the financial discipline. The cost discipline and buyback programme that drive the thesis were built over two to three years of deliberate management effort. Enrique Lores is new to the role and new to payments. If the strategy shifts — towards acquisitions, towards product experimentation that requires spending, or away from aggressive buybacks — the compounding case weakens.

  • Consumer credit deteriorates. Charge-off rates improved significantly in the most recent quarter. If that improvement reverses — if consumers start struggling with the buy-now-pay-later balances, particularly in a recessionary environment — the credit exposure could become material. The current 3% annualised charge-off rate is well below the 10% exit threshold, but the direction matters as much as the level.

  • Agentic commerce bypasses the wallet layer entirely. AI systems that complete purchases autonomously, without presenting a human with a checkout page to interact with, could route transactions through infrastructure that never touches PayPal at all. This is an early-stage risk but a structural one, and the company's own filings acknowledge it.

Gaps in the Picture

The one significant data gap this cycle is the branded checkout metric itself. It is disclosed in earnings call materials and supplementary presentations, not in the quarterly regulatory filing, and the latest filing contains no separate figure. The thesis depends on this metric more than any other single number; the gap cannot be filled from the filing alone and must be resolved from the next earnings cycle's disclosures.

The owner earnings calculation used to derive the valuation bands combines total capital expenditure with net income and depreciation, without separating maintenance spending from growth spending. For a software-heavy platform like PayPal, the true cost of maintaining the existing business is likely lower than total capital expenditure — which means the earnings yield may be modestly understated. The direction of any error is conservative: the true yield is probably somewhat higher than the 12% currently calculated.
