Recent Development

The results covering the three months to March 2026 delivered the clearest across-the-board confirmation the thesis has seen in over a year. Azure grew 40% — matching its pace from the quarter six months earlier and sitting 15 percentage points above the exit-condition floor. Commercial remaining performance obligations (the contracted backlog of future revenue) reached $627 billion, a near-doubling in twelve months, and roughly a quarter of that is scheduled to convert to revenue in the next year alone. Operating profit grew 20% and the margin held at 46%, well above the 42% line that would force an exit review.

Two drivers came back under pressure rather than cleanly positive. The first is the ratio of free cash flow to net income, which ran at 0.48 on a nine-month basis — well below the 0.65 level that would trigger an exit check on an annual basis. The mechanism is straightforward: the company spent $80 billion on property and equipment in the nine months to March 2026, nearly double the same period a year earlier. That spending is not yet at the point where an exit condition fires (the rule requires two consecutive fiscal years below the threshold and no improvement in cloud revenue — neither leg is met), but the trajectory is one to watch into the June 2026 annual results. The second is infrastructure spending itself, running at 33–37% of revenue, which would trigger an exit check if Azure growth simultaneously fell below 25%. It has not, and the backlog data suggests it is unlikely to do so in the near term. Both pressures are detailed below. The overall picture leaves the verdict unchanged: see The Bottom Line.

About Microsoft

Microsoft makes its money in three interlocking ways. The largest is cloud infrastructure: Azure, the company's computing platform, lets businesses run their own software on Microsoft's hardware in one of more than 400 data centres across 70 regions. Azure has grown at roughly 35–40% a year for the past several years, and it has become the centre of Microsoft's bet on artificial intelligence — the company has been building out AI-ready computing capacity at a rate that consumed $65 billion in the year to June 2025. The second is productivity software: the Microsoft 365 suite (which includes Outlook, Word, Excel, Teams, and the newer Copilot AI assistant) is used by hundreds of millions of people through subscriptions, predominantly in corporate settings. A third is a cluster of platform businesses — LinkedIn (the professional network), Xbox (gaming), and Bing (search and advertising) — that together contribute meaningfully but are not the primary growth engine.

The most important structural fact about the business is how difficult it is to leave. When a company uses Microsoft's Azure Active Directory — the system that manages who is allowed to log into which applications — switching away is not a matter of choosing a different supplier. It means simultaneously replacing email, the employee directory, every application's login system, every security policy, and every access-control rule across the entire organisation. Most large enterprises have thousands of such integrations. That switching difficulty is what has allowed the company to sustain returns on invested capital of 22–27% over five years, well above the 8–10% cost of that capital.

The Case for the Bet

Microsoft is held as a platform-layer business whose enterprise lock-in — centred on the identity and login infrastructure that underpins every application an organisation uses — creates a structural cost advantage that compounds over time. The central debate is whether the current surge in AI infrastructure spending permanently compresses cash generation or builds a wider structural position as Azure monetises artificial intelligence at scale. The evidence so far favours the latter. The specific conditions that must keep holding are:

  1. Azure quarterly revenue growth stays above 25% while infrastructure spending runs above 20% of revenue. Both conditions active simultaneously for two consecutive quarters would signal the AI bet is failing to earn a return.

Latest reading: Azure grew 40% in both the three months to March 2026 and across the nine months to March 2026. Infrastructure spending ran at 33–37% of revenue — above 20% — but since Azure growth remains firmly above 25%, the combined exit condition is not triggered. The 15 percentage-point gap above the threshold is large.

  1. The ratio of free cash flow to net profit stays above 0.65 on an annual basis, and any deterioration must not coincide with a deterioration in cloud revenue.

Latest reading: On a nine-month basis the ratio was 0.48 — well below 0.65 — driven by $80 billion of infrastructure spending in the period. The exit condition requires two consecutive fiscal years at that level with no improvement in Azure revenue contribution. Azure is growing strongly, so the second leg is not met. This is nonetheless the condition requiring the closest monitoring at the June 2026 annual results.

  1. Consolidated operating profit margin stays above 42%.

Latest reading: The margin was 46.3% for the three months to March 2026 and 47.4% for the nine months to March 2026. Both are more than four percentage points above the exit threshold.

  1. The IRS transfer-pricing dispute (covering tax years 2004–2013, where the tax authority is seeking an additional $28.9 billion plus interest and penalties) does not escalate to a court proceeding, and no single quarter carries a tax charge above $5 billion as a result of the dispute.

Latest reading: The dispute remains at the administrative appeals stage. No Tax Court filing was disclosed in the March 2026 quarterly filing, and the company stated it does not expect a significant change to its tax contingency reserves in the next twelve months. The exit condition is not triggered.

  1. No formal antitrust complaint is filed by a competition authority seeking structural remedies — such as a forced separation of Teams from other products or restrictions on cloud bundling.

Latest reading: The March 2026 quarterly filing notes ongoing regulatory scrutiny in risk factors, but no specific complaint or court action seeking structural remedies is disclosed anywhere in the filing. The exit condition is not triggered.

  1. Consolidated gross profit margin (the share of every revenue dollar left after the direct cost of delivering the service) stays above 65%.

Latest reading: The margin was 67.6% for the three months to March 2026 and 68.2% for the nine months. Both exceed the 65% floor by more than two percentage points. The slight year-on-year compression is driven by AI infrastructure costs rolling through the cost base but is not approaching the threshold.

  1. Commercial contract backlog (the total value of revenue promised by customers under existing agreements but not yet delivered) grows at least 30% year on year and does not shrink in absolute terms from one quarter to the next.

Latest reading: The backlog reached $627 billion as of March 2026, a 99% increase year on year. No quarter-on-quarter decline was disclosed.

The Share Price

The share price at $390.49 sits within the hold band — above the point where the rules call for adding to the position, and just below the point where they call for trimming it. To be precise: the trim line sits at $402, about $12 above the current price.

The valuation method used here measures the company's earnings as a yield against its market capitalisation — in the same way a bond yield measures interest payments against the bond's price. At $390, that yield works out to about 2.1%. The thesis considers a yield of 3.5% to be a fair price, a yield of 5.0% to be a genuinely cheap price (corresponding to a share price of around $161), and a yield of 2.0% to be expensive enough to trim (corresponding to about $402). The current price sits a hairbreadth below the trim line on that scale.

A separate discounted cash flow analysis — one that estimates the present value of all future earnings under a 12% annual growth assumption and a 9% discount rate — produces an intrinsic value of $429 per share, about 10% above the current price. That implies the stock offers a thin margin of safety at best, and no margin of safety at all under more conservative growth assumptions.

What's Wobbling

Two of the nine conditions came back mixed rather than clean in the three months to March 2026, and both trace to the same root cause.

Infrastructure spending as a proportion of revenue. The company spent $30.9 billion on property and equipment in the three months to March 2026 — 37.2% of revenue. Over the nine months, the figure was $80.1 billion, or 33.1% of revenue. Both are well above the 20% threshold embedded in exit condition 1. The condition also requires Azure growth to be below 25%, and Azure is growing at 40%, so the compound condition is not triggered. But the trajectory is the central question: if Azure growth were to decelerate while spending remained elevated, the condition would become live.

Free cash flow conversion. The ratio of free cash flow (cash from operations minus capital spending) to net profit ran at 0.48 for the nine months to March 2026. The exit condition requires two consecutive full fiscal years at below 0.65, with no improvement in cloud revenue contribution. On both counts the condition is not yet triggered: a nine-month figure is not a full fiscal year, and Azure is growing strongly. But the annual figure for the year ending June 2026 will be the first proper test of whether the spending cycle has peaked or is still climbing. If the full-year ratio comes in below 0.65 and Azure growth is unchanged or improving, the condition remains clear; if Azure were to slow at the same time, it would need to be flagged for a second year of observation.

Neither wobble changes the current verdict. Both are the expected symptoms of a deliberate capital-allocation bet on AI infrastructure, and the thesis explicitly contemplated them. The question is whether the returns on that spending begin to show up — in higher Azure revenue per dollar of capacity — over the next two to four quarters.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • AI spending earns no return. If Azure's growth rate decelerates toward the mid-teens while the infrastructure bill remains above 20% of revenue, the central thesis — that today's capital spending is building a structural competitive position, not just maintaining one — will have been wrong. Free cash flow would stall near today's level for years, and the valuation would need to compress to reflect that the business is now capital-intensive rather than capital-light.
  • The IRS dispute settles at the upper bound. The full $28.9 billion claim, plus interest and penalties accumulated since 2023, would represent a cash outflow equivalent to roughly one full year of free cash flow at current rates. That is survivable from a balance-sheet standpoint — the company carries $78 billion in cash — but it would materially reduce capital available for buybacks and the AI build-out in the period of settlement.
  • A competition authority imposes structural remedies. If the US Federal Trade Commission or a European regulator were to require the separation of Teams from other Microsoft products, or to restrict how Azure services are bundled with productivity software, the cross-selling leverage that reinforces the switching-cost moat would be reduced. The identity layer would survive, but the compounding effect of adding AI and productivity services on top of it would be weakened.
  • Open-source AI models commoditise the Azure AI value proposition. The infrastructure layer Microsoft is building is designed to run AI workloads at scale. If leading AI capabilities become freely available and cheap enough to run on commodity hardware, the premium Microsoft charges for Azure AI services would compress, reducing the expected return on the infrastructure investment.
  • Copilot adoption disappoints. The base case assigns meaningful weight to Microsoft 365 Copilot — the AI assistant embedded in the productivity suite — driving higher revenue per seat across the commercial subscriber base. If seat penetration stalls below 10% of the subscriber base, the revenue uplift embedded in the base case would not materialise on schedule.

Gaps in the Picture

The AI annual recurring revenue figure cited in the thesis ($37 billion, growing 123% year on year as of the results published in early 2026) is not disclosed in quarterly filings. It appears only in investor day and earnings-call presentations, which are not covered by the filing-based analysis here. The quarterly numbers confirm the revenue lines that contain Copilot and Azure AI — Microsoft 365 Commercial cloud grew 19% and Azure overall grew 40% — but do not allow a direct re-check of the standalone AI ARR figure. This is a monitoring gap: a key compass metric is only re-disclosed periodically rather than every quarter.

The fair-value anchor used in the standing orders and TL;DR — the yield-implied price of $229 — diverges substantially from a bottom-up scenario analysis that derives midpoint values of $371 (bear), $587 (base), and $953 (bull) from discounted cash flows. The yield method and the discounted cash flow method are asking different questions — the former takes today's earnings as given and asks what yield they deserve; the latter asks what the earnings will be in three to five years and discounts them back. Both are legitimate lenses; the divergence reflects genuine uncertainty about whether the AI capital cycle will eventually lift earnings materially above today's level, not an error in either method. The yield method's $229 fair-value anchor will drift upward as earnings grow; the discounted cash flow midpoints are already priced in at the base-case scenario level.

The risk-free rate used in the hurdle-rate calculation — 4.49% — was read from the same source used for the broader portfolio rather than from a Microsoft-specific data file. This is the standard procedure when no per-holding rate is available; it is noted here for completeness.

The Rules

The pre-committed rules are:

  • If the thesis is confirmed — all monitored conditions clear, no exit trigger fired: hold the position as it stands. No additional buying at the current price; the margin of safety is insufficient for a new add. The position is a compounding bet, not a recovery trade.
  • If the thesis is weakening — one or more conditions deteriorating but not yet at exit-trigger level: do not add. Re-run the validation against the specific deteriorating condition after one additional quarter. Determine whether the weakening is cyclical (infrastructure timing) or structural (competitive displacement) before reassessing size. If a second consecutive quarter confirms the signal, escalate to the thesis-broken review.
  • If the thesis is broken — any one of the seven exit conditions has fired: exit the full position within 30 days. No averaging down. Log which condition triggered and in which quarter, for future learning.
  • If the price falls below the buy band: confirm the thesis is intact before adding. If intact, begin adding in tranches — first below $352, then below $322, with each tranche covering 25–33% of the intended add-on allocation.
  • If the price rises above the trim line: trim 20–25% of the position to harvest the premium. If Azure growth continues confirming the base case, retain the remaining position. Do not trim below half the current holding unless a thesis exit condition is also triggered.
  • If the price rises above the exit line: close the full position. At that level the market is pricing in roughly 17 years of base-case compounding in full; the implied forward return falls below the long-run equity premium with zero margin of safety. No re-entry within 90 days.
