Berkshire: The Bet Still Works

The Bottom Line

The bet is working. At the Share Price, the stock sits in the upper half of the hold band, at fair value. Book value per share grew 10.5% in full-year 2025, operating earnings rose 6.5% in the three months ending in March 2026, and the Oregon Court of Appeals reversed the most damaging wildfire verdict against Berkshire's energy subsidiary in April 2026 — all without any of the four written exit conditions coming close to triggering. The condition worth watching is GEICO's claims trajectory: its ratio of losses to premiums rose in the first quarter of 2026 relative to an unusually clean prior-year period, and the next few quarters will tell whether the deterioration is cyclical or structural.

Recent Development

The standout development in the three months ending in March 2026 was not inside the businesses themselves but in a courtroom: the Oregon Court of Appeals reversed the first-phase PacifiCorp wildfire verdict in April 2026, eliminating what had been a cloud of uncertain multi-billion-dollar liability over Berkshire's energy business. That reversal, combined with the previously announced sale of PacifiCorp's Washington state operations to Portland General Electric for $1.9 billion in cash, materially reduces the tail risk that had weighed on any sum-of-the-parts valuation of Berkshire Hathaway Energy.

The operating businesses continued their recent trajectory. Pre-tax earnings from operating subsidiaries rose 6.5% in the first quarter of 2026 compared to the same quarter a year earlier. Berkshire's railroad subsidiary, BNSF — which runs freight lines across the western United States — posted its best recent quarterly earnings growth, with after-tax earnings climbing 13.4%. Book value, the core metric the thesis tracks, reached $727.2 billion at the end of March 2026, up $9.8 billion from year-end 2025 and extending a full-year 2025 gain of 10.5%.

The one mixed note was GEICO, Berkshire's car-insurance business. Its loss ratio — losses and loss-adjustment expenses as a share of premiums earned — rose 4.9 percentage points in the first quarter of 2026 compared to the same quarter a year earlier, driven by higher claims frequency (bodily-injury coverage up 5–7%) and higher severity (up 12–14%). That earlier quarter had unusually low catastrophe losses, making the year-on-year comparison look worse than the underlying trend warrants. GEICO remains profitably underwriting, but the direction is worth tracking. More detail in The Case for the Bet.

About Berkshire

Berkshire Hathaway is a holding company that earns money through three reinforcing engines. The first is insurance: Berkshire owns GEICO, the large direct-to-consumer car insurer, plus a group of specialist underwriters that collectively write a wide range of policies from aviation and nuclear risk to reinsurance. Insurance businesses collect premiums before paying claims, which creates a pool of cash — called the float — that Berkshire can invest. At nearly $177 billion as of early 2026, this float costs Berkshire nothing (premiums have historically exceeded claims) and funds a substantial portion of its investment activities.

The second engine is a set of major wholly-owned businesses: BNSF, one of the two largest freight railroads in the western United States; Berkshire Hathaway Energy, a regulated utility and energy business with operations spanning several states; Pilot Travel Centers, the largest network of truck stops in the US; and a range of manufacturers and retailers from Precision Castparts (aerospace components) to Dairy Queen and Fruit of the Loom. The third engine is an investment portfolio of publicly traded equities worth approximately $298 billion, anchored by positions in Apple, Bank of America, Coca-Cola, and American Express.

Greg Abel became chief executive on 1 January 2026, succeeding Warren Buffett, who remains chairman. Abel built Berkshire Hathaway Energy from a regional Iowa utility into a multi-state energy platform and has been inside Berkshire's management structure since 1999. His capital allocation decisions at the parent level are still early: the first notable deployment was the January 2026 acquisition of OxyChem (the chemicals business of Occidental Petroleum) for approximately $9.5 billion.

The Case for the Bet

The investment case rests on a single idea: that Berkshire's management team will continue to reinvest retained earnings and insurance float into high-returning assets at rates that exceed what could be achieved by holding an index fund. The specific conditions that must hold for this to remain true are:

  1. Book value per share grows at a rate that matches or exceeds the MSCI World total return over any rolling five-year period. This is the primary scoreboard. If Berkshire's compounding engine trails a passive world-equity index for five consecutive years, the compounding edge is structurally gone.

Latest reading: Shareholders' equity grew 10.5% in full-year 2025, reaching $717.4 billion, and rose a further $9.8 billion to $727.2 billion in the three months to March 2026. The rolling five-year scorecard continues to support the thesis.

  1. No large acquisition that signals a breakdown of capital discipline. Specifically, no transaction exceeding $20 billion at a reported consideration above 1.5 times the book value of the target, or one that subsequently requires a goodwill write-down exceeding $5 billion.

Latest reading: The OxyChem acquisition (approximately $9.5 billion, January 2026) falls below the $20 billion threshold and does not trigger this condition. The first quarterly reporting following completion shows no goodwill impairment concerns. The condition is satisfied, though Abel's broader track record at the parent level remains short.

  1. No structural culture change — such as initiating a regular dividend, breaking up the conglomerate, or capitulating to activist pressure — that would signal the decentralised owner-operator model has been abandoned.

Latest reading: No dividend was declared, no break-up discussions are disclosed, and the Q1 2026 filing contains no indication of activist activity. The organisational model appears intact. Greg Abel continues as CEO with no reported changes to the leadership structure.

  1. The price-to-book ratio stays below 2.0 times. Above that level, the valuation fully prices in optimistic compounding, and the opportunity cost versus a passive index becomes clearly negative.

Latest reading: The price-to-book ratio stood at approximately 1.52 times as of the Q1 2026 filing date, using year-end 2025 equity of $717.4 billion and the current share price of $504. This is comfortably below the 2.0 times exit trigger.

The Share Price

Berkshire's valuation is measured against its book value per share (the net worth of the business divided by the number of shares outstanding) rather than against a traditional earnings multiple, because the reported net profit figure swings wildly from year to year depending on how public stock markets move — a consequence of accounting rules that force Berkshire to mark its $298 billion equity portfolio to market each quarter.

At $504 per B-share, the price-to-book ratio is approximately 1.52 times. The fair-value anchor in the investment framework is 1.45 times, reflecting the quality of the franchise with an adjustment for the uncertainty of the Abel transition. The buy edge — the level below which the shares are attractive enough to add — sits at 1.25 times, corresponding to roughly $415 per B-share. The trim edge sits at 1.75 times, corresponding to roughly $581 per B-share.

The current price of $504 sits in the upper half of the hold band: above fair value but meaningfully below the trim edge. The price is at fair value in terms of the gap between the current share price and the $481 anchor. Put plainly, the share price is not cheap enough to buy more and not expensive enough to reduce the position.

What's Wobbling

GEICO's loss ratio — losses and loss-adjustment expenses as a share of premiums earned — worsened by 4.9 percentage points in the three months ending in March 2026 compared to the same quarter a year earlier. The deterioration was driven by higher claims frequency (bodily-injury coverage up 5–7%) and higher severity (up 12–14%). The comparison quarter was an outlier: Q1 2025 had near-zero catastrophe losses, so the year-on-year move looks worse than the underlying trend warrants. GEICO is still a profitably underwriting business — the condition for concern is sustained results where claims and expenses together exceed premiums, not the current reading. Policies-in-force data, which would reveal whether GEICO is losing customers to competitors such as Progressive, are not disclosed in the quarterly report and require the annual letter.

Share repurchases were described by management as "relatively minor" — approximately $235 million in the first quarter of 2026, at an average price close to the fair-value anchor. At a price-to-book near 1.45, management is signalling that the shares are not cheap enough to repurchase aggressively. This is internally consistent with the framework: buybacks are most attractive below the buy-band edge, not near fair value.

Neither wobble triggers a written exit condition. Both are being monitored.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Abel's capital allocation record is short. One acquisition (OxyChem, $9.5 billion, a commodity chemicals business) is not enough to assess whether Berkshire's compounding edge continues under new leadership. A poorly priced large acquisition — above $20 billion at more than 2.5 times book value of the target — would directly trigger an exit condition.
  • A large catastrophe event could simultaneously hurt the insurance business and flip the cost of the float from negative to positive. A hurricane or earthquake causing more than $15 billion in net losses for Berkshire would compress operating earnings sharply for one to two years while also increasing the cost of the float, the foundational advantage of the insurance engine.
  • GEICO faces a structural competitor in Progressive. Progressive's telematics-based pricing (using in-car data to price policies individually) is faster and more precise than GEICO's traditional model. A deterioration of 200 basis points (two percentage points) in GEICO's combined ratio costs approximately $890 million in annual underwriting profit. A sustained ratio above 100% for several quarters is the watch signal.
  • BHE's renewable investment pipeline is compressed. The legislation enacted in July 2025 (the "One Big Beautiful Bill Act") restricts new eligibility for clean-energy tax credits after December 2025. Existing projects are grandfathered, but the economics of Berkshire Hathaway Energy's future renewable buildout are less attractive. This reduces BHE's long-term earnings growth potential, though it does not threaten the existing regulated-return asset base.
  • The equity portfolio is concentrated. Roughly 65% of Berkshire's $298 billion public equity portfolio is in five positions. A simultaneous drawdown in Apple, Bank of America, and Coca-Cola would reduce reported book value and paper equity, though it would not directly impair the operating businesses.

The Rules

The pre-committed action plan governs what follows from each combination of thesis state and share-price position. There is one clause per state:

  • If the thesis is confirmed (all conditions intact, no exit triggered): hold. No action. Let it compound.
  • If the thesis is weakened (one or more conditions deteriorating but not broken): do not add to the position. Run a targeted re-validation on the specific deteriorating driver. Decide after the next quarterly filing.
  • If the thesis is broken (an exit condition fires): exit the full position within 30 days. No averaging down.
  • If the price falls below the buy edge ($415): confirm the thesis is intact first. If confirmed, add to the position.
  • If the price rises above the trim edge ($581): trim 25–33% of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.
  • If the price rises above the exit edge ($647): exit the full position. The market is pricing in roughly 10 years of base-case compounding instantly; redeploy proceeds at the next buy-edge crossing or into a cheaper alternative. No re-entry within 90 days.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
