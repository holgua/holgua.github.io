About Dynatrace

Dynatrace makes software that monitors, analyses, and explains the behaviour of complex enterprise technology systems in real time. Large organisations running software across hundreds of servers, cloud environments, and third-party services face a fundamental problem: when something goes wrong, or when performance degrades, the cause is usually buried in a web of dependencies that no human team can trace manually at speed. Dynatrace sells a platform — anchored in a proprietary monitoring agent called OneAgent — that automatically maps every component in a customer's technology landscape, collects performance data continuously, and surfaces root causes of problems using a combination of rule-based and artificial-intelligence methods. Customers pay a recurring subscription fee, typically renewing and expanding their use year after year.

The company earns the overwhelming majority of its revenue from these subscriptions: $1,930 million of the $2,018 million in total revenue for the fiscal year ending 2026-03-31. The customer base sits at roughly 4,100 large enterprises globally, skewed toward companies for which a technology outage carries severe business consequences — financial institutions, retailers, manufacturers with digital supply chains, and cloud-native businesses. Revenue retention above 100% for three consecutive years is the most direct available signal that existing customers are finding the platform worth expanding, not replacing.

The Case for the Bet

The bet rests on a single idea: that Dynatrace's proprietary platform has become so embedded in the technology infrastructure of its customers that replacing it would cost more in disruption than any savings from switching, and that this structural advantage will allow the company to grow its recurring revenue at high rates for years while consistently converting a quarter or more of that revenue into cash. Seven conditions define the boundaries of that bet:

  1. Net revenue retention holds at or above 110%. Net revenue retention (what percentage of last year's recurring revenue base is still paying this year, after accounting for expansions, contractions, and cancellations) is the most direct test of whether the switching-cost moat is real. A single quarter below 107% triggers a watch; two consecutive quarters below 107% triggers an exit.

Latest reading: 110% (constant currency, 2026-03-31). This is the third consecutive year at precisely this level. The figure is defined in the annual report as covering "customer renewals, expansion, contraction and churn" on a constant-currency basis — making it a comprehensive measure, not a cherry-picked one.

  1. Annual recurring revenue grows at 13% or above in constant-currency terms. Annual recurring revenue growth in constant currency is the headline measure of whether the business is expanding at a rate consistent with the thesis. Two consecutive quarters below 13% constant-currency growth would trigger an exit.

Latest reading: 18% reported growth year-on-year, to $2,054 million (2026-03-31). The constant-currency equivalent is not separately disclosed in the annual filing; the 18% reported figure suggests constant-currency growth is at least at that level, well above the 13% floor.

  1. Free cash flow margin stays above 20% for any full fiscal year. Free cash flow margin (the share of total revenue that becomes cash after all operating costs and investments) is the measure of how efficiently the business converts growth into real money for owners. A full fiscal year below 20% triggers an exit.

Latest reading: 26.2% margin for fiscal year 2026 (ending 2026-03-31), up from 25.5% the year before. Free cash flow was $529 million on revenues of $2,018 million. The exit trigger is not close.

  1. No open-market equity sales by the chief executive or chief financial officer above $5 million in any 90-day window. Sales of that magnitude by the two most senior executives, in the open market rather than through pre-planned programmes, would signal that those closest to the business have lost confidence in the thesis.

Latest reading: No open-market chief executive or chief financial officer equity sales are disclosed in the annual report (2026-05-20 filing). The company repurchased and retired $479 million of its own shares in fiscal year 2026, including $37.71 per share on average in the final quarter — the opposite direction from an insider sell signal.

  1. Dynatrace Platform Subscription penetration does not stall below 72% of annual recurring revenue for three consecutive quarters. The Dynatrace Platform Subscription is the company's consumption-based pricing tier, which allows customers to expand their use without a new procurement cycle. Its penetration of the annual recurring revenue base is a leading indicator of future expansion capacity. Stalling — especially if management stops disclosing the metric — would suggest the platform is losing its upgrade pull.

Latest reading: Not disclosed in the annual report (2026-05-20 filing). The last quarterly disclosure placed the figure at approximately 70% of annual recurring revenue. The annual filing does not carry this metric; monitoring requires the next quarterly results release.

  1. No publicly announced confirmed Dynatrace customer wins by Cisco–Splunk or Palo Alto–Chronosphere. Bundled-suite competitors — specifically Cisco, which acquired Splunk in 2024, and Palo Alto Networks, which acquired Chronosphere in 2026 — pose the most credible displacement risk. The condition fires only on confirmed named customer wins, not on acquisition announcements alone.

Latest reading: Not triggered (2026-05-20 filing). The annual report names both acquisitions as competitive risks but does not cite confirmed Dynatrace customer losses. The threshold has not been crossed; the risk has, however, moved closer.

  1. Net cash stays above $600 million without a disclosed merger or acquisition rationale. Cash on the balance sheet above this level provides the business with the financial flexibility to invest and weather a downturn without needing equity dilution. A decline below the threshold without an explained strategic purpose would signal financial stress or undisclosed capital deployment.

Latest reading: Net cash of $823 million (2026-03-31), up from $625 million a year earlier. The company carries $274 million in long-term debt against cash and equivalents well in excess of that figure. The exit trigger is not close.

The Share Price

At $44, Dynatrace trades at a free-cash-flow yield (annual free cash flow divided by the market's total valuation of the business) of approximately 4.1%. The framework anchors fair value at a yield of 4.4%, which corresponds to a share price of roughly $41. At $44, the share price sits approximately 6% above that anchor — in the language of the framework, modestly above fair value.

That gap is real but not dramatic. It does not trigger the trim order — which requires the yield to fall below 3.3%, corresponding to a share price around $55 — and it is well within normal variance. Put plainly, the price is slightly rich but not stretched. At a yield of 5.9%, corresponding to a share price near $31, the buy order would activate; the current price is $13 above that line.

The fair-value anchor is based on the current year's free cash flow of $529 million and today's market capitalisation, not on peer multiples or projected earnings several years out. A separate analysis using peer companies' valuation multiples on projected future earnings produces a significantly higher central estimate around $74 per share — but that analysis and this one are not contradictory; they are measuring different things. The current-yield view asks what today's cash generation is worth; the peer-multiple view asks what the business might be worth if it executes its growth plan. Both are reasonable lenses; neither is wrong.

What's Wobbling

One driver returned a mixed reading in the latest filing cycle: the competitive landscape for artificial intelligence in enterprise software.

The annual report discloses that Palo Alto Networks acquired Chronosphere during 2026. This is directly relevant to exit condition six, which fires on confirmed Dynatrace customer wins by a named bundled-suite competitor following acquisition. The filing acknowledges the risk without evidencing any confirmed customer displacement. The distinction matters: Chronosphere is a specialist in observability for cloud-native applications and is now in the hands of a company with the scale and existing customer relationships to promote it aggressively inside enterprise accounts where Dynatrace also operates.

Dynatrace's direct response — the general availability of Dynatrace Intelligence, its agentic artificial-intelligence system trained on data from 4,100 enterprise environments — arrived in the same fiscal year. That counter-investment is meaningful because it creates a proprietary training-data advantage that point-solution competitors cannot replicate without similarly large installed bases. That is the moat holding, for now. The wobble is real, the line has not been crossed, and The Case for the Bet describes exactly what crossing would look like.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Competitive displacement accelerates. If Cisco–Splunk or Palo Alto–Chronosphere begins winning named Dynatrace enterprise accounts, exit condition six fires. The combination of Palo Alto's security-platform relationships and Chronosphere's cloud-native observability capability is a plausible path to displacement in accounts where security and observability budgets are consolidating.
  • Net revenue retention declines toward 107%. A sustained decline in the rate at which the existing customer base expands would signal that the switching-cost moat is eroding. Two consecutive quarters below 107% triggers an exit regardless of reported revenue growth.
  • AI makes enterprise monitoring a commodity. General-purpose artificial-intelligence tools and open-source monitoring standards (such as OpenTelemetry) may reduce the value premium that Dynatrace commands by making it easier for customers to build equivalent monitoring capabilities without a proprietary vendor.
  • Share-based compensation destroys owner-level returns. The company issues shares to employees equivalent to roughly 15% of annual revenues. After accounting for this dilution, the cash generated for owners — rather than for employees — is considerably lower than the reported free-cash-flow figure suggests. The $479 million buyback programme partially offsets dilution, but net share-count change over time is the test that matters.
  • Macro pressure compresses enterprise software budgets. Annual contract structures limit immediate exposure to a downturn, but a sustained period of enterprise technology-budget compression would slow new customer additions and reduce expansion from the existing base, pressuring growth toward the bear-case scenario.

Gaps in the Picture

The free-cash-flow figure used in the valuation (and throughout the conditions) is the reported pre-share-based-compensation number from the annual filing: $529 million. The actual cash available after accounting for dilution from employee share grants is materially lower — approximately $230 million for fiscal year 2026, once $300 million in share-based compensation is subtracted. The framework uses the pre-dilution figure by design, anchoring to the company's own reported measure; how to weight the dilution question is a judgement call that each position-holder must make independently. Both figures are real; neither is hidden. The tension between them is the central valuation debate noted in the annual financial analysis.

The platform subscription penetration rate — the share of annual recurring revenue on the consumption-based pricing tier — is not disclosed in the annual report. The last known reading was approximately 70% from a quarterly disclosure. This metric is important because it is one of the seven written exit conditions; monitoring it requires reading each quarterly results announcement separately, as the annual filing does not carry it.

The Rules

The evaluation maps to five standing rules, each corresponding to a different combination of thesis health and share-price position.

If the thesis is confirmed — the conditions above are holding and none of the written exit triggers has fired — maintain the full position, monitoring the quarterly key metrics (net revenue retention, constant-currency annual recurring revenue growth, free-cash-flow margin, platform subscription penetration). Consider adding modestly on dips below $38, the bear-case fair-value midpoint, while net revenue retention holds at or above 107%.

If the thesis is weakening — net revenue retention is declining toward 107%, or constant-currency annual recurring revenue growth is decelerating toward 14% — reduce to approximately half the position within 30 days of the trigger. Monitor for two additional quarters before deciding whether to exit fully or rebuild. The weakening signals to watch are: net revenue retention between 107% and 109% for a single quarter; constant-currency growth between 13% and 15% for a single quarter; platform subscription penetration stalling below 72%.

If the thesis is broken — any of the seven written exit conditions is confirmed — exit the full position within one quarter. The broken conditions are defined precisely in The Case for the Bet. Do not add to or hold a position on a broken thesis.

If the share price falls below the buy edge ($31, where the free-cash-flow yield rises above 5.9%) — accumulate toward a full position. This level represents a materially undervalued entry relative to the base-case discounted-cash-flow analysis. The thesis must still be intact for the buy order to apply.

If the share price rises above the trim edge ($55, where the free-cash-flow yield falls below 2.9%) — trim toward a half to two-thirds position. At that level, the base-case fair value is within reach and the remaining return requires bull-case execution. A high-quality compounder with strong net revenue retention and substantial net cash warrants partial continued ownership even through high-multiple periods; full exit waits for the exit edge or a thesis break.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
