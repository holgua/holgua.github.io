Recent Development

The three months ending March 2026 were the clearest confirmation the AI investment cycle is working rather than merely consuming capital. Azure cloud services grew 40% compared with the same quarter a year earlier — matching the 40% rate recorded in the quarter ending September 2025 — and the broader Intelligent Cloud segment grew 30%. Commercial contracted backlog reached $627 billion, up 99% year-on-year. Operating income grew 20% in the quarter to $38.4 billion, with operating margins of 46.3% on revenue of $82.9 billion — more than four percentage points above the 42% exit floor. Gross margins held at 67.6%, above the 65% exit threshold.

The one number that fell below a written threshold is the free-cash-flow-to-net-income ratio. For the nine months through March 2026, operating cash flow of $127.5 billion minus capital spending of $80.1 billion produces a free cash flow figure of $47.3 billion against net income of $98.0 billion — a ratio of 0.48, below the 0.65 line in exit condition two. Capital spending reached 33% of revenue for the nine-month period, up sharply from 23% for the full prior fiscal year. The compound exit condition, however, requires both this ratio to stay depressed for two consecutive full fiscal years and Azure revenue growth to slow below 25% simultaneously. With Azure at 40%, the compound trigger is not within reach. As explained in the Standing Orders, the two thresholds must fire together before any action follows.

The quarterly report contained no new development on the US tax authority dispute, which remains in administrative appeals, and no new antitrust enforcement action from the Federal Trade Commission.

About Microsoft

Microsoft makes its money in three overlapping businesses. The largest is the productivity-and-collaboration layer: the software that runs email, word processing, spreadsheets, and business communication for roughly 400 million commercial seats worldwide, delivered mainly as a subscription called Microsoft 365. The second is cloud infrastructure: Azure, a global network of data centres that companies rent to run applications and, increasingly, artificial-intelligence workloads instead of buying their own servers. The third is a collection of personal computing and gaming businesses — Windows, Xbox, LinkedIn, and the Bing search engine — that together contribute roughly a quarter of revenue and operate at lower margins than the other two.

What ties all three together is the enterprise identity system. Every company that uses Microsoft 365 relies on a service called Azure Active Directory (now rebranded as Entra ID) to manage which employees can access which systems. Every device that a company issues is enrolled against it. Every application — from email to security tools to enterprise resource planning — authenticates against it. Replacing this layer requires a company to change its email system, its device management, its security controls, and its application integrations simultaneously, a project so disruptive that almost no large enterprise ever attempts it without a compelling reason. This is what makes the business sticky: customers do not leave not because Microsoft is cheap, but because leaving is extraordinarily expensive. Revenue from this suite has grown at 15% or more annually for several years, and the contracted backlog of $627 billion confirms that enterprise customers are extending commitments, not planning exits.

The current chapter is a capital-allocation bet. Between the 2021 and 2025 fiscal years, Microsoft's annual capital spending rose from $21 billion to $65 billion — money spent building the data-centre infrastructure needed to run artificial-intelligence services at scale. That spending compresses free cash flow now; whether it builds a structural advantage or merely a temporary cost burden is the central question in the investment case.

The Case for the Bet

The case rests on the proposition that Azure's switching-cost moat is widening rather than eroding, and that the current capital spending surge is building durable infrastructure rather than defending against an advance that cannot be stopped.

  1. Azure revenue growth stays above 25% while capital spending remains above 20% of revenue. The thesis exit condition fires when growth drops below 25% for two consecutive quarters at the same time that spending stays above 20% — a compound signal that the infrastructure build has failed to earn its keep. Growth below 25% alone is not an exit; neither is elevated spending alone.

Latest reading: Azure and other cloud services grew 40% in the three months ending March 2026 (Q3 fiscal year 2026), matching the 40% rate recorded in Q1 and holding comfortably above the 25% floor. The nine-month revenue for the Intelligent Cloud segment reached $98.5 billion, up 29%. Spending at 33% of revenue is elevated, but the compound trigger is nowhere in evidence at 40% growth.

  1. Free cash flow to net income stays above 0.65 for two consecutive fiscal years, or Azure artificial-intelligence revenue continues expanding. The exit condition requires sustained cash-flow suppression over multiple years combined with no improvement in cloud AI revenue — a signal that the spending is permanently impairing returns rather than temporarily funding growth.

Latest reading: For the nine months through March 2026, the ratio is 0.48 on a pre-share-buyback basis — below the threshold. This covers three quarters, not two fiscal years. The full fiscal year 2026 will include the June 2026 quarter; the exit condition requires the ratio to stay depressed for two consecutive full-year periods with no Azure AI improvement. GitHub Copilot is explicitly cited in the filing as a driver of increased infrastructure cost, confirming AI consumption is growing even without a standalone revenue disclosure for that period.

  1. Operating margin stays above 42%. A sustained decline below 42% for two consecutive quarters would signal structural margin compression beyond the AI investment cycle.

Latest reading: Operating margin was 46.3% in Q3 fiscal year 2026 and 47.4% for the nine-month period — both more than four percentage points above the 42% exit floor. Operating income grew 20% in the quarter.

The Share Price

At $389, the share price sits at the trim edge — the point at which earnings yield (net income expressed as a fraction of market value) falls to 2.06%, approaching the 2.0% line at which the rules call for trimming. The fair-value anchor under the written thesis is a yield of 3.5%, which corresponds to a share price of approximately $229. The current price is 70% above that anchor, well above fair value by any mechanical reading of the written thresholds.

To put the yield in plain terms: at $389, every $100 of Microsoft's market value earns roughly $2.06 — a return below what a straightforward government bond offers. The investment case depends entirely on growth: if earnings keep compounding at something close to their historical rate, the yield on the entry price rises over time and the total return exceeds the hurdle. If growth stalls, the current yield is insufficient.

The price is high by the rules' own measure. That does not make it wrong — it makes it contingent. The price is right if the growth assumptions hold. See the Case for the Bet for the conditions that must continue holding.

What's Wobbling

Two of the nine thesis drivers came back mixed in the latest filing, and neither is close to triggering an exit on its own.

The free-cash-flow-to-net-income ratio slipped to 0.48 for the nine months through March 2026, below the 0.65 threshold in exit condition two. The cause is not an earnings-quality problem — accruals ratios have remained consistently negative for five years, meaning operating cash flow consistently exceeds reported earnings, which is the cleanest possible earnings quality signal. The cause is deliberate capital spending: $80.1 billion in property, plant, and equipment additions over the nine-month period, compared with $47.5 billion in the same period a year earlier. Exit condition two requires this compression to persist for two consecutive full fiscal years with no improvement in cloud artificial-intelligence revenue. With Azure growing at 40% and Copilot expanding, the second half of that compound condition is not satisfied.

Capital spending itself is the second mixed signal. At 33% of nine-month revenue and 37% in the most recent quarter, it is well above the 23% recorded for the full prior fiscal year. The written exit condition requires Azure growth to fall below 25% simultaneously — the logic being that high spending without revenue coverage is the failure mode, not high spending alongside 40% growth. That logic is not yet tested. The question is what happens when, or if, Azure growth moderates.

Neither wobble changes the read on the thesis. Both are monitored conditions, not triggered ones. The conditions to watch at the next quarterly filing are whether the free-cash-flow ratio improves as the September 2025 quarter drops out of the nine-month window, and whether Azure's growth rate holds above 30%.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • The AI infrastructure bet fails to monetise. If Azure growth decelerates to the mid-teens as open-weight models — those distributed freely by companies like Meta — commoditise the inference workloads driving current growth, then $65 billion or more per year in capital spending produces below-hurdle returns. Free cash flow would remain compressed for longer than the thesis allows, and the bear-case scenario of −3.2% a year over three years would describe the outcome. This is the single most important risk in the investment case.
  • The tax dispute escalates. The US tax authority's $28.9 billion assessment plus penalties for the 2004–2013 years is live and in administrative appeals. A move to Tax Court or a single-quarter settlement charge above $5 billion would trigger mandatory review and absorb capital that would otherwise fund share buybacks. At $29–35 billion, the charge would be painful but survivable — roughly 1% of market value.
  • Antitrust intervention reaches structure. The Federal Trade Commission probe spans cloud bundling, Teams integration, and artificial-intelligence market structure. A formal complaint seeking a forced separation of Teams from Microsoft 365 or interoperability mandates on cloud infrastructure would weaken the portfolio lock-in moat in a way that is harder to quantify than a one-time financial charge.
  • Capital allocation deteriorates. A large acquisition at a premium multiple — as with the $69 billion Activision purchase — absorbs cash and adds integration risk without immediate moat benefit. A second large deal of that kind while the AI CapEx cycle is still peaking would stretch resources further.
  • OpenAI losses scale beyond expectations. Microsoft recorded a $4.7 billion equity-method loss from its OpenAI stake in the 2025 fiscal year. If OpenAI's cost structure expands faster than its revenue, further charges reduce reported net income without affecting the underlying business — but they do consume cash and create noise in the financial results.

Gaps in the Picture

The owner-earnings calculation in the written thesis sets maintenance capital spending equal to depreciation, making the two cancel and leaving net income as the earnings proxy. Depreciation is not separately disclosed in Microsoft's SEC filings under the accounting taxonomy used — the filing reports it within the cash-flow statement bridge but not as a standalone line. This means the precise split between maintenance and growth capital spending is estimated, not observed. The thesis explicitly acknowledges this simplification.

The latest re-validation flagged that the fair-value anchor of 3.5% earnings yield (corresponding to $229) diverges from the current earnings yield of 2.06% by more than 40%, and that a separate fair-value model using Microsoft's own historical price-to-cash-flow multiple range implies a midpoint well above $2,000. These divergences reflect the gap between a yield-based anchor calibrated to the business's earnings power and a market-price model anchored to how investors have historically priced cash-flow growth. Both warnings are on file; the yield-based anchor is the governing rule until it is deliberately revised.

A risk-free rate for computing the hurdle is not available in Microsoft's own market-data file; the rate was read from the S&P 500 ETF's market-data file as a fallback (snapshot 2026-07-10).

The Rules

The rules translate current conditions and share price into one of six branches. Exactly one branch applies at any moment; the rules do not require interpretation.

  • If all exit conditions are intact and no threshold has fired: hold the position without adding. Azure growth sustained, enterprise backlog compounding, moat intact. Continue monitoring at each quarterly earnings release. No additional sizing at prices near today's level — the margin of safety is too thin for a new add.
  • If any single thesis driver deteriorates but no written exit condition has fired: do not add to the position. Re-run the re-validation against the specific weakening driver — whether Azure growth, free-cash-flow conversion, operating margin, or contracted backlog. Monitor for one additional quarter before reassessing size. Determine whether the weakness is cyclical (spending timing) or structural (competitive displacement). If a second consecutive quarter confirms the signal, escalate to the broken-thesis review.
  • If any written exit condition fires: exit the full position within 30 days. No averaging down. Note the quarter for future learning.
  • If the share price falls to or below the buy edge of $160: confirm all exit conditions are intact first. If they are, begin adding in tranches: a first tranche below $352 (40% below the base-case scenario midpoint), a second tranche below $322 (40% below the fair-value estimate from the discounted-cash-flow model).   • If the share price rises to or above the exit edge of $1605: exit the full position. At that price the earnings yield falls to 0.5% — below half the risk-free rate with no margin of safety. Redeploy proceeds at the next buy-edge crossing or into a cheaper alternative. No re-entry for 90 days to defuse anchor bias.
