About US Treasury 0-1y

The Vanguard U.S. Treasury 0–1 Year Bond UCITS ETF holds a basket of US Treasury bills maturing within one year and reinvests income internally — no cash is distributed. The fund is domiciled in Ireland, listed on the London Stock Exchange, and denominated in US dollars. Its mandate is deliberately narrow: every holding is a direct obligation of the US federal government, carrying no credit risk beyond the sovereign. The weighted-average time to maturity across the basket is roughly four to five months, meaning the portfolio rolls continuously into newly issued bills and tracks the prevailing short-term US interest rate closely.

The annual cost — the fund's ongoing charges figure — is 0.07% of assets per year. Net cost after any securities lending revenue is not separately disclosed; no lending programme is confirmed. No tracking-difference or tracking-error data is available from the data provider for this London-listed UCITS vehicle; the mandate's simplicity and Vanguard's scale make meaningful tracking divergence unlikely.

The Case for the Bet

The fund is held as a USD cash-equivalent within a broader EUR-denominated portfolio. The relevant question is not whether the T-bill rate offers a superior risk-adjusted return relative to equities — at current yields it does not — but whether the vehicle reliably delivers what it claims to deliver, at a cost that is not eroding the thesis. Three conditions govern that question:

  1. Annual cost stays below 0.10% for every consecutive pair of quarterly reviews. The fund charges 0.07% per year, confirmed from the Vanguard published figure. Comparable ultra-short-duration Treasury funds from other managers are available at similar costs. If the cost were to rise above 0.10% for two consecutive quarters, the cost advantage over alternatives would be gone and reallocation would make sense.

Latest reading: 0.07% (2026-07-20, sourced from Vanguard published OCF; yfinance does not surface this figure for UCITS listings). Three basis points below the 0.10% ceiling — no flag warranted.

  1. Fund size stays above USD 1 billion. A T-bill fund's value proposition rests on instant liquidity; a fund that shrinks materially below the threshold risks wider spreads or, in stress, redemption gating. The floor is set conservatively: the fund is estimated at roughly USD 2.4 billion based on prior tracking-service data, more than double the minimum.

Latest reading: Not automatically verified this cycle — yfinance does not surface assets-under-management for UCITS ETFs. Carry-forward estimate from Trackinsight: approximately USD 2.4 billion (above floor). Manual verification against the Vanguard factsheet is required quarterly.

  1. No US sovereign credit downgrade below AA− by two of the three major rating agencies simultaneously. The fund holds obligations of a single sovereign obligor. A downgrade of that magnitude by two agencies would signal a structural shift in creditworthiness and would require reassessing the cash-equivalent premise entirely.

Latest reading: US sovereign ratings as of the latest review: Fitch AA+, Moody's Aaa, S&P AA+. No two-agency downgrade below AA− has occurred. This condition is monitored manually.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • The Federal Reserve cuts rates aggressively in response to a recession, compressing T-bill yields toward or below 2.5%. At that level, USD T-bill carry would be materially weaker than EUR cash at the European Central Bank rate, and the hold-as-cash-equivalent rationale would weaken.
  • The fund's annual cost rises above 0.10% for two consecutive quarters — the cost ceiling in exit condition (1). Vanguard has historically been a low-cost manager, but fee changes at any fund are possible, particularly if the fund's size shrinks and the economics of running it change.
  • The fund's size falls below USD 1 billion, triggering exit condition (2). A sustained redemption cycle — for instance, if the EUR/USD rate moved sharply or if the fund lost institutional mandates — could bring total assets below the floor.
  • US sovereign credit is downgraded below AA− by two of the three major rating agencies, triggering exit condition (3). This would be an extraordinary event, but the ratings are not immune to deterioration; the 2011 S&P downgrade is a reminder.
  • Index methodology or fund mandate changes, which cannot be automatically detected by the pipeline, would require a manual response.

The Rules

If vehicle-integrity conditions are intact and the basket yield is between 3.5% and 5.5%, hold. The T-bill carry is positive and the vehicle is doing what it was selected to do.

If the basket yield rises above 5.5% — the buy-edge level — and vehicle-integrity conditions are intact, add at the margin if cash is available. The T-bill rate at that level would be unusually attractive relative to EUR alternatives.

If the basket yield falls below 3.5% — the trim-edge level — and vehicle-integrity conditions are intact, trim at the margin. USD T-bill carry at that level would be materially weaker relative to EUR cash.

If the basket yield falls below 3.0% — the exit-edge level — exit the position. At that yield, the carry advantage over EUR cash has disappeared and reallocation is warranted.

If vehicle-integrity conditions weaken — costs drifting toward the ceiling, fund size declining toward the floor, or a sovereign credit watch — do not add. Re-assess at the next quarterly review and monitor the relevant figures before any increase in allocation.

If any exit condition fires — costs above 0.10% for two consecutive quarters, fund size below USD 1 billion, or US sovereign credit downgraded below AA− by two agencies — exit. Reinvest proceeds in EUR cash or short nominal German government bonds.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
