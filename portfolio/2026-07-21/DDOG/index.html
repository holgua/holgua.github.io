Recent Development

Datadog crossed the $1 billion quarterly revenue threshold for the first time in the three months ending in March 2026, growing 32% compared with the same quarter a year earlier. That re-acceleration is the headline: after decelerating to roughly 26–28% annual growth in the prior two years, the business picked up speed. The dollar-based retention rate — the metric that measures how much existing customers spend year-over-year — recovered to the low-120% range, up from the high-110% range in early 2025. Neither movement triggered any exit condition; both represent the thesis performing as written.

The stock-compensation line, which is the central financial tension for this business, showed marginal improvement on a revenue-share basis: compensation-related share awards amounted to roughly 20.5% of revenue in the quarter, down from 22.3% in the same period a year earlier. No acquisition above $2 billion was announced; no regulatory action of any kind was disclosed; no hyperscaler launched a product that changed the competitive landscape in a way not already reflected in prior filings.

Taken together, the filing is exactly the kind that keeps a thesis intact without moving the Share Price verdict: operationally strong, valuation unchanged, watch list unchanged.

About Datadog

Datadog sells subscription software that lets engineering teams watch what is happening inside their cloud-based applications in real time — measuring application performance, collecting system logs, catching errors, and, increasingly, monitoring artificial-intelligence workloads. A customer installs a small piece of software (called an agent) on every computing unit in its cloud environment; that agent continuously streams data back to Datadog's platform, where it is correlated across signals from different clouds and different application layers simultaneously. The result is a unified view of a customer's entire technology infrastructure — something that individual cloud providers' own monitoring tools, which see only their own cloud, cannot replicate for businesses that use multiple clouds at once.

The business compounds through a pattern called land-and-expand: a customer typically begins with one product (say, application performance monitoring), then adds further modules as their cloud usage grows — log management, security monitoring, AI workload tracking, and so on. As of March 2026, roughly 56% of Datadog's customers were using four or more products, and 11% were using ten or more, both figures higher than a year earlier. Each additional product deepens the cost and effort of switching to a competing platform, which is why existing customers have been expanding their spending by more than 20% per year on average.

The Case for the Bet

Datadog's moat rests on one simple observation: replacing Datadog after it has been embedded across a customer's infrastructure is not a procurement decision — it is months of re-instrumentation work. The case for owning the business is that this switching-cost franchise, combined with a multi-product expansion motion, will sustain above-average revenue growth and expanding cash-generation margins for long enough to justify ownership at a sufficiently low entry price. The following conditions are the ones that must hold for that case to remain intact:

  1. Dollar-based net retention rate stays above 112%. This metric measures the percentage of last year's revenue that existing customers are still paying, plus whatever additional products they have adopted. A reading above 100% means the installed base is growing without any new customers at all. The thesis holds that switching costs keep this metric sustainably above 112%; a sustained decline below that level would signal that hyperscaler native tools or AI-native competitors are beginning to displace Datadog within established accounts.

Latest reading: Low-120% as of 2026-03-31. The rate recovered from the high-110% range a year earlier, driven by increased usage from existing customers — the primary moat-health signal, and it is moving in the right direction.

  1. Gross margin stays above 77%. Gross margin (the share of every revenue dollar left after paying for cloud hosting, technical support, and third-party software) reflects Datadog's ability to pass through rising infrastructure costs. Sustained compression below 77% would indicate that cloud-hosting costs are forcing price absorption the business cannot offset, structurally impairing the economics of every customer relationship.

Latest reading: 79.2% in the three months ending in March 2026, essentially flat versus the same quarter a year earlier. The 4.2-point cushion above the exit floor remains intact; no compression trend visible.

  1. Share-based compensation below 25% of revenue with a credible normalisation path. Datadog compensates its employees partly with equity — shares in the company rather than cash. Because this dilutes existing shareholders, it is a real cost even though it does not appear as a cash outflow. At levels above 25% of revenue with no sign of coming down, the gap between stated cash generation and true economic earnings for each existing share grows wide enough that the growth story becomes illusory even in optimistic scenarios.

Latest reading: 20.5% in the three months ending in March 2026, improved from 22.3% a year earlier. Below the 25% threshold, with revenue growing faster than the absolute dollar amount of equity awards — a credible normalisation trajectory.

  1. Revenue growth above 20%. The base case assumes Datadog continues to grow at least 20% per year. Sustained deceleration below that pace for two consecutive quarters would signal either competitive displacement or a macro-driven halt to cloud spending — either of which would require revisiting the valuation framework.

Latest reading: 32% year-on-year revenue growth in the three months ending in March 2026 ($1,006 million versus $762 million in the same quarter a year earlier). Comfortably above the 20% floor; no deceleration trigger active.

  1. No single acquisition above $2 billion. Datadog has made technology acquisitions in the $10 million to $220 million range — targeted deals to close product gaps without betting the balance sheet. A deal above $2 billion at premium multiples would introduce integration complexity inconsistent with the current earnings base and would suggest management had shifted from disciplined platform assembly to defensive acquisition at inflated prices.

Latest reading: No acquisition of any kind was disclosed in the quarter ended March 2026. The track record of disciplined tuck-in acquisitions remains intact.

  1. No material regulatory enforcement action on cross-border data telemetry or AI training data. Datadog's agents collect detailed operational data from its customers' infrastructure, which creates exposure to data-sovereignty laws in the European Union and certain Asia-Pacific jurisdictions. A material fine under European data-protection law or a mandatory market-exit requirement affecting more than 5% of revenue would impose structural compliance costs and reduce the addressable market.

Latest reading: No enforcement action, regulatory fine, or market-exit order of any kind was disclosed in the quarter ended March 2026. Standard regulatory risk-factor language was reiterated, unchanged from prior filings.

The Share Price

At $261, Datadog's share price sits well above fair value by the method applied here. The valuation framework uses the cash the business generates each year (free cash generation, net of the equity compensation cost) as a proxy for owner earnings, then expresses the current price as a percentage yield on those earnings. At roughly 0.27%, that yield is a fraction of what it would need to be to represent fair value. The fair-value anchor for this holding is a 2.5% yield — equivalent to approximately $28 per share at current cash-generation levels — representing the midpoint between the conservative owner-earnings calculation and the growth-investor's preferred entry range.

A separate scenario analysis builds a range of three-year fair values from the ground up: a bear case ($37 per share, assigned 25% likelihood) where hyperscaler competition erodes the retention rate, a base case ($115 per share, 50% likelihood) where the business executes as planned, and a bull case ($603 per share, 25% likelihood) where AI observability becomes a category-defining product line. The two anchors — $28 from the yield method and $115 from the base-case scenario analysis — reflect different methodologies and are not contradictory; together they bound the "fair value" range. Both sit far below the current price. The details of this divergence are in Gaps in the Picture.

The Rules call for a trim of 25–33% of any existing position at the current price, and no new money to be deployed. No exit order exists in the written rules for this price level, because the valuation has not yet reached a point where the market is pricing in so many years of perfect compounding that the rules call for a full exit.

What's Wobbling

One condition is generating a mixed reading rather than a clean confirmation: the cash-generation margin. Free cash generation in the quarter ending in March 2026 was $289 million, healthy in absolute terms and above the 25% thesis floor, but the margin compressed by roughly 3.4 percentage points compared with the same quarter a year earlier. The cause was a combination of higher cloud-hosting costs and continued headcount growth. The margin has not crossed any threshold that triggers concern — the 25% watch floor and the exit condition floor are both well below the current 28.7% quarterly reading — but the direction of travel is worth noting.

Nothing else is wobbling. The retention rate is moving in the right direction, not against it. Revenue growth is accelerating, not decelerating. Share-based compensation as a share of revenue is improving, not worsening. The hyperscaler competition risk is unchanged — it is a structural background threat that the quarterly filings do not move in either direction. The regulatory situation is quiet.

The margin compression is the single item to track in the next two or three quarterly filings. If the cash-generation margin continues declining toward 25% as headcount investment accelerates faster than revenue, it would move from a watching condition to a watch-threshold condition, at which point a re-read of the Case for the Bet would be needed.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Hyperscaler bundling accelerates. Amazon Web Services, Microsoft's Azure platform, and Google's Cloud are all building monitoring tools as free additions to enterprise cloud contracts. The risk is not that these tools are already as capable as Datadog — they are not — but that every new cloud workload provisioned on a single platform is a potential foothold Datadog never gets. If this trend accelerates, the dollar-based retention rate would be the first metric to show it.
  • AI observability fails to add incremental revenue. Datadog's growth re-acceleration in early 2026 was partly attributed to AI-related workloads — customers monitoring large-language models and graphics processing unit clusters. If that usage grows but hyperscalers integrate comparable monitoring at no additional cost, the re-acceleration reverses.
  • Share-compensation dilution persists above 20% of revenue. At the current rate, Datadog issues roughly one-fifth of its annual revenue in equity awards each year. If this level does not decline as the company scales, the gap between stated cash generation and per-share owner earnings widens permanently, and growth that looks impressive at the company level translates into much slower growth in the value of each individual share.
  • A large acquisition at a stretched price. Datadog's balance sheet holds roughly $3.5 billion in cash and liquid assets. That is enough to fund a substantial acquisition. A deal above $2 billion at the kind of premium-to-revenue multiples common in enterprise software would introduce integration complexity and capital-allocation risk that the current thesis does not account for.
  • Source-code access incident tail risk. In April 2025, an unauthorised party gained access to a Datadog source-code repository. The company stated that no customer data was affected, and no further regulatory action was disclosed in subsequent filings. The tail risk — a delayed regulatory response or a broader security concern that damages enterprise trust — is low probability but non-zero.

Gaps in the Picture

Two fair-value estimates coexist and they disagree materially. The cash-generation yield method anchors fair value at approximately $28 per share (reflecting the current cash-generation level against the 2.5% target yield). The three-year scenario analysis produces a base-case estimate of approximately $115 per share (built from projected cash generation in three years, discounted at a 10% rate). Both are internally consistent; they are simply answering different questions. The $28 figure asks what the business is worth at today's cash-generation level with no assumed growth. The $115 figure asks what the business is worth if the base-case growth scenario plays out over three years. Neither is wrong. A future update will consolidate them into a single anchor; until then, both figures stand.

The owner earnings figure used throughout this evaluation nets out share-based compensation against stated free cash generation — a choice that matters. Investors who treat equity awards as a non-economic accounting charge will arrive at much higher valuations using the stated cash flow number ($1.0 billion). Investors who treat them as a real dilution cost to existing shareholders will arrive at the SBC-adjusted figure ($250 million). The two views produce valuations that differ by a factor of four.

The Rules

The following rules were set before this evaluation was run and are applied without amendment here.

If the thesis is confirmed — retention rate above 112%, revenue growing above 20%, no exit condition triggered — then hold or continue watching with no new position. The current yield of approximately 0.27% is well below any reasonable entry level; revisit only if the yield rises to at least 1.5% (equivalent to roughly $200 per share at current cash-generation levels).

If the thesis weakens — the retention rate declining toward 115%, or revenue growth falling below 20% without breaching the exit thresholds — then do not initiate a position. A weakening but not broken thesis at the current valuation has negative risk/reward and no action is warranted ahead of the next earnings cycle.

If the thesis breaks — the retention rate falling to 112% or below for two consecutive quarters, gross margin falling below 77% for two consecutive quarters, or share-based compensation rising above 25% of revenue with no credible plan to bring it lower — then do not enter, and if already holding, exit the full position within 30 days. No averaging down.

If the share price falls to the buy band — the cash-generation yield rising to 4.0% or above, equivalent to roughly $75 per share at current levels — then confirm the thesis is intact before acting. Specifically, the retention rate should be at or above 115% and the cash-generation margin at or above 25% for two consecutive quarters. If those conditions hold, initiate in two tranches over one quarter.

If the share price rises above the trim band — the cash-generation yield falling below 1.5%, equivalent to roughly $47 per share at current levels — then do not initiate a new position, and if holding, trim 25–33% of the position. The current price of approximately $261 already places this rule in its active state.

If the share price rises above the exit band — the market is pricing in so many years of compounding at no discount that reallocation to a holding with positive risk/reward is warranted — then exit the full position or remain out, and do not re-enter within 90 days. No exit threshold has been authored at this time; this rule awaits a price level to be set.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
