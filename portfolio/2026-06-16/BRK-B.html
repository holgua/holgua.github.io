Berkshire: The Bet Still Works

The Bottom Line

The compounding machine is running. Book value per share grew 10.5% in 2025 and edged higher still in the first three months of 2026, operating earnings rose in every business segment that matters, and a major legal liability at the energy subsidiary was reversed on appeal in April 2026. None of the four written conditions that would end this investment have come close to triggering. The share price sits almost exactly at fair value — neither a bargain nor expensive — so the position neither needs adding to nor trimming. The one thing to watch is whether Berkshire's car-insurance business, GEICO, can hold its competitive footing against a rival that has outmanoeuvred it on pricing technology over the past two years; that story is still unresolved.

About Berkshire

Berkshire Hathaway is a holding company that owns a large and deliberately diverse collection of businesses. The easiest way to understand it is as three concentric layers.

The innermost layer is insurance. Berkshire owns GEICO (one of the largest car insurers in the United States), a specialist reinsurance business called General Re, and several smaller insurance operations. Together these collect premiums that, in most years, more than cover claims — meaning the business earns a profit on the underwriting itself. More importantly, customers pay premiums before claims are settled, so Berkshire holds a large pool of money that belongs to claimants in the future but can be invested today. This pool, called the float, stands at roughly $177 billion. Because the insurance operations run profitably, Berkshire pays nothing to hold this float — it is, in effect, free money to invest. That is the engine room of the entire enterprise.

The middle layer is a set of wholly owned operating businesses: BNSF, one of the two railroads that dominate freight transport across the western United States; Berkshire Hathaway Energy (BHE), a regulated electricity and gas business operating across several US states; Precision Castparts, which makes aerospace components; Pilot Travel Centres, which operates a network of truck stops; and dozens of smaller manufacturers, retailers, and service businesses. These earn predictable cash flows and require relatively little new capital to sustain, making them excellent recipients for the insurance float.

The outermost layer is a publicly traded equity portfolio worth roughly $298 billion at the time of the latest filing — concentrated positions in companies like Apple, American Express, Bank of America, and Coca-Cola. This portfolio provides further compounding alongside the operating businesses.

Since January 2026, Gregory Abel has served as chief executive, following Warren Buffett's transition to the chairman role. Buffett remains a substantial shareholder. The central question for this investment — which the annual results will gradually answer over the coming years — is whether Abel's capital allocation judgement will match the record his predecessor built over six decades.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that Berkshire's management team will continue to reinvest the cash the business generates — and the float the insurance operations provide — into high-returning businesses and securities at rates that exceed what a shareholder could achieve independently. In other words: every dollar left inside Berkshire becomes worth more than a dollar in the hands of the shareholder, because the people running the capital allocation are better at finding uses for it.

For that bet to remain worth holding, five things need to keep working:

  1. Book value per share grows at a rate that beats the MSCI World index over any five-year stretch. If it trails for five consecutive years, the compounding edge is gone.
  2. Insurance float stays large and near-zero in cost. The float is the foundation of the investment engine. If underwriting losses become persistent, the float stops being free and the whole model degrades.
  3. The combined ratio of the insurance operations stays below 100%. A combined ratio (the ratio of claims plus expenses to premiums collected) below 100% means the insurance business is profitable on its own, before any investment income. Above 100%, Berkshire is paying to hold the float.
  4. Operating earnings keep growing. The railroad, the energy business, and the operating companies need to generate real cash earnings, not just accounting profits inflated by the equity portfolio's mark-to-market swings.
  5. Capital allocation remains disciplined. No large acquisition that reveals the new management lacks the pricing discipline Buffett built. The specific alert is any transaction above $20 billion at more than 1.5 times the target's book value, or one that subsequently requires a goodwill write-down of more than $5 billion.

The Rules

The pre-committed rules translate each state of the thesis and the share price into one clear instruction:

  • If the thesis is confirmed — all conditions working, no exit condition triggered: hold and let it compound. No action.
  • If the thesis is weakening — one or more conditions deteriorating but no exit condition crossed: do not add to the position. Assess the specific deteriorating driver at the next quarterly filing before deciding anything further.
  • If the thesis is broken — an exit condition has fired: exit the full position within thirty days. No averaging down.
  • If the share price falls below the buy-band edge: confirm the thesis is intact first. If it is, add to the position.
  • If the share price rises above the trim-band edge: trim 25 to 33% of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.
  • If the share price rises above the exit-band edge: exit the full position. At that price, the market is pricing in roughly ten years of base-case compounding instantly; the implied forward return falls below the long-term equity premium, and redeployment beats continuing to hold.

The Conditions Today

Book value growth versus the index. In the twelve months to the end of 2025, Berkshire's shareholders' equity grew 10.5% — from $649 billion to $717 billion. In the first quarter of 2026 alone it grew a further $9.8 billion to $727 billion, putting the per-share book value at approximately $337. No five-year trailing period shows book value trailing the index; this condition is working comfortably.

Insurance float. The float reached approximately $177 billion at the end of March 2026, up $500 million from year-end 2025. More importantly, the cost of that float was negative in the first quarter — meaning Berkshire was paid, on net, to hold it. The free-leverage pillar is intact.

Combined ratio — insurance underwriting. This is the one condition that shows a mixed reading. GEICO's combined ratio rose from 79.8% in the first quarter of 2025 to 87.3% in the same period of 2026, driven by higher claims frequency and severity. That is a meaningful increase — though GEICO is still profitably underwriting at 87.3%, well below the 100% break-even line. The complication is that the first quarter of 2025 had almost no catastrophe losses, making it an unusually flattering baseline. The aggregate insurance underwriting result across all of Berkshire's insurance operations improved year-over-year, from $1.7 billion to $2.3 billion pre-tax, because the reinsurance arm and the smaller speciality businesses more than offset GEICO's weaker quarter. The condition is met — but GEICO deserves a watchful eye. See What's Wobbling for the detail.

Operating earnings. Pre-tax operating earnings across Berkshire's businesses were $12.4 billion in the first quarter of 2026, up 6.5% from $11.6 billion in the same quarter of 2025. For the full year 2025, operating earnings rose 16.8% compared to 2024. BNSF posted its best quarterly earnings growth in recent memory — revenues up 4.8% and pre-tax earnings up 13.4% in the quarter, as freight yields rose and fuel costs fell. BHE contributed $744 million, up modestly year-on-year. This condition is working well.

Capital allocation discipline. Berkshire made one material acquisition in the period: OxyChem, a chemicals business, for approximately $9.5 billion in January 2026. That is Abel's first significant deployment of capital at parent level. At $9.5 billion it falls well below the $20 billion alert threshold, and the pricing has not yet triggered a goodwill concern. Buybacks in the first quarter were modest — about $235 million total — which is the appropriate pace for a share price sitting near fair value. The rules are clear that buying back stock above intrinsic value destroys value; the fact that buybacks slowed is the discipline working as designed. This condition is intact.

The Share Price

Berkshire's share price is assessed not against earnings (the large equity portfolio makes reported profits too volatile to be useful) but against book value — the accounting value of everything Berkshire owns minus what it owes. A price-to-book ratio of 1.45 (meaning the share costs $1.45 for every dollar of book value) has been designated as fair value. Below 1.25 is the buy zone; above 1.75 is the trim zone; above 2.0 is the exit zone.

At the time the latest data was gathered, the share price of $484 implies a price-to-book ratio of approximately 1.45 — essentially at the fair-value anchor. That is neither cheap nor expensive. The book value calculation uses year-end 2025 equity of $717 billion; if the first-quarter 2026 figure of $727 billion is used instead, the implied ratio falls marginally below 1.45. Either way, the share sits squarely at fair value.

The historical context is useful: Berkshire has rarely traded below 1.0 times book value over the past decade, and above 2.0 times only briefly during periods of market euphoria. The 1.25 to 1.75 band captures most of the price range that has prevailed during normal market conditions. Buying at 1.25 and holding until 1.75 has, historically, been a rewarding trade; the current price sits in the middle of that band.

What's Wobbling

Two things are worth watching, neither of which crosses any written exit condition today.

GEICO's claims costs. The car-insurance operation reported a combined ratio of 87.3% in the first quarter of 2026, up from 79.8% in the same quarter of 2025. The jump reflects higher frequency (more accidents per car insured) and higher severity (more expensive claims when accidents happen). The first-quarter 2025 comparison is a flattering one — that period had almost no catastrophe-related losses, making it an outlier benchmark. GEICO at 87.3% is still solidly profitable. The more relevant concern is longer-term: a rival carrier, Progressive, has used telematics-based pricing (pricing based on how an individual customer actually drives, measured by a phone app or a plug-in sensor) to grow market share while keeping its combined ratio lower. GEICO has been catching up on telematics, but policies-in-force — the most direct measure of whether it is winning or losing customers — is not broken out in the quarterly filing. That data comes from the annual letter, which is not yet available for 2026.

Share repurchases have been modest. Berkshire spent approximately $235 million buying back its own shares in the first quarter of 2026 — a small amount relative to its scale. Management described the buybacks as "relatively minor." The explanation is straightforward and consistent with the rules: at a price near fair value (1.45 times book), buying back stock is not meaningfully accretive. The buyback pace is a signal of capital discipline, not a warning sign. But it is worth noting that the cash fortress — roughly $369 billion in cash and short-term government securities — continues to sit largely uninvested. That cash earns a reasonable return on short-term government paper, but it earns far less than Berkshire's operating businesses do. The longer it sits, the more the story depends on Abel finding a genuinely good place to put it.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • A large acquisition at a poor price. The central risk of the Abel era is deploying the $369 billion cash pile into businesses that do not earn their cost of capital. One transaction above $20 billion at an inflated multiple could write off years of compounding in a single stroke. OxyChem at $9.5 billion is well within acceptable size; the next deal will be watched closely.
  • A catastrophic insurance year. A once-in-a-generation natural disaster, a pandemic-level liability event, or a cascading set of smaller catastrophes could consume a year's worth of underwriting profit and dent the float. Berkshire's scale provides resilience, but the insurance business is fundamentally exposed to low-probability, high-severity events.
  • The equity portfolio compresses significantly. Roughly $298 billion is concentrated in publicly traded securities, of which five names account for about two-thirds. A sustained bear market would reduce book value, pushing the price-to-book ratio up even as the share price falls — temporarily distorting the valuation signal.
  • GEICO loses market share structurally. If Progressive's telematics advantage proves durable and GEICO cannot close the gap, the car-insurance business will gradually shrink as a share of Berkshire's underwriting float. That would not break the thesis immediately, but it would slow the float's growth.
  • A regulatory reversal at BHE. The energy subsidiary's renewable-energy buildout was predicated on federal tax credits that were curtailed under legislation enacted in July 2025. The practical impact appears manageable — the existing installed base earns regulated returns regardless — but any further adverse regulatory change could impair BHE's investment pipeline.

Gaps in the Picture

Shares outstanding — extraction gap. Berkshire's dual-share-class structure (Class A and Class B) means the standard financial data extraction cannot directly pull a single diluted share count from the filed data. The validation context derived the figure from market capitalisation divided by share price ($1.04 trillion divided by $483.62), arriving at 2.15 billion Class B equivalent shares. Management discloses a per-share book value directly in the annual letter; cross-checking against that figure would confirm whether the implied share count is accurate.

GEICO policies in force. The most direct measure of GEICO's competitive position — the total number of policies it holds — is not disclosed in the quarterly filing. It appears in the annual letter. Without it, the assessment of whether GEICO is winning or losing customers against Progressive relies on the combined-ratio trend rather than the more decisive volume measure.

Buyback pause duration. Berkshire did not repurchase shares in 2025. The implicit signal is that management did not believe the share was trading below intrinsic value during that year. The formal buyback policy requires the price to be below intrinsic value by a meaningful amount and the cash balance to remain above $30 billion. Both conditions were presumably met in the direction of restraint throughout 2025. No public explanation was given beyond the standard policy language.
