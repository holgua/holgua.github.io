How to Read These Notes

Each note covers one position. It opens with where the price sits relative to the buy and trim edges, then walks through the evidence — filing excerpts, KPIs, and driver states — before stating the recommended action verbatim from the written thesis. The ranking table orders holdings by composite conviction score (quality-adjusted expected return above the linker hurdle). A higher rank means more upside per unit of quality at current prices, not a recommendation to buy more. The system is informational; all decisions are yours.

How the Ranking Works

The figure that follows each holding’s name above is its conviction score — one number that summarises how strongly the standing rules read the holding today. Higher means stronger conviction; lower means weaker. The score is a way to line the holdings up against each other on a single day; it is not a forecast of next year’s return.

Every holding’s score is built from the same three inputs and the same formula:

conviction_score = quality_weight × (expected_annual_return − single_company_handicap)

The handicap is subtracted in percentage-point terms, then the quality weight is applied as a multiplier. The three inputs come from three different places on disk and are described one by one below; the final score is recomputed every time the portfolio is ranked and lives in Portfolio/Ranking-{date}.md.

Input 1 — expected annual return

A single percentage figure (e.g. +12.50%, −0.50%) describing what the holding is expected to earn per year from today’s price, before tax, on the central scenario. The figure can be negative when the standing read is that the price has run ahead of what the business can support.

Source: each holding’s value-side write-up produces three figures — a bearish case, a central case and a bullish case. The ranking uses the central case only. The number is stored as forward_irr_base_pct in Holdings/{TICKER}/Analysis/{TICKER}-Ranking-Inputs.json, which is regenerated from the write-up whenever the holding is refreshed.

What it means: if the figure is +12.50%, the read is that a buyer paying today’s price would earn roughly that much per year if the conditions described in the per-holding note hold. A negative figure says the read is that today’s price is too high for the underlying numbers to keep up.

Input 2 — a small handicap for single-company holdings

A flat 2.0 percentage points, subtracted from the expected return of every single-company holding (an expected return of +12.50% becomes +10.50% after the handicap). Funds receive no handicap and pass the expected return through unchanged.

Source: a portfolio-level constant set in src/portfolio/rank_portfolio.py. Whether a holding counts as a single instrument or a fund is read from its asset_type field in the same Ranking-Inputs file (company and bond are single-instrument; etf is a fund and receives no handicap).

What it means: owning a single company concentrates risk that a broad fund spreads across hundreds. The handicap puts a small thumb on the scale against single-company bets so that a fund with the same headline return ranks slightly above an individual company. Two percentage points is a deliberate, tunable default — not a precise measure of the risk gap.

Input 3 — business-quality weight

A multiplier applied to the handicapped return. For single-company holdings, three settings:

Moat qualityMultiplierAwarded for
Wide×1.20A wide, durable competitive advantage that is hard for a rival to copy
Narrow×1.00A narrower advantage; left at face value
None×0.80No clear advantage; marked down

Source: the moat assessment is decided in each company’s value-side write-up and copied into Holdings/{TICKER}/Analysis/{TICKER}-Ranking-Inputs.json. Holdings backed by durable businesses are favoured because the same headline return is more likely to keep showing up year after year; holdings with no defence are discounted because the same return is less reliable.

Funds and bonds use the same 0.80/1.00/1.20 dial as companies. The dial measures durability of the cash-flow base — 1.20 means capital impairment is structurally hard (a deep moat for a company, a basket that IS the market for a fund, a sovereign-IG obligor for a bond); 0.80 means a single point of failure (no moat, a thematic single-sector basket, sub-investment-grade credit). Funds are bucketed from basket structure (sector breadth + top-10 concentration + sovereign-IG carve-out), not from look-through ROE — so a diversified market-cap fund and a tech-heavy concentrated fund are not awarded the same tier just because their top holdings happen to have similar profitability.

Putting it together — a worked example

For an example single-company holding the inputs are an expected annual return of +12.50%, the single-company handicap of −2.0 percentage points, and the wide-quality multiplier of ×1.20. The score is:

1.20 × (12.50 − 2.0) = 1.20 × 10.50 = +12.60

That is the figure printed next to the holding at the top of the list above. The same arithmetic runs for every other holding on the page.

Reading the score

A score comfortably above zero says the standing rules read the holding as worth holding from here. A score near zero says the read is roughly flat. A score below zero says the read is negative — the holding sits at the bottom of the list and rewards a careful look at the linked note. The size of the gap between two scores is meaningful (a holding at +27 is in clearly stronger territory than one at +4); the second decimal place is not.
