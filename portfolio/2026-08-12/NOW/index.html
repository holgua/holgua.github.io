About ServiceNow

ServiceNow sells software to large organisations — governments, banks, manufacturers, healthcare systems — that automates the internal processes employees use to get things done. When an IT team needs to provision a new laptop, when a finance department runs an approval workflow, when a security team responds to an alert, ServiceNow is typically the platform those processes run through. The company calls its product the Now Platform. It connects IT, human resources, security, and finance workflows in one data model, meaning the accumulated history of what employees have done and how processes are configured lives inside ServiceNow's infrastructure, not the customer's own systems.

That architecture is the source of the business's durability. Moving from ServiceNow to a competitor requires migrating years of accumulated workflow logic, thousands of software integrations, and the habits of tens of thousands of employees. The company charges annual subscription fees, billed in advance, which means cash arrives before the service is delivered — a structural advantage that keeps operating cash flow substantially higher than reported accounting profit. In the financial year ending December 2025, operating cash flow was $5.4 billion while reported net profit was $1.7 billion.

ServiceNow generated $13.3 billion in revenue in the year ending December 2025, of which 97% came from subscription contracts. It has approximately 8,700 enterprise customers, and its largest product category — IT service management, the digital helpdesk layer that enterprise IT departments run — commands roughly 42% of its market. The company has for the past two years been adding an artificial intelligence layer on top of its existing platform, branded Now Assist; this product crossed $1 billion in contracted annual value in the three months ending June 2026.

The Case for the Bet

The thesis rests on one proposition: ServiceNow holds a defensible position in enterprise workflow automation, built on switching costs that compound annually, and the company can monetise that position for years by raising prices and selling adjacent products to captive relationships. Seven specific conditions define the boundary of that proposition — any one of them failing would indicate the proposition is wrong:

  1. Subscription revenue growth above 15% per year. If growth falls below 15% for two consecutive quarters without a clear macroeconomic explanation, it suggests that AI competition from Microsoft and Salesforce is winning at the margin — new customers choosing alternatives or existing customers not expanding their subscriptions.

Latest reading: Subscription revenues grew 24.6% in the three months ending 2026-06-30, and 23.4% in the six months ending on the same date. Both are well above the 15% threshold. Growth was driven by purchases by new and existing customers across all workflow pillars.

  1. Net renewal rate at or above 97%. The renewal rate is the single most critical signal: it measures what fraction of expiring subscription value renews. Seven consecutive years at 98% is the empirical foundation of the switching-cost claim. A fall below 97% would indicate that contract value is eroding at the margin — that customers are choosing not to renew or are renewing at lower contract values.

Latest reading: The renewal rate was 98% for both the three and six months ending 2026-06-30, unchanged from the comparable 2025 periods. The seven-year streak is intact.

  1. Current remaining performance obligations growth above 18%. Remaining performance obligations (the total contracted revenue that has not yet been recognised, essentially the forward pipeline) growing at 18% or more confirms that new and expanded contracts are replacing expiring ones at a pace that supports the growth thesis. Two consecutive quarters below 18% would signal that the contracted backlog is weakening.

Latest reading: Total obligations were $29.0 billion as of 2026-06-30, up 21% from a year earlier. The near-term tranche — obligations due within the next twelve months — also grew 21%. Both are above the 18% threshold.

  1. Now Assist AI product reaching $2.5 billion in contracted annual value by the end of 2026. Now Assist is the AI pricing layer added on top of existing subscriptions. Failing to reach $2.5 billion by the end of 2026 would indicate that AI monetisation is stalling and the primary incremental revenue driver above the base platform is not delivering.

Latest reading: The quarterly filing does not separately disclose the Now Assist contracted annual value. The most recent public figure — $1 billion, disclosed in relation to the June 2026 quarter — comes from company communications outside the filing. Whether the path to $2.5 billion by year-end is on track cannot be assessed from this filing alone.

  1. Reported gross margin above 70% for any given quarter. The condition requires two consecutive quarters below 70% to trigger — one quarter is a warning, not a confirmation. The Armis and Veza acquisitions have introduced large non-cash amortisation charges into cost of revenues; the question is whether the underlying business retains its economics once those charges are absorbed.

Latest reading: Reported gross margin was 70.7% in the three months ending 2026-06-30, down from 77.5% in the same period a year earlier. The margin has not yet breached 70%. Management has guided that gross margin will continue to decline through the end of 2026. One quarter of printing at or below 70% starts the two-quarter clock; Q3 2026 is the decisive test.

  1. No debarment notice or formal exclusion proceeding from the Department of Justice. The investigation centres on a conflict-of-interest hire in ServiceNow's federal sales channel. A debarment would directly impair approximately 13% of revenues (the concentration of a single federal channel partner that has risen from 11% to 13% of total revenues between 2025 and the June 2026 quarter).

Latest reading: The investigation is ongoing as of 2026-07-23. No debarment notice or formal exclusion proceeding has been issued. The company continues to cooperate and deliver documents to the Department of Justice and the Army Suspension and Debarment Office.

  1. Non-GAAP operating margin above 28%. This metric excludes stock-based compensation and acquisition-related charges. Falling below 28% for two consecutive quarters would indicate that AI investment is consuming economics faster than it generates incremental revenue.

Latest reading: Non-GAAP operating margin was 29.4% in the three months ending 2026-06-30 and 30.6% in the six months ending on the same date. Both are above the 28% threshold, though compressing from roughly 31% a year earlier, driven by elevated stock-based compensation (up 31% in the June quarter) and acquisition-related costs.

The Share Price

At $125, the share price sits above the trim line. The owner earnings yield — the company's cash generation net of stock-based compensation, expressed as a percentage of market capitalisation — stands at 2.03%. The fair-value yield anchored in the thesis is 3.87%, which corresponds to a price of approximately $66 per share. At $125, the market is pricing the business at roughly 91% above that estimate of intrinsic value.

To put that concretely: at $125 and with owner earnings of $2.6 billion, an investor buying today pays approximately 49 times what the business generates for them after accounting for stock-based compensation. The trim threshold was set at 2.37% — approximately $107 per share — on the basis that above that level the stock prices in growth expectations that exceed even the optimistic scenario. At the current reading, trimming is called for rather than adding. The exit threshold sits at $291 per share, which is not approached.

A separate peer-based estimate of fair value, using market multiples from comparable software businesses rather than a direct cash-flow model, arrives at approximately $105 per share. That higher estimate uses reported cash flow before netting out stock-based compensation — a methodology that produces a more favourable reading. The thesis framework anchors to the more conservative number because stock-based compensation of $1.95 billion per year is a real cost to shareholders, not a bookkeeping entry. Both methodologies agree that the stock is materially overvalued relative to intrinsic value; they disagree on the magnitude. The discrepancy is noted in Gaps in the Picture.

What's Wobbling

One condition is in challenged territory: gross margin. The reported gross margin of 70.7% in the three months ending June 2026 sits less than one percentage point above the 70% floor. The challenge is almost entirely a consequence of the Armis and Veza acquisitions: when ServiceNow paid $6.4 billion for Armis and roughly $500 million for Veza, it acquired intangible assets that are now amortising through the cost of revenues line at approximately $160–190 million per quarter. That is an accounting charge, not cash leaving the business, but the exit condition is written against reported numbers. Management has guided that the gross margin will continue to decline through the end of 2026. Two consecutive quarters below 70% trigger exit condition 5. The September 2026 quarter print is the next decisive data point.

Two other conditions are mixed and worth watching, though neither approaches a threshold. The Department of Justice investigation into the Army procurement conflict-of-interest remains open. No escalation to formal debarment proceedings has occurred, and the company continues to win new federal contracts. What has changed is the concentration: the single federal channel partner now accounts for 13% of revenues, up from 11% a year ago, which increases the materiality of exit condition 6 if the investigation were ever to escalate. The non-GAAP operating margin is also compressing year-over-year — from approximately 31% to 29.4% in the June quarter — driven by stock-based compensation growing faster than revenue and acquisition-related costs. The margin remains above the 28% threshold.

The conditions that form the core of the switching-cost thesis — subscription growth, renewal rate, contracted pipeline growth — all confirmed cleanly in the June filing. The moat itself is not wobbling. The wobble is in the economics layer that sits between the moat and the reported numbers, introduced by the decision to acquire three large companies in twelve months.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Gross margin falls below 70% for two consecutive quarters. Management has guided further compression through the end of 2026. If Q3 and Q4 both print below 70%, exit condition 5 triggers — even though the underlying cause is non-cash amortisation from the acquisitions rather than deteriorating unit economics on the platform.
  • The Department of Justice investigation escalates to debarment proceedings. The investigation has been open since mid-2024 with no escalation, but a formal debarment notice targeting the federal channel would impair approximately 13% of revenues immediately and call into question the GSA OneGov federal-platform positioning.
  • AI competition erodes new logo capture, compressing subscription growth toward 15%. Microsoft's productivity suite and Salesforce's enterprise relationship network give each of them distribution leverage that ServiceNow cannot match. A world where enterprise buyers choose to expand within existing Microsoft or Salesforce relationships rather than add a ServiceNow subscription would show up first in growth deceleration.
  • Armis, Moveworks, or Veza integration proves harder than expected. Three acquisitions totalling more than $9 billion, closed across twelve months, represent the most significant operational execution test ServiceNow has run. Goodwill impairment, management turnover from integration friction, or acquired-product revenue that does not materialise would each signal a capital allocation mistake at scale.
  • Stock-based compensation remains above 10% of revenue into 2027 and beyond. At 14.7% of revenue in the year ending December 2025, stock-based compensation costs real-dollar value to shareholders through dilution. Management has committed to reducing this below 10% by 2029. If the commitment slips, cash generation net of dilution continues to understate the apparent cash-flow multiple.

Gaps in the Picture

The underlying numbers carry three caveats worth naming.

First, the maintenance versus growth split of capital expenditure is not separately disclosed. The valuation framework uses total capital expenditure as a conservative proxy for maintenance capital needs, which understates owner earnings somewhat — the true maintenance requirement for a cloud software platform is likely a fraction of total spending. The figures used are nonetheless a reasonable conservative anchor.

Second, two fair-value estimates coexist and they disagree substantially. The thesis framework anchors to approximately $66 per share, derived from a direct cash-flow model using owner earnings net of stock-based compensation. A separate peer-multiple analysis, using market comparable ratios applied to reported cash flow before the stock-based compensation deduction, arrives at approximately $105 per share. Both approaches agree the stock is overvalued at $125; they disagree on by how much. A future update to the valuation framework will resolve the methodology question.

Third, the Now Assist contracted annual value — the metric that defines exit condition 4 — is not disclosed in the quarterly filing. The only figure available is from company communications indicating the product crossed $1 billion in the June 2026 quarter. Whether the path to $2.5 billion by year-end 2026 is on track cannot be verified from the public filing.

The Rules

If the thesis conditions are broadly intact (most drivers confirming, all seven exit conditions unbroken), the rule is to hold the existing position with no new capital added at the current price. The stock trades at a 91% premium to the base-case intrinsic estimate; holding is the appropriate response to a high-quality business at a high price, not adding.

If the thesis shows signs of weakening — one condition challenging, not yet breaking — the rule is to hold without adding, give the business two quarterly filings to demonstrate stabilisation, and not treat any price pullback as a buy-the-dip signal given the starting overvaluation. A weakened thesis at these prices is a hold at reduced conviction, not an entry opportunity.

If any of the seven exit conditions fires — if the thesis breaks — the rule is to exit the full position within 30 days with no averaging down. At a 91% premium to intrinsic, a broken thesis provides no price cushion whatsoever.

If the share price falls to or below $47 (owner earnings yield crossing 5.37%), and all seven exit conditions remain unbroken, the rule is to add to the position in tranches across one or two quarters. That price level represents a 25% margin of safety on the base-case intrinsic estimate — the first genuinely attractive entry point.

If the share price rises to or above $107 (owner earnings yield crossing below 2.37%), the rule is to trim 25–33% of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding. At $125, the stock already sits above this level.

If the share price rises to or above $291, the rule is to exit the full position. The market would be pricing in roughly 7 years of base-case owner earnings compounding instantly; the implied forward return falls below a reasonable long-term equity premium, and redeployment into a cheaper alternative is called for. No re-entry within 90 days thereafter.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
