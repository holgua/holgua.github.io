Recent Development

The first quarter of 2026 was a confirmation, not a catalyst. Total payment volume grew 11% year-on-year to $1.88 trillion on an annualised basis, and the number of individual payment transactions rose 7% compared with the same quarter a year earlier. The buyback programme continued at pace — 34 million shares retired in three months at an average price of $44.60, bringing the share count down from 920 million at the end of 2025 to 892 million by the end of March 2026. The company's remaining buyback authorisation of $12.4 billion is roughly a third of its current market capitalisation.

What the quarter did not resolve is the single biggest uncertainty in the thesis: whether PayPal's share of branded-checkout transactions is eroding structurally. The quarterly filing provides no dedicated metric for this. Two pending class-action lawsuits — the Darcy and Norfolk County cases — allege that the company concealed impediments to its branded checkout strategy in 2025, covering the period when management was publicly guiding to 2027 targets. These suits are at the pleadings stage and are compensation cases, not regulatory enforcement; they do not prove a 2-percentage-point share loss, which is the specific threshold in the exit conditions. But they leave the thesis's most sensitive driver in an unresolved state.

One secondary wobble: the quarterly operating margin declined to 18% from 20% a year earlier, driven by a rising share of lower-margin Braintree-processed transactions in the mix. The full-year 2025 operating profit of $6.1 billion remains firmly above the $5.5 billion exit-condition floor — that threshold has not been crossed. The condition is not triggered, but the direction warrants monitoring. See The Case for the Bet for the full set of conditions and their current readings.

About PayPal

PayPal operates a two-sided digital payments network: consumers hold payment credentials in a PayPal account, and merchants accept PayPal because their customers expect it. The relationship is mutually reinforcing — more merchant acceptance attracts more consumers, and vice versa. The network has 439 million active accounts across roughly 200 countries and territories.

Revenue comes from a percentage of each transaction through its branded checkout (the "Pay with PayPal" button); from Braintree, its unbranded payment infrastructure for large merchants; from Venmo, its peer-to-peer payments app expanding into commercial transactions; and from Buy Now, Pay Later products.

The business is capital-light — roughly 2.5 cents of physical investment per dollar of revenue — and earns 15% on the capital deployed. It generates $5 billion or more in annual cash surplus above investment needs, directed almost entirely into buying back its own shares. That programme has retired roughly a fifth of the total share count since 2021.

The Case for the Bet

The thesis is that PayPal's market price reflects a fear of structural decline that the actual financial results do not support, and that disciplined capital return — buying back shares at distressed prices — is sufficient to compound per-share value even if revenue growth stays modest.

The conditions that must keep holding for the bet to work:

  1. Branded checkout share loss does not exceed 2 percentage points in a single fiscal year. PayPal's consumer-checkout share — roughly 47% in the US — is the primary moat metric. If competitors displace it at a rate of more than 2 percentage points per year, that signals secular displacement rather than cyclical softness, and the network's commercial value begins to erode more rapidly than buybacks can offset. The exit condition is specific and quantitative to avoid triggering on noise.

Latest reading: The quarterly filing for the three months ending March 2026 provides no branded-checkout volume breakdown. Total PayPal-branded revenue grew $140 million year-on-year. Two class-action suits allege undisclosed impediments to branded checkout growth, but no data has been disclosed that confirms the 2-percentage-point threshold.

  1. No regulatory consent order materially restricts core product features. Open civil investigative demands are normal for a business of this scale; the exit condition requires a consent order that actually restricts what PayPal can offer customers.

Latest reading: No consent order is in place as of the March 2026 filing. US regulatory demands remain outstanding; UK's Financial Conduct Authority opened new investigations in March 2026. The PPH Securities action was dismissed with prejudice. Exit condition (2) is not triggered.

  1. Consumer credit charge-off rate stays below 10%. PayPal extends credit through its Pay Later products; sustained consumer default rates above 10% would divert resources from the buyback programme.

Latest reading: Consumer net charge-off rate fell to 3.0% annualised in the first quarter of 2026, from 4.5% a year earlier. Transaction loss rate held at 0.06% of total payment volume. Exit condition (3) is not triggered.

  1. Management maintains the buyback programme without taking on excessive debt. Consistent share retirement at distressed prices is the bet's core mechanism; suspension, large acquisitions, or leverage above approximately 2.0 times net debt to operating profit breaks it.

Latest reading: 34 million shares repurchased in the three months ending March 2026 for $1.5 billion at an average of $44.60 per share; $12.4 billion of authorisation remains. Share count fell from 920 million to 892 million in the quarter. Exit condition (4) is not triggered.

  1. GAAP operating profit stays above $5.5 billion annually. If annual operating profit falls below $5.5 billion, or management explicitly guides non-GAAP operating profit below $5.0 billion for the following year, margin discipline has been abandoned.

Latest reading: Full-year 2025 GAAP operating profit was $6.065 billion, 13.9% above 2024 and well above the exit-condition floor. Q1 2026 operating margin declined to 18% from 20%, driven by Braintree mix, but the annual trajectory remains intact. Exit condition (5) is not triggered.

The Share Price

The current share price of $46 sits in the lower half of the hold band — above the buy edge of $41 (where the business's earnings yield reaches 12.5%) but with room before the trim edge at $60 (where the yield would fall to 8.5%). The fair-value anchor — the price at which the earnings yield equals the long-run equilibrium rate of 10.5% — is $48. The share is 5% below that anchor today.

In plain terms: at $46, the business generates about $11.1 cents of owner earnings for every dollar of market capitalisation. Per the Rules: above 12.5 cents (i.e., below $41) the price is cheap enough to add; below 8.5 cents (i.e., above $60) the price is rich enough to trim; above 6.4 cents (i.e., above $78) the price is expensive enough to exit. Today's reading sits comfortably in the middle zone where the appropriate action is to hold and monitor the conditions.

At under 8.6 times trailing earnings and below the company's own book value (the net assets on the balance sheet), the share price encodes a view that PayPal's earnings will decline materially and permanently. The filed accounts do not support that view: return on invested capital rose from 12.5% to 15.0% in 2025, operating profit grew 13.9% year-on-year, and the buyback programme is actively compressing the share count. The gap between what the market price implies and what the accounts show is the source of the investment opportunity — and also the principal reason why the bet carries risk, since the market may be correct about a deterioration that the latest figures have not yet confirmed.

What's Wobbling

Two things are wobbling without crossing a line.

The first, and more material, is the branded-checkout disclosure gap. The company does not provide a branded-checkout transaction volume figure in its quarterly filings, which means the primary exit trigger — a 2-percentage-point share loss in a single year — cannot be directly measured from the filings. The Darcy and Norfolk County class actions add legal uncertainty on top of the information gap: if the plaintiffs' allegations are proven, they would suggest the disclosed figures understated the branded-checkout challenge during 2025. The suits are at the pleadings stage and involve monetary damages, not regulatory enforcement; no data has yet confirmed the 2-point threshold. But the combination of a missing metric and active litigation creates a posture of "monitor carefully, do not add" rather than "accumulate with confidence."

The second wobble is the operating margin step-down in the first quarter of 2026. The cause — Braintree's unbranded processing volume growing faster than the high-margin branded checkout business — is structural rather than cyclical, and it will persist as long as that mix continues to shift. The full-year 2025 operating profit figure ($6.1 billion) provides enough headroom above the $5.5 billion exit condition that a single quarter of margin compression does not trigger a response. If the mix effect compounds across two or three further quarters without a branded-checkout offset, the trajectory becomes more concerning.

Neither wobble fires an exit condition today. Both warrant continued monitoring at the next quarterly filing.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Branded-checkout share loss proves structural. If artificial-intelligence agents, Apple Pay, or competing checkout tools displace PayPal's consumer-button share by more than 2 percentage points in a fiscal year, the exit condition fires. This has not been confirmed — but neither has it been disproved.
  • A regulatory consent order restricts core products. Active civil-investigative demands and new UK investigations could convert into enforcement actions limiting what PayPal can offer — particularly its buy-now-pay-later product.
  • Management fails the CEO transition. Enrique Lores brings operational rigour but no payments background. The highest-risk scenario is an early large acquisition that redirects buyback capital at the worst possible moment.
  • Agentic commerce routes around PayPal. If OpenAI's Stripe-powered checkout standard or Visa's agent-commerce rails become the default for AI-assisted purchases, the 439-million-account network risks being bypassed. PayPal's integrations with AI assistants are defensive; the protocol competition is unresolved.
  • Consumer credit cycle deterioration. Charge-off rates improved in early 2026 but remain elevated. A sustained macro downturn could push rates toward the 10% exit-condition threshold and simultaneously impair transaction volume.

Gaps in the Picture

Owner earnings are computed as free cash flow net of stock-based compensation, without separating maintenance from growth capital expenditure. If a meaningful portion of the $852 million in 2025 capital spend is genuinely growth investment, the true earnings yield is somewhat higher than reported — making the valuation slightly conservative.

The fair-value anchor ($48) is derived from an earnings-yield method anchoring to current earnings power at a 10.5% equilibrium yield. A separate bottom-up scenario analysis derives a base-case fair value in the range of $90–$141 per share. Both are legitimate; the yield method captures what the business earns today, the scenario analysis captures projected growth. The $48 figure drives the Standing Orders; the $90–$141 range drives the three-year scenario returns in How the Score Adds Up.

Two fair-value anchors coexist: the yield-method figure of 48 and the bottom-up scenario figure of 141 (peer/self multiples). A future schema migration will resolve the divergence.

The branded-checkout share metric — the most important variable in the thesis — is not disclosed in quarterly filings. The analysis relies on proxies rather than the direct figure.
