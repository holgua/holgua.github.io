About the Bond

The Federal Republic of Germany issued this bond (international securities identification number DE0001030583) in February 2021, with a maturity date of 2033-04-15. It is an inflation-linked sovereign bond — a type of government debt that ties its principal and coupon payments to a published price index. In Germany's case, the index is the Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices excluding tobacco, maintained by Eurostat. As inflation rises over the life of the bond, the principal value adjusts upward, and each coupon payment (set at 0.10% of the index-adjusted principal) rises in step. A buyer is therefore locking in a real return — a return measured in purchasing-power terms — rather than a nominal one.

The mechanics work through what is called the index ratio. At issuance the index ratio was 1.0, meaning €100 of face value corresponded to €100 of inflation-adjusted value. By 2026-06-22 the index ratio had grown to 1.25978, reflecting the cumulative German consumer-price inflation since 2021. A buyer today pays the dirty price of €119.62 per €100 original face value at settlement, not €94.90. The €94.90 is the quoted clean price in real terms (same purchasing-power units as the issuance date). To get the actual euros that change hands, multiply by the index ratio (1.25978), then add accrued interest.

The bond is a straightforward AAA-rated sovereign obligation. Germany has never defaulted on a EUR-denominated debt. Credit risk here is effectively zero. The sole economic risk a holder bears is that the real yield turns negative and stays negative long enough that it would have been better, in purchasing-power terms, to have held EUR cash instead.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is structural: this bond is held as a permanent inflation-hedge allocation within the portfolio, not as a trade on interest-rate direction. The fundamental proposition is that a AAA sovereign linker paying a positive real yield preserves purchasing power more reliably than EUR cash or conventional (non-inflation-linked) bonds, because its principal grows with actual inflation rather than depending on the bond market's forecast of it.

Three numbered conditions must remain inactive for the holding to stay open:

  1. Real yield remains above −1.0%. If the bond's real yield falls below −1.0% and also EUR cash offers a higher after-tax real return for two consecutive quarters, the inflation-hedge benefit has evaporated. That combination — persistently negative real return plus a better alternative available — triggers exit condition (1).
  2. Germany's sovereign credit rating stays above AA−. Exit condition (2) fires if any two of the three major rating agencies (Fitch, Moody's, and S&P) downgrade Germany below AA−. A downgrade of this magnitude would represent a structural shift in issuer creditworthiness that the original thesis did not price in.
  3. The HICP ex-tobacco index methodology remains intact. Exit condition (3) fires if Eurostat materially restructures the index in a way that severs the link between the index and realised consumer-price inflation — for example, substituting a different reference index entirely.

All three conditions are monitoring-based, not price-based. The bond's clean price is almost irrelevant to whether the bet is working; what matters is the real yield the price implies and whether the three exit triggers remain dormant.

The Rules

The pre-committed action plan translates each possible state of the bet into an instruction:

  • If all three exit conditions are dormant and the real yield is within the holding band (−0.50% to 1.50%): Hold. The inflation-hedge is working as intended.
  • If any driver is weakened but no exit condition has fired (e.g. real yield drifting below 0% but above −0.50%): Do not add. Re-assess against EUR cash and conventional bonds before making any move.
  • If any exit condition fires: Close the position. Move proceeds into nominal short-duration German government bonds or EUR cash.
  • If the real yield rises above 1.50% (the bond becomes cheap in real-yield terms): Add to the position. A 7-year AAA EUR linker yielding above 1.50% in real terms is an unusually attractive entry price.
  • If the real yield falls below −0.50% (the bond becomes expensive): Trim 25% on the first crossing. If the real yield is still below −0.50% at the next quarterly review (at least 90 calendar days later), trim a further 25%. The ladder stops there — no further trims are triggered by additional crossings unless exit condition (1) fires.
  • If the compound exit trigger fires (real yield below −1.0% for at least 90 days and EUR cash offering a superior real return): Exit the full position immediately. The inflation-hedge function is no longer adding value relative to the available alternative.

What's Wobbling

No driver has moved into a challenged or weakened state. The closest thing to a wobble is the marginal 1-basis-point increase in break-even inflation between the 2026-06-19 and 2026-06-22 readings (1.93% to 1.94%), driven by a 1-basis-point rise in the nominal yields on the two bracketing German bonds. This is not material — the driver remains firmly within its acceptable range and the change is within the noise floor of a single-business-day read.

One structural change to watch is the anticipated shutdown of the Bundesbank's bond-data service around 2026-06-30. After that date, the pipeline that fetches the nominal Bund yields used to compute break-even inflation may lose its data source. If that happens, the break-even reading will become unavailable at the next refresh rather than staying current. This has no impact on the recommendation today — the real yield driver and the compound trigger are computed independently — but it is worth noting for the next quarterly review.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • ECB rate cuts compress the cash-comparison advantage. If the ECB cuts rates materially, the real return on EUR cash falls. This actually widens the linker's advantage, not narrows it — lower ECB rates favour the linker. The risk runs the other way only if rate cuts also compress real yields on the linker itself (e.g. because inflation expectations rise simultaneously).
  • Deflation scenario. If consumer-price inflation falls substantially below zero, the index ratio stops growing and the inflation-adjustment that gives the bond its value evaporates. At sustained deflation the linker effectively becomes a conventional low-coupon bond at an unattractive real yield.
  • ECB rate rise above break-even. A sharp rise in the ECB deposit rate that pushes the real cash yield well above the linker's real yield would activate the cash-comparison leg of exit condition (1). It would need to persist for two consecutive quarters alongside a real yield below −1.0% to trigger the exit — but a sustained period of very high ECB rates and simultaneously compressed linker real yields would qualify.
  • Index methodology drift. Even short of the outright Eurostat restructuring that triggers exit condition (3), gradual changes to the HICP basket composition could cause the index to diverge from the inflation households actually experience. This is a slow-moving risk with no current signal.
  • Data-source loss. The Bundesbank's bond-data shutdown expected around 2026-06-30 may require a manual data-sourcing step for break-even inflation at the next quarterly refresh. If the break-even figure becomes unavailable, the compound exit trigger can only be assessed on the real yield leg — the cash-comparison leg remains computable from the ECB deposit rate directly.
