The Bottom Line

Data as of 2026-07-12 · Latest filing 2026-05-04.

The bet is working. Berkshire's operating businesses — its insurance division, its railroad, its energy utility, and hundreds of smaller companies it owns outright — are compounding value at the pace the bet requires, and no written exit condition is close to triggering. The one thing to watch closely is Berkshire's car-insurance business, GEICO, where the latest quarter showed higher claims costs, though the company's aggregate underwriting result actually improved year-on-year and GEICO remains solidly profitable.

Recent Development

The most significant development in Berkshire's latest quarterly filing — covering the three months ending in March 2026 — was a legal ruling in Oregon. The Oregon Court of Appeals reversed a jury verdict that had held Berkshire's energy subsidiary, Berkshire Hathaway Energy, liable for damages from PacifiCorp's 2020 wildfires in the state. The reversal materially reduces the worst-case legal exposure that has hung over the energy business for several years, with bonds totalling around $719 million potentially dischargeable as a result. The energy unit also agreed to sell its Washington state operations to Portland General Electric for approximately $1.9 billion in cash, a further step in simplifying its regulatory exposure.

The compounding machinery itself continued to run well. Berkshire's total equity — the broadest measure of the assets built up on behalf of owners — grew by a further $9.8 billion in the quarter to $727.2 billion. Operating earnings, which strip out the large and frequently misleading swings caused by accounting rules on the investment portfolio, rose 6.5% in the quarter compared to the same period a year earlier. Berkshire's railroad business, BNSF, had a strong quarter: pre-tax earnings rose 13.5% to $1,820 million on revenues up 4.8%, with the operating ratio — a measure of how much it costs to run each dollar of revenue through the railroad — improving to 65.6%.

The single wobble in an otherwise strong quarter was GEICO. The car-insurance business saw its combined ratio (the proportion of every premium dollar consumed by claims and expenses) rise to 87.3% from 79.8% in the same quarter a year earlier, driven by higher claims frequency and severity. The comparison is somewhat flattering to the prior year — the first quarter of 2025 was unusually clean — and GEICO at 87.3% remains comfortably profitable. But the shift is real and bears watching, as addressed in The Standing Orders and What's Wobbling.

About Berkshire

Berkshire Hathaway is, in structure, a holding company — a business that owns other businesses rather than making one product or selling one service. What makes it unusual is the mechanism through which it funds those acquisitions. The core of the model is insurance. When Berkshire's insurance businesses — which include GEICO for car insurance, Berkshire Hathaway Reinsurance Group for large specialty risks, and several other carriers — collect premiums from policyholders, those premiums sit on the balance sheet as a liability (claims not yet paid) and as investable cash. This pool of cash, which Berkshire calls its "float," stood at approximately $176.9 billion at the end of the first quarter of 2026. The crucial detail is that Berkshire earns money on this float — through investment returns — while paying almost nothing for it. In fact, the insurance operations have been profitable enough in recent years that the float has effectively had a negative cost: the insurance businesses have brought in more in premiums than they have paid out in claims and expenses, meaning policyholders have, in aggregate, been paying Berkshire to hold their money.

That float funds a second engine: wholly-owned operating businesses. BNSF is one of two large freight railroads covering the western half of the United States. Berkshire Hathaway Energy is a regulated electricity and gas utility operating across multiple US states with significant renewable generation assets. These are capital-intensive businesses that earn predictable, regulated returns rather than spectacular ones, but they generate substantial and consistent cash. The third engine is a concentrated stock portfolio — roughly $298 billion at year-end 2025, with large positions in a handful of companies — that compounds independently of the operating businesses.

Warren Buffett, who built this structure over six decades, handed the chief-executive role to Greg Abel at the start of 2026. Buffett remains chairman. The central unresolved question for the investment case is whether Abel will deploy capital with the same discipline — buying durable businesses at sensible prices — or whether the extraordinary record the structure produced under its founder will prove difficult to replicate at its current scale.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that Berkshire's management will keep compounding retained earnings and the insurance float into high-returning businesses at rates that justify holding the shares rather than an index fund. Four conditions must keep holding for the bet to remain intact:

  1. Book value per share must not trail a broad world equity index for five or more consecutive years. Book value per share (the total assets minus liabilities, divided by the number of shares) is the broadest single measure of how much wealth Berkshire is creating per share. The condition sets an exit trigger: if this growth rate trails the MSCI World Index for five or more years in a row, it would suggest the compounding edge is gone.

Latest reading: Shareholders' equity grew by 10.5% in the full year 2025 compared with 2024, reaching $717.4 billion. In the first quarter of 2026, equity grew a further $9.8 billion to $727.2 billion, with book value per share at approximately $337. The condition is firmly intact.

  1. No large acquisition that reveals a breakdown in capital discipline. The specific trigger is any single transaction above $20 billion where the price paid is above 1.5 times the book value of the target, or where a goodwill write-down above $5 billion follows within a few years. A write-down of that size would signal that the price paid was wrong — that Abel overpaid for something that turned out to be worth less than he thought.

Latest reading: The only significant acquisition in the filing period was OxyChem, a chemical business acquired in January 2026 for approximately $9.5 billion — comfortably below the $20 billion threshold. No goodwill impairment has been recorded. The condition is intact.

  1. No structural culture change that abandons the decentralised owner-operator model. Paying a regular cash dividend, breaking the company into separate listed entities, or capitulating to activist investors who want to change the structure would signal that the model is being dismantled.

Latest reading: No dividend has been initiated. No break-up or activist engagement is disclosed. The quarterly filing contains no management succession disclosures. The condition is intact.

  1. The price-to-book ratio must not exceed 2.0 times. At that point, the valuation would be pricing in aggressive compounding already delivered — and the opportunity cost of holding against a passive index would become clearly negative, regardless of the quality of the underlying business.

Latest reading: The price-to-book ratio at the current share price of $504 and year-end 2025 equity stands at approximately 1.52 times. Using the more recent March 2026 equity figure of $727.2 billion, the implied ratio is approximately 1.50 times. Both are comfortably below the 2.0 exit line.

The Share Price

The share price at $504 sits just above the fair-value anchor of $481, which corresponds to a price-to-book ratio of 1.45 — the level that reflects a quality franchise with some discount for the uncertainty around the chief-executive transition. That puts the price in the upper half of the hold range: above fair value but well below the trim line of $581.

The price-to-book measure is the right lens here because Berkshire's earnings are too volatile to use as a direct anchor — the accounting rules require Berkshire to mark its massive equity portfolio to market through the income statement every quarter, which produces swings of tens of billions of dollars in reported profit that say nothing about the underlying health of the business. In 2022, Berkshire reported a loss of $22.8 billion; its operating businesses were all profitable that year.

What's Wobbling

One driver is showing a mixed reading rather than a clean confirmation: GEICO's combined ratio.

In the three months ending in March 2026, GEICO's combined ratio — the percentage of every premium dollar consumed by claims and administrative costs — rose to 87.3%, up from 79.8% in the same period a year earlier. Higher claims frequency (roughly 5–7% more claims per insured car) and higher claims severity (costs per claim up 12–14%) drove the deterioration. GEICO's pre-tax underwriting earnings fell to $1,416 million from $2,173 million in the prior-year quarter.

Two things soften the reading. First, the first quarter of 2025 was an unusually clean period with near-zero catastrophe losses, which makes the year-on-year comparison look worse than it would against a normal benchmark quarter. Second, a combined ratio below 100% means GEICO is still profitably underwriting — it took in more in premiums than it paid out — and the aggregate underwriting result across all of Berkshire's insurance businesses actually improved, as other units more than made up for GEICO's step-down.

The exit condition for this driver is framed as a market-share and competitive concern — not a single quarter's combined ratio print. The specific watch item is whether Progressive's use of telematics-based pricing (pricing each driver based on their actual driving behaviour rather than demographic categories) is pulling better risks away from GEICO at a rate that structurally erodes the business. That signal would show up in policy-count trends, which are not separately disclosed in quarterly filings and require the annual shareholder letter for a fuller read.

Share repurchases in the quarter were described by management as "relatively minor" — approximately $235 million in total — consistent with the policy of buying only when the price is clearly below intrinsic value. At a price-to-book ratio close to the fair-value anchor of 1.45, the current price does not clear that bar.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Abel's capital allocation proves inferior. The first major deployment — OxyChem at approximately $9.5 billion — is a defensible but unremarkable acquisition of a commodity chemical business. Two or three more allocations are needed before a verdict on Abel's quality is fair. An acquisition above $20 billion at a stretched price would immediately fire the exit condition.
  • A large catastrophe event. Berkshire's insurance businesses write specialty risks — aviation, nuclear liability, very large property losses — that other insurers cannot absorb. A single extreme event above $15 billion in net losses would temporarily push float costs from negative into positive territory and reduce operating earnings materially.
  • Wildfire liabilities at Berkshire Hathaway Energy escalate further. The Oregon reversal is a significant positive, but other wildfire cases remain active, and the cumulative accrual still stands at $2.9 billion. A resumption of adverse rulings in other jurisdictions would reopen the tail-risk question.
  • GEICO loses ground to Progressive. Progressive's telematics pricing model is structurally faster at segmenting good risks from bad ones. If GEICO's combined ratio deteriorates to above 100% for several consecutive quarters and market-share data show policies leaving, the competitive moat becomes a narrowing one.
  • The equity portfolio drops sharply. Approximately $298 billion in a concentrated set of holdings — with a large portion in a handful of names — is exposed to correlated drawdown. A 30% decline in the portfolio would subtract roughly $89 billion from reported equity, though it would not impair the operating businesses.

Gaps in the Picture

Share count data carry a caveat: Berkshire's share structure (two classes of share, with each Class A share equivalent to 1,500 Class B shares) is not straightforwardly extractable from the standard structured filing data. The diluted share count used here — approximately 2,162.7 million Class B equivalents — is derived indirectly from the market capitalisation divided by the share price. It should be cross-checked against the per-share book value that Berkshire discloses directly in its filings; the latest quarterly report states per-B-share book value of approximately $337, which is consistent with the derived figure but is not identical to it.
