Recent Development

The headline from Elastic's annual filing for the year ending 30 April 2026 is that the business continued to accelerate on its two most watched metrics while the stock barely moved from where it was when the filing period began. Customer expansion — measured by how much more existing customers spend year over year — came in at 112%, seven percentage points above the level at which the written exit condition for this metric would fire. Cloud-hosted revenue grew 22% for the year, reaching 48% of total revenue, which is more than double the 10% threshold below which the exit condition for platform commoditisation would trigger.

The other development worth noting is what did not happen. The company acquired one small business during the year — an artificial intelligence embedding specialist called Jina AI, bought for approximately $36.8 million — which is well below the $500 million threshold at which an acquisition without a credible cash-flow return plan would constitute an exit condition. Stock-based compensation, the primary ongoing concern in the thesis, declined to 17.73% of revenue from 18.19% the prior year, breaking the consecutive-year streak that would have required a harder look at the exit condition for compensation discipline. The margin to the 18% threshold remains thin, so this is a watch item rather than a cleared item, but the direction is right.

The full picture — all seven exit conditions confirmed or remaining silent, across eight monitored thesis drivers — puts the evaluation at a straightforward hold relative to the Standing Orders and the buy edge described in the Share Price.

About Elastic

Elastic N.V. is an enterprise software company that makes its money from organisations that need to search, monitor, and secure large volumes of data. Its core product is the Elasticsearch platform — a distributed search and analytics engine originally developed as an open-source project by the company's co-founder, Shay Banon, and now extended into three overlapping use cases: enterprise search and artificial intelligence retrieval, infrastructure observability (monitoring the health of software systems in real time), and security event monitoring for threat detection.

The business model is subscription-based. Customers pay either to run Elastic software themselves on their own servers, or to run it through Elastic Cloud, the company's hosted service. Hosted revenue has been growing faster than self-managed revenue for several years — it was 48% of total revenue in the financial year ending April 2026, up from 43% two years earlier — and that shift matters because cloud subscriptions grow organically as customers ingest more data. Elastic earns roughly 76 cents of gross profit on every dollar of subscription revenue. The company employs roughly 3,700 people, reports under Dutch law as a public company listed in New York, and had approximately $1.74 billion in annual revenue in its most recent financial year.

One detail worth naming explicitly: Elastic earns a meaningful portion of its revenue from large enterprise customers, and its two co-founders, Banon and Steven Schuurman, retain roughly 12% of the company's shares between them. The remaining 88% is held by institutions. Elastic has no dividend.

The Case for the Bet

The central argument is that Elastic has built software so deeply embedded in its customers' operations that replacing it would be more disruptive than simply paying the renewal bill. Every year that a customer runs Elasticsearch in production — indexing logs, powering search applications, or ingesting security events — the corpus of indexed data and the operational knowledge built around that corpus grows. Ripping out the platform requires re-architecting query logic, retraining operations staff, and migrating data that may be measured in petabytes. The customer expansion rate of 112% is direct evidence that this friction is working: customers are paying about 12% more each year on average without being priced out.

The specific conditions that must keep holding for this argument to remain sound are:

  1. The customer expansion rate stays above 105%. This metric measures how much more existing customers spend year over year. If it falls below 105% for two consecutive quarters, it signals that the switching-cost moat is no longer generating durable expansion — customers are reducing or churning rather than deepening. The 105% level was chosen because a rate below that, sustained, would imply net contraction in the installed base.

Latest reading: 112% as of 30 April 2026, seven percentage points above the exit threshold. The count of customers spending more than $100,000 per year grew from over 1,510 to over 1,720 during the financial year, and the count spending more than $1 million per year reached over 240. Customer deepening is active.

  1. Elastic Cloud revenue growth stays above 10% annually. The cloud-hosted product is the primary mechanism through which Elastic compounds revenue per customer over time. A deceleration below 10% for a full financial year would confirm that Amazon's competing open-source search product (OpenSearch, which forked from the original Elasticsearch codebase and is now maintained by a foundation with over 1,400 contributors) is commoditising the platform in the segment where growth matters most.

Latest reading: 22% growth in the year ending April 2026, with the annual-contract portion of cloud revenue growing 28%. The cloud mix reached 48% of total revenue, up from 43% two years prior. No sign of deceleration toward the exit floor.

  1. The cash-flow margin stays above 14%. Operating cash flow minus capital expenditure — a measure of how much actual cash the business generates for every dollar of revenue, after spending on the equipment and infrastructure required to run it — must stay above 14% of revenue on an annual basis. Below that level, the description of Elastic as a capital-light business generating durable cash becomes harder to sustain, and the investment case weakens materially.

Latest reading: 18.5% in the year ending April 2026, on $321.8 million of operating cash flow less $5.1 million of capital expenditure, divided by $1,739.3 million in revenue. That is 450 basis points (about 4.5 percentage points) above the exit floor, and management has guided to approximately 21.5% for the following financial year.

  1. Stock-based compensation stays below 18% of revenue for two consecutive years. Elastic pays a substantial portion of its workforce in shares rather than cash. In financial terms, those shares dilute existing shareholders over time in a way that cash salary does not. The exit condition requires the compensation ratio to exceed 18% for two consecutive years before triggering — one elevated year is not automatically disqualifying.

Latest reading: 17.73% in the year ending April 2026, down from 18.19% the year before. The prior year at 18.19% was the first of a potential consecutive-year streak; FY2026 at 17.73% breaks that streak. The multi-year trend is improving (20% → 18.19% → 17.73%), but the distance to the 18% threshold remains thin enough that it warrants watching at the next annual filing.

  1. No acquisition exceeds $500 million at a revenue multiple above 8 times without a disclosed path to cash-flow return within three years. This condition exists because acquisitions at high multiples without credible returns destroy owner value. The thresholds were chosen to be objectively verifiable from regulatory filings — price and revenue multiple are disclosed in acquisition announcements; the accretion commitment would need to appear in writing within the same announcement.

Latest reading: The only acquisition in the year ending April 2026 was Jina AI, an artificial intelligence embedding and search company, for approximately $36.8 million in cash. That is more than 13 times below the $500 million threshold. Capital discipline was intact for the year.

  1. The accounting operating margin reaches positive territory by the financial year ending April 2028. The company has been running at an accounting operating loss, primarily because stock-based compensation is counted as an expense under accounting rules even though it does not involve a cash payment. The trajectory of improvement — from a loss of roughly 3.7% of revenue in FY2025 to a loss of 1.9% in FY2026 — implies the accounting margin could turn positive by FY2028. If it does not, it would suggest the compensation problem is structural rather than transitional.

Latest reading: Accounting operating loss narrowed by 39% year-over-year, from $(54.9) million to $(33.5) million, driven by gross margin expansion to 76%. The improvement in FY2026 was approximately 1.8 percentage points. At that pace, the FY2028 target is reachable.

  1. Datadog's revenue growth does not exceed Elastic's by more than 15 percentage points for two consecutive years while Elastic drops below Leader status in the Gartner Observability ranking. This is a competitive-displacement condition. It requires two things to happen simultaneously — Datadog accelerating away from Elastic on the observability growth metric AND Elastic's recognition in the independent analyst ranking deteriorating. Either condition alone is not sufficient to trigger an exit; both must fire for two consecutive years.

Latest reading: The annual filing names Datadog as a primary competitor in the observability segment but discloses no comparative revenue growth data and no Gartner ranking data, because those come from external sources. No exit condition is triggered. This driver must be checked against Datadog's own filings and Elastic's investor relations materials, not from the annual filing alone.

The Share Price

The evaluation system puts Elastic's fair value at $101 per share, derived from the base-case discounted-cash-flow model that assigns a 15% annual cash-flow growth rate and a 10% cost of capital over a three-year period. Against today's price of $59, the share is deeply below fair value on that measure — roughly 42% below, in absolute terms.

The yield-based measure (how much annual cash flow the business generates divided by the price a buyer would pay today) reads 5.17% at $59. The buy edge for adding sits at a yield above 5.5%, which corresponds to a share price around $56. The trim edge for harvesting some of the position sits at a yield below 2.2%, which corresponds to roughly $140. At $59, the share is in the lower half of the hold band — above the level where the Rules call for adding, but well below the level where they would call for trimming.

In plain terms: by the measure used to set the bands, the share is cheap but not cheap enough to trigger the pre-committed add rule. It is close. A decline of roughly $3 in the share price — less than 5% — would move it below the buy edge.

Why the Market Sees It Differently

The market is pricing Elastic's shares as though the business will grow its cash flow at roughly 5.8% per year over the coming years. The evaluation's base case assumes 15% annual growth — a gap of more than nine percentage points. That gap is what creates the 41% distance between the current price and the fair-value estimate, and it is severe enough to fall into the highest tier of divergence in the evaluation system.

The straightforward reading of why the market sits where it does involves three hypotheses. First, the market may be pricing the stock-based compensation problem as permanent — if Elastic never meaningfully compresses its compensation from 17% of revenue, the true economic earnings for ordinary shareholders remain close to zero regardless of how fast headline cash flow grows, because the growth largely accretes to employees. Second, the market may assign substantially more weight to the bear scenario than the thesis does: a 20% probability on a bear case that implies a cash-flow growth rate of around 9% would, in aggregate, pull the implied price down toward what the market currently shows. Third, the market may be discounting a structural competitive shift — specifically, that Amazon's open-source search product will commoditise the on-premises segment while Datadog continues to outgrow Elastic in observability, gradually compressing Elastic's addressable market over time.

The current filing data does not support the second or third hypothesis as the primary driver. The customer expansion rate remains at 112%, cloud revenue is growing at 22%, and no exit condition has tripped. The first hypothesis — the compensation dilution discount — is the most defensible reading of the divergence. The question the market is implicitly asking is whether management will actually compress stock-based compensation toward 12–14% of revenue by FY2029, as the base-case thesis requires. The answer will come from the next three annual filings, not from this one.

What's Wobbling

Two items merit attention, though neither changes the hold call at today's price.

The stock-based compensation ratio is the one wobble that sits close to a threshold. At 17.73% of revenue in FY2026, it is declining — but only by about half a percentage point per year. The exit condition requires it to exceed 18% for two consecutive fiscal years. FY2025 at 18.19% was the first such year; FY2026 at 17.73% broke the streak. If FY2027 returns above 18%, the streak would restart, and a second consecutive year above 18% would trigger the exit condition. Management's compensation plan does not contain an accounting-earnings or cash-flow hurdle, which means there is no structural incentive built into the plan to compress this ratio. It requires deliberate management will, not automatic plan mechanics.

The competitive-position driver returned a silent reading this cycle — the annual filing names Datadog as a competitor but discloses no comparative growth data and no Gartner ranking information. Exit condition 7, which requires Datadog's revenue growth to exceed Elastic's by more than 15 percentage points for two consecutive years AND Elastic's Gartner Observability ranking to drop below Leader status simultaneously, cannot be evaluated from the annual filing alone. It requires checking Datadog's own filings and Elastic's investor relations materials. That check is not part of the annual-filing re-validation; it is a separate monitoring task.

The share price is also approaching the buy edge from above. At $59, it is roughly $3 above the $56 level where the buy rule would activate. A further decline would move it into buy territory, which would require confirming thesis integrity before acting — something the most recent annual filing supports.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • The stock-based compensation never converges. This is the primary economic risk in the holding. If compensation stays permanently at 15–17% of revenue, the cash flow generated by the business accretes largely to employees rather than to shareholders through dilution. Owner earnings — true economic earnings after adjusting for the dilutive cost of equity-based pay — were approximately $24 million in FY2026 on $1.74 billion of revenue. At that level, the current share price implies a multiple of more than 260 times true owner earnings. The entire investment case depends on management compressing that ratio over the next several years. If they do not, the return to ordinary shareholders will be much lower than the headline cash-flow figures suggest.
  • Amazon's OpenSearch erodes the self-managed segment faster than anticipated. The open-source fork now has more than 1,400 contributors and is sponsored by a foundation with technology-industry backing. It has replicated much of the functionality of older versions of the platform and costs nothing for organisations willing to manage it themselves. If enterprise IT departments increase their tolerance for managing open-source search infrastructure, the self-managed renewal base could shrink faster than the cloud segment can replace it.
  • Datadog widens the observability growth gap. Datadog was growing at approximately 28% per year versus Elastic's 17% at the time of the most recent assessment. If that gap persists or widens for two fiscal years while Elastic's Gartner ranking deteriorates, exit condition 7 would fire. The two conditions must happen simultaneously, which makes the trigger specific, but the competitive dynamic in observability is unfavourable and needs watching.
  • The federal revenue ramp does not materialise on the expected timeline. Elastic obtained a FedRAMP High authorisation in June 2026 and has a government blanket pricing agreement in place. Federal revenue is currently sub-material — below 2% of total revenue. If government IT budget cuts under current austerity programmes delay or reduce the expected ramp, the growth projections embedded in the base case would need to be revised downward.
  • A large acquisition destroys capital. Management has $160 million remaining in its buyback authorisation and a $1.37 billion cash balance. There is no stated commitment to use that capital at current prices. A poorly priced acquisition at a high revenue multiple without a credible path to cash-flow return would constitute an automatic exit condition and represent a permanent destruction of shareholder value.

The Rules

The rules below are the pre-committed conditions that govern what action follows from each state of the evaluation. They are stated as-is; the choice of whether and when to act on them belongs to whoever holds the shares.

  • If the thesis is confirmed (all drivers within tolerances, no exit condition triggered): hold the position. Do not add unless the price falls into the buy band — yield above 5.5%, corresponding to roughly $56 on the FY2026 cash-flow figure. Monitor the customer expansion rate and cloud revenue growth quarterly; confirm the stock-based compensation ratio at each annual filing.

  • If the thesis shows signs of weakening (one or more drivers moving in the wrong direction but no exit condition yet triggered): do not add. If the customer expansion rate falls toward 107% or cloud revenue growth decelerates below 15% for two consecutive quarters, re-run the evaluation and assess whether to reduce the position. One quarter of deterioration is not sufficient to confirm a structural break; two are required before acting.

  • If the thesis breaks (any written exit condition fires): exit the full position within 30 days. The seven exit conditions are: customer expansion rate below 105% for two consecutive quarters; cloud revenue growth below 10% for a full financial year; cash-flow margin below 14% on an annual basis; stock-based compensation above 18% for a second consecutive financial year; an acquisition above $500 million at a revenue multiple above 8 times without a disclosed cash-flow accretion plan within three years; accounting operating margin still negative by the financial year ending April 2028; or Datadog's revenue growth exceeding Elastic's by more than 15 percentage points for two consecutive fiscal years while Elastic's Gartner Observability ranking falls below Leader status. No averaging down on a thesis break.

  • If the price falls into the buy band (yield above 5.5%, approximately $56 or below): confirm first that no exit condition has tripped. If the thesis is intact, add to the position in tranches. Do not deploy more than half of the intended position in a single purchase — the bear case implies a permanent loss of roughly 49% from today's price, which is severe enough to require staged entry with confirmation between purchases.

  • If the price rises into the trim band (yield below 2.2%, approximately $140 or above): trim 25% of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding for continued compounding.

  • If the price rises into exit territory (cash-flow yield below 1.5%, approximately $200 or above): exit the entire remaining position. At that price, the market would be pricing in essentially the full bull case, and the asymmetry of holding further would have inverted. No re-entry within 90 days.
