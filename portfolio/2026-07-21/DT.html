About Dynatrace

Dynatrace sells software that monitors other software — specifically, the cloud-based applications and infrastructure that large enterprises depend on. When a bank's mobile app slows down, when a retailer's checkout system starts throwing errors, when an airline's booking platform behaves unexpectedly, the operations teams responsible for fixing the problem need to know what broke and why. Dynatrace's platform watches every component of those environments in real time, draws a live map of how they connect to each other, and uses an AI engine — the company calls it Davis — to pinpoint the root cause automatically, rather than leaving engineers to sift through logs manually. The platform is deployed by installing a single software agent on each server, container, or cloud service in a customer's environment; the agent collects data autonomously and feeds it into the central platform. As of the most recent fiscal year, roughly 4,100 enterprise customers were subscribed, and 96% of revenue came from recurring subscriptions.

The commercial model is important. The primary offering is the Dynatrace Platform Subscription (DPS), a consumption-based contract under which customers pay for the volume of data they collect and the range of capabilities they activate — observability, security analytics, log management, and AI-native monitoring can all be layered on as the customer's needs grow. This structure means that as a customer expands its cloud footprint, its spending on Dynatrace tends to grow with it, without requiring a new procurement cycle. It is the structure behind the 110% net revenue retention figure: on average, each existing customer spent 10% more than the previous year purely by using more of what they already had.

The Case for the Bet

The core investment premise is that Dynatrace's deep integration into enterprise technology stacks makes it expensive to replace, that the consumption model naturally expands as customers migrate more workloads to the cloud, and that the business will generate growing free cash flow for at least the next several years without requiring heavy capital investment. The thesis stands if the following conditions remain true:

  1. Annual recurring revenue grows at least 13% in constant currency for any consecutive two-quarter period. Revenue growth is the primary signal of competitive health. If growth falls below that floor for two consecutive quarters, it indicates the platform is losing ground to competitors — and the thesis is broken.

Latest reading: ARR reached $2,054 million at 31 March 2026, up 18% year-on-year in constant currency. Growth has been: 25% (FY2023), 24% (FY2024), 18–19% (FY2025 and FY2026). The deceleration from 25% to 18% over three years is visible, but the current level is well above the exit floor.

  1. Net revenue retention stays at or above 107% on a trailing twelve-month basis for at least two consecutive quarters. Net revenue retention measures whether existing customers are expanding or contracting. A reading above 100% means the installed base is growing; a reading below 107% for two quarters would signal that competitive pressure or budget cuts are eating into the subscription base.

Latest reading: 110% for FY2026 (same as FY2025, down from 111% in FY2024). Three consecutive years at or above 110%. The trend is flat rather than improving, but it is not deteriorating toward the 107% watch level.

  1. Free cash flow margin stays above 20% for any full fiscal year. The cash generation rate, measured as a percentage of total revenue, is the structural health signal for the business. A sustained margin below 20% would indicate that cost inflation or defensive spending is eroding the economic returns of the subscription model.

Latest reading: 26.2% for FY2026 ($529 million on $2,018 million of revenue), up from 25.5% the prior year. The five-year range is 25–29% — consistent and expanding.

  1. No senior executive (chief executive or chief financial officer) sells shares in the open market for more than $5 million within any 90-day window. Large open-market sales by the people closest to the business are treated as a disqualifying signal of insider-level loss of confidence.

Latest reading: No chief executive or chief financial officer open-market sales disclosed in the fiscal year. The chief revenue officer sold approximately $340,000 of restricted stock in December 2025, a routine vesting transaction. The company repurchased $479 million of its own shares in FY2026 — the direction of management's own capital is toward more ownership, not less.

  1. Dynatrace Platform Subscription represents at least 72% of annual recurring revenue over any three consecutive quarters, and management continues to disclose the metric. The DPS penetration figure measures how broadly customers have migrated from legacy point-tool licences to the consumption platform. A stall below 72% would suggest the expansion engine has stopped, which matters because DPS is the mechanism behind the 110% net revenue retention.

Latest reading: The annual filing for FY2026 does not disclose DPS as a percentage of annual recurring revenue. The platform is described extensively in qualitative terms, but the numeric penetration figure was not published. This driver is currently silent — it cannot confirm or challenge the thesis until the disclosure resumes in a quarterly earnings release.

The Share Price

At $44.65, the share price sits modestly above the fair-value anchor of $41.30. That anchor is computed from the cash the business generates: $529 million of annual free cash flow divided by the share count gives a per-share yield of approximately 4.07%, and the thesis treats 4.4% as the fair-value yield. Because the current yield (4.07%) is slightly below the fair-value yield, the price is slightly above the fair-value level. The gap is small — about 8% — but it means there is no meaningful margin of safety at today's price.

The buy edge sits at $31, where the yield would reach 5.9% — 1.5 percentage points above the fair-value level. The trim edge sits at $55, where the yield would compress to 3.3% — indicating that the market has priced in more than the base case delivers. Both edges are well away from where the price sits today; the share is in the middle of the hold band, not near either threshold.

It is worth understanding what the fair-value anchor does and does not capture. The 4.4% yield threshold is calibrated to today's normalised cash generation, not to growth projections. A business growing its cash flow at 16–18% per year is worth more than a flat-cash-flow business at the same yield — but the method deliberately does not embed that growth into the fair-value anchor, because growth is not guaranteed. The market appears to take a more sceptical view: the reverse calculation of what long-term growth rate is implied by the $44.65 price suggests approximately 10–11% annual cash-flow growth, well below the base case of 16–17%. That gap between what the market assumes and what the base case expects is the source of the potential upside embedded in the TL;DR — and the reason the thesis remains intact despite the modest premium to the fair-value anchor.

What's Wobbling

Two signals from the most recent filing deserve attention, though neither has crossed a line that the thesis treats as a threshold.

Subscription gross margin — the percentage of each subscription-revenue dollar left after the cost of delivering the service — compressed by one percentage point in FY2026, from 86% to 85%. The cause is higher cloud-hosting costs as customers migrate to the SaaS-delivered version of the platform rather than the on-premises agent. The total gross margin (which includes service revenues) actually improved by one percentage point to 82%, softening the picture. Neither figure approaches an exit condition — the only cash-flow floor in the thesis is a 20% free-cash-flow margin, which the business is exceeding at 26%. But a second consecutive annual compression in subscription gross margin would raise questions about whether the SaaS transition is being absorbed cleanly or whether pricing needs to adjust. Watch for this in the FY2027 results.

The competitive consolidation around Dynatrace is accelerating. The 2024 Cisco-Splunk deal and the 2026 Palo Alto–Chronosphere deal mean that two of Dynatrace's most significant competitive threats — the networking/security incumbents with existing enterprise relationships — have now acquired dedicated observability assets. The filing also names emerging AI-native monitoring tools as new competitive vectors. The relevant counter-evidence is the enterprise deal count: 22 contracts above $1 million in annualised contract value in the final quarter of the fiscal year. That number shows that at the top of the enterprise market, Dynatrace is still winning. The exit condition for competitive displacement requires confirmed named-customer losses to Cisco-Splunk or Palo Alto–Chronosphere, which has not occurred. The signal remains a watch, not a trigger — but it bears careful monitoring against the Case for the Bet as the next few quarterly filings arrive.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Cisco's distribution machine puts Splunk-integrated observability in front of every Dynatrace account that runs Cisco networking or security infrastructure, offering discounts the relationship cannot easily refuse. Revenue retention starts compressing toward 107%, then below.
  • Datadog expands its full-stack platform to match Dynatrace's depth in AI-powered causal analysis, eroding the technical differentiation that keeps large enterprise accounts locked. New logo wins slow; existing accounts stop expanding.
  • The SaaS infrastructure cost of the Dynatrace Platform Subscription grows faster than the company can pass on through pricing, compressing free-cash-flow margin from the current 26% toward the 20% threshold. A full fiscal year below 20% triggers the thesis break.
  • Enterprise IT budgets contract sharply in a macro downturn. Observability is operationally critical but not immune to cost-cutting in a severe environment; deal cycles lengthen and ARR growth decelerates below 13%.
  • Bernhard Greifeneder, the co-founder and chief technology officer whose team built the Davis AI engine, departs without a disclosed succession plan. The causal-AI architecture is the core of the switching-cost moat; a management transition at the technical leadership level is a moat-integrity event, not a routine personnel change.

The Rules

The following rules govern what happens in each state of the holding. They were set when the analysis was written and do not change in the moment.

  • If the thesis is confirmed — the key conditions (net revenue retention, annual recurring revenue growth, free-cash-flow margin) are all holding — then maintain the full position and continue monitoring the quarterly metrics. Consider adding modestly on dips below $38 while net revenue retention stays at or above 107%.
  • If the thesis is weakening — one or more conditions are deteriorating toward but not yet breaching a threshold — then reduce to a half position within 30 days of the trigger and monitor for two additional quarters before deciding whether to rebuild or exit.
  • If the thesis is broken — any exit condition is confirmed (net revenue retention below 107% for two consecutive quarters, annual recurring revenue growth below 13% for two consecutive quarters, free-cash-flow margin below 20% for a full fiscal year, or confirmed enterprise displacement by Cisco-Splunk or Palo Alto-Chronosphere) — then exit the full position within one quarter. Do not add on a broken thesis.
  • If the share price falls below the buy edge — cash-flow yield reaches 5.9% or above, implying a price of approximately $31 — then accumulate toward a full position, provided the thesis remains intact. Stage the purchase across one or two quarters.
  • If the share price rises above the trim edge — cash-flow yield falls to 3.3% or below, implying a price of approximately $55 — then trim 25–33% of the position to harvest the premium. Do not exit fully unless the thesis is simultaneously breaking.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
