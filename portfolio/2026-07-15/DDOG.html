Recent Development

Revenue at Datadog grew 32% in the three months ending in March 2026 compared with the same period a year earlier — a clear re-acceleration from the prior year's 26–28% annual pace. The most important single data point from that filing is that the company's customer-expansion rate, measured as trailing-twelve-month dollar-based net retention, improved from the "high-110%'s" a year ago to the "low-120%'s" as of March 2026. In plain terms: each dollar that existing customers spent a year ago became roughly $1.20 this year, driven by those customers adding more Datadog products to their environments, not by winning new logos.

Two other figures reinforce the picture. The count of customers spending at least $100,000 per year grew from 3,770 to 4,550 — a 21% increase — and those accounts now represent 90% of Datadog's total revenue run-rate, up from 88%. The proportion of customers using ten or more products jumped from 6% to 11%. Every incremental product a customer ingests deepens the integration footprint and raises the cost of switching to an alternative.

The single wobble in the quarter was a contraction in cash-generation margin: in the three months ending in March 2025, Datadog converted 32% of revenue into free cash flow; in the equivalent period this year, the figure was 29%. Higher cloud-hosting costs and additional headcount drove the compression. The margin remains well above the threshold at which a structural concern would arise, and seasonal patterns explain part of the gap. For what the pre-committed plan calls for at the current price, see the Standing Orders.

About Datadog

Datadog makes money by helping companies understand what is happening inside their software and the infrastructure that runs it. When a business deploys an application to a cloud computing environment — on servers operated by Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, or Google Cloud — that application generates a continuous stream of signals: whether components are running, how quickly they are responding, whether error rates are rising, how much computing capacity they are consuming. Datadog collects those signals from every layer of the stack and surfaces them through a single interface, replacing the patchwork of point tools — one for logging, another for performance metrics, a third for security alerts — that engineers would otherwise have to run and stitch together themselves.

The company charges for the volume of data ingested and monitored, which means revenue grows automatically as its customers' cloud deployments expand. The model works because once an organisation has connected its entire infrastructure to a Datadog monitoring agent — the small software programme that sits on every server, container, and computing function — pulling it out is a multi-month engineering exercise rather than a purchasing decision. That stickiness is the core of the investment case. The platform has expanded from a handful of monitoring products to more than 25, including tools for application security, synthetic testing, and, most recently, monitoring the performance of large-language-model applications and the graphics-processing-unit clusters that power them. Revenue for the year ending December 2025 was $3.4 billion, up 28% from the prior year.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that Datadog's switching-cost moat is deep enough and durable enough to compound revenue at above-market rates for long enough to justify a substantial purchase price — and that the right entry point exists somewhere below the current share price. Four conditions must hold for that case to remain intact:

  1. Customer-expansion rate stays above 112%. The company does not publish a precise retention percentage, but it discloses a qualitative band each quarter. A rate above 112% means the installed customer base is still growing revenue organically; below that level for two consecutive quarters, the land-and-expand flywheel has slowed structurally, likely because cloud providers' own monitoring tools have become good enough for enough customers to slow Datadog's expansion.

Latest reading: As of March 2026, the trailing twelve-month retention rate was in the "low-120%'s," up from the "high-110%'s" a year earlier. The re-acceleration directly contradicts any near-term concern about flywheel slowdown. The exit condition is not approached.

  1. Gross margin stays above 77%. The portion of revenue left over after paying for third-party cloud hosting and direct service costs (roughly 80 cents on every revenue dollar) measures whether Datadog can pass cost increases through to customers or must absorb them. A sustained fall below 77% would signal that rising infrastructure costs are structurally impairing the unit economics of each customer relationship.

Latest reading: Gross margin was 79% in the three months ending in March 2026, unchanged from the same quarter a year earlier. Revenue and hosting costs grew proportionally. No deterioration.

  1. Share-based pay stays below 25% of revenue, with a credible glide-path to normalise. Datadog generates roughly $1 billion of free cash flow per year on $3.4 billion of revenue — a healthy number on paper. But the company also grants its engineers and executives roughly $750 million of share-based pay each year, which costs shareholders in dilution even though it does not flow through reported cash flow. When compensation consumed more than 25% of revenue without a plan to reduce it, the gap between cash flow and true per-share value creation would be wide enough to undermine any valuation case even in the bull scenario.

Latest reading: In the three months ending in March 2026, share-based pay consumed 19.6% of revenue, down from 21.6% in the same quarter a year earlier. Revenue is growing faster than compensation. The trend is in the right direction.

  1. Revenue growth stays above 20% for successive quarters. At Datadog's current size, 20% annual revenue growth is the base case for a business with roughly 120% net retention — it is approximately what the installed base delivers before new logo additions. Sustained growth below 20% for two consecutive quarters would suggest that the expansion rate is falling toward churn rather than staying above it, most likely from competitive displacement or a broad pullback in enterprise technology spending.

Latest reading: Revenue grew 32% in the three months ending in March 2026, re-accelerating above the base-case range. The exit condition is not approached.

The Share Price

At $258, the share price sits well above fair value by the measure applied here. The method converts reported free cash flow into a yield: annual free cash flow divided by market value. At current cash generation of roughly $1 billion per year and a market value of roughly $92 billion, that yield is approximately 0.27% — meaning a buyer at today's price receives about 27 cents per year for every $100 invested in Datadog's earnings power, before any assumption about future growth.

The fair-value anchor is set at a yield of 2.5%, which corresponds to a share price of roughly $28. That anchor is explicitly conservative — it sits between two other valuation methods applied in the underlying analysis (a scenario-weighted cash-flow model produces a central estimate of around $78 per share; a peer-comparable approach produces a range of roughly $46–$235). The trim threshold fires at a yield of 1.5% (roughly $47 per share); the current yield of 0.27% is well below even that level.

The practical implication: the share price does not need to fall to $28 to be interesting — it needs to fall to somewhere in the $100–$200 range for an expected return above the hurdle rate to become available. At $258, none of the three scenarios produces a positive three-year annualised return from the current price to even the most optimistic fair-value estimate.

What's Wobbling

One driver was flagged as mixed rather than confirmed: the cash-generation margin compressed from 32% in the three months ending in March 2025 to 29% in the equivalent period this year. The sources were additional cloud-hosting costs ($41.8 million higher than the prior year) and increased headcount. Neither represents a structural shift — the first reflects seasonal timing of infrastructure provisioning, and the second reflects investment in growth rather than loss of operating leverage. The margin remained above the 25% watch threshold. No exit condition was triggered.

Two informational warnings accompany the valuation calculation. The fair-value anchor of 2.5% yield diverges from the current cash-generation yield of 0.27% by 89%, and from the scenario-model's implied yield of 0.67% by 73%. These are not errors in the framework — they are signals that the fair-value anchor has not been updated to reflect the difference between the free-cash-flow basis and the earnings-after-dilution basis. Both use the same 2.5% threshold but apply it to different numerators ($1 billion of free cash flow produces a price of roughly $120; $250 million of earnings after share-based pay produces a price of roughly $28). This ambiguity does not change the direction of the verdict — the share price is expensive by either basis — but it does mean the specific alert prices differ depending on which numerator is used.

The hyperscaler competitive driver — tracking whether Amazon, Microsoft, and Google's own monitoring tools are gaining share at Datadog's expense — was silent in the latest filing. The filing named cloud-provider tools as a generic competitive risk but provided no data on win/loss rates or customer displacement. Absence of evidence in a competitive context is not reassurance, and this driver warrants active monitoring against the customer-expansion rate trend.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • The AI-native customer cohort — Datadog's fastest-growing segment, contributing roughly seven percentage points of quarterly revenue growth — is the most likely source of near-term disruption. The filing explicitly flags that large customers in this cohort "have rapidly increased their usage of our product and then optimised or may in the future optimise their usage." If AI-workload spend rationalisation becomes widespread across the cohort, the customer-expansion rate could fall faster than the trailing twelve-month figure suggests, because trailing figures smooth recent changes.

  • Amazon's CloudWatch, Microsoft Azure Monitor, and Google Cloud's native monitoring suite are available at effectively zero additional cost to companies already paying for those cloud platforms. For single-cloud deployments, the price-performance case for a third-party monitoring tool narrows materially. Multi-cloud complexity is Datadog's structural defence, but that defence is only as strong as enterprise multi-cloud adoption stays widespread.

  • Share-based pay consumed 22% of revenue in the full year ending December 2025, transforming what looks like $1 billion of free cash flow into $108 million of reported profit. If the compensation ratio does not trend toward industry norms over the next several years, per-share value creation remains diluted even in the bull scenario. A convertible note maturing in 2029 ($1 billion face value, zero coupon) adds a further dilution overhang once its hedge protection runs out.

  • A single acquisition above $2 billion at premium multiples would depart from the disciplined small-deal track record (the largest disclosed acquisition to date was $220 million) and introduce integration risk that the current earnings base cannot easily absorb.

  • Regulatory enforcement related to cross-border data telemetry or AI-model training privacy — a material fine under the EU's data-protection rules or a market-exit requirement under the EU AI Act affecting more than 5% of revenue — is a low-probability but high-impact path to thesis disruption. The filing disclosed no current enforcement actions.

Gaps in the Picture

  • The risk-free rate (the yield on the ten-year German government bond) was not available in the market-data snapshot used for this analysis. The expected-return premium over safe assets could therefore not be computed. The bottom-line assessment uses the thesis-state and price-position dimensions only.

  • The exit-order price has not been resolved. The exit rule in the action plan contains the placeholder {years} — the number of years of base-case compounding the exit price implies — and that figure was not filled in at the time of the last review. The Standing Orders section therefore omits an exit-level price.

  • Two fair-value anchors coexist: the yield-method-derived $28 and the bottom-up $106 (peer and scenario multiples). A future schema migration will resolve the divergence. In practice, the TL;DR prices ($18 buy, $47 trim) reflect the earnings-after-dilution basis, which deducts share-based pay from the earnings numerator; the free-cash-flow basis produces higher anchor prices ($75 buy, $200 trim). A future settings update will confirm which basis the action-plan thresholds intend.

  • Maintenance capital expenditure is not separately disclosed. The cash-flow figures in this analysis treat all capital expenditure as operating (approximately $50 million per year), which slightly understates true free cash flow. At Datadog's revenue scale, the distortion is small but is not modelled precisely.

The Rules

The action plan has six clauses, one per state the evaluation can reach:

  • If the thesis is confirmed intact and the share price is at or above the trim-band edge (as it is today): do not initiate a position. Monitor customer-expansion rate, revenue growth, and AI observability traction quarterly. Revisit entry only if the cash-generation yield rises to the trim-band level of 1.5% (approximately $200 per share on a free-cash-flow basis) with the thesis still intact.

  • If the thesis shows early signs of weakening — one or more drivers declining toward watch thresholds but no exit condition triggered: do not initiate a position. If the customer-expansion rate declines toward 115% or revenue growth falls below 20%, reassess within one earnings cycle. A weakening thesis at a stretched valuation has strictly negative expected return.

  • If the thesis is broken — any written exit condition fires (customer-expansion rate below 112% for two consecutive quarters; gross margin below 77% for two consecutive quarters; share-based pay above 25% of revenue with no credible glide-path; revenue growth below 20% for two consecutive quarters; a single acquisition above $2 billion; a material regulatory enforcement action affecting more than 5% of revenue): do not enter. If already holding, exit the full position within 30 days. No averaging down.

  • If the share price falls below the buy-band edge (cash-generation yield above 4.0%, approximately $75 per share on a free-cash-flow basis): the share price has corrected into the value-investor margin-of-safety zone. Confirm the thesis is intact before acting — customer-expansion rate at or above 115% and cash-generation margin at or above 25% for two consecutive quarters. Initiate in two tranches over one quarter.

  • If the share price is at or above the trim-band edge (cash-generation yield below 1.5%, approximately $200 per share on a free-cash-flow basis): do not initiate. If already holding, trim 25–33% of the position. Do not add.

  • If the share price is at or above the exit-band edge: exit the full position (or remain out). Redeploy proceeds to a holding with a positive expected return. No re-entry within 90 days to remove anchor-price bias. (This clause is active but the specific exit-level price is not yet resolved — see Gaps in the Picture.)

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
