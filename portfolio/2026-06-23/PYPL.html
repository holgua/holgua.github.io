About PayPal

PayPal is a global digital payments network that has operated for 25 years. A consumer stores payment credentials once; merchants accept PayPal because consumers expect it to be there. The network spans 439 million active accounts across roughly 200 countries and processed $1.79 trillion in total payment volume in the year ending December 2025. PayPal earns a small percentage of each transaction — a take-rate — through the classic checkout button, the Venmo peer-to-peer payments product, and a buy-now-pay-later service.

The complication is margin mix: Braintree, PayPal's unbranded payment-processing product (which competes with Stripe and Adyen), is growing faster than the higher-margin branded checkout, compressing the blended take-rate even as total volume rises. Enrique Lores became chief executive in March 2026 — the third in five years — after serving on the PayPal board since 2021. He brings cost-discipline experience from his prior role at HP Inc., but no fintech background.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that PayPal's share price severely undervalues a payments network that can continue generating $5–7 billion a year in cash earnings even without winning any new competitive battles, and that management's commitment to using that cash to buy back shares at distressed prices will compound the value of each remaining share at a double-digit annual rate over a medium-term horizon.

Stated as a set of conditions, the bet holds as long as:

  1. Branded-checkout share loss stays contained. The first exit condition fires if branded-checkout volume falls by more than two percentage points in a single fiscal year, signalling that the market is genuinely bypassing PayPal rather than the pattern being a temporary mix shift.

  2. Overall payment volume keeps growing. Total payment volume (the full value of all transactions passing through PayPal's network) should continue rising: a sustained decline would signal that the network itself is losing relevance, not just that one segment is under pressure.

  3. Active account count stays stable or growing. The 439-million-account base is the foundation of the two-sided network. A sustained decline in active accounts — especially on the consumer side — would directly erode the network-effect argument.

  4. Operating income stays above $5.5 billion. The floor is $5.5 billion in annual operating profit under generally accepted accounting principles (the official profit figure before financing items), or a management forecast below $5.0 billion for the following year. Either would signal that the cost discipline underpinning the thesis has broken.

  5. Buyback discipline is maintained. The per-share compounding story depends on management continuing to retire shares at these prices. The condition breaks if management abandons the programme or takes on debt exceeding 2.0 times earnings before interest, tax, depreciation, and amortisation — without a compelling strategic reason.

  6. No consent order restricts core products. A regulatory enforcement action — from the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau or the Department of Justice — that materially restricts PayPal's core product features would directly impair the earnings base.

  7. Consumer credit charge-offs stay below 10%. PayPal runs a consumer lending book (buy-now-pay-later and consumer credit). If net charge-offs (the proportion of loans written off as unrecoverable) rise above 10% without a disclosed recovery path, the credit book becomes a drain on the thesis.

The Rules

The pre-committed action plan translates the above into six branches:

  • Thesis holds, price inside band: hold. Continue compounding.
  • Driver weakens, no exit condition fires: do not add. Check the specific driver. Decide after two consecutive confirming quarters — one quarter of softness is not enough.
  • Exit condition fires: exit within 30 days. No averaging down.
  • Price falls below $41: confirm thesis intact, then add in tranches over one to two quarters.
  • Price rises above $60: trim 25–33%. Retain the core holding.
  • Price rises above $78: exit the full position. Redeploy; no re-entry for 90 days.

The Conditions Today

Branded-checkout share loss (Condition 1). The weakest read. Branded-checkout volume is not separately disclosed in the filing for the three months ending March 2026. A securities class action filed in February 2026, Darcy v. PayPal, alleges management withheld information about impediments to the checkout strategy. No exit condition has fired — the allegation is at pleadings stage — but the combination of non-disclosure and pending litigation makes this the item to watch most closely.

Overall payment volume (Condition 2). Confirms. Total payment volume rose 11% in the three months ending March 2026, with 6.5 billion transactions processed. The network is growing.

Active accounts (Condition 3). Mixed. Active accounts grew 1% year-on-year to 439 million. Growth has plateaued, and transactions per account have declined modestly, but the account base is not shrinking.

Operating income (Condition 4). Mixed but above the floor. Full-year 2025 operating profit was $6.065 billion — above the $5.5 billion exit threshold. The three months ending March 2026 showed a 2-percentage-point margin contraction versus the prior year (Braintree mix), but the annualised quarterly run-rate implies roughly $5.95 billion for 2026 — still above the floor.

Buyback discipline (Condition 5). Confirms strongly. PayPal repurchased 34 million shares for $1.5 billion in the three months ending March 2026, with $12.4 billion of authorisation remaining. At this pace, shares outstanding could fall from 882 million to roughly 759 million by 2028.

Regulatory status (Condition 6). Mixed. Two Consumer Financial Protection Bureau civil investigative demands are open (covering Venmo and PayPal Credit). Neither has converted to an enforcement action — the specific exit trigger — and a prior shareholder class action was dismissed with prejudice.

Consumer credit charge-offs (Condition 7). Confirms. The net charge-off rate improved to 3.0% annualised in the three months ending March 2026, down from 4.5% a year earlier. Well below the 10% exit threshold.

The Share Price

At $42 today, the share price sits just above the buy edge of $41.

The fair-value anchor — the price implied by treating the current level of owner earnings (what a business produces after maintaining its assets) as a permanent, zero-growth feature of the business — is $48. At $42, that puts the share price about 12% below fair value, or modestly below fair value. The earnings yield (the ratio of what the business earns to what the market charges for it) is approximately 12%, against an anchored fair-value yield of 10.5% — a gap that places the price at the edge of the holding band rather than inside the buying zone.

What the market appears to be pricing in is more extreme than that gap implies. Reverse calculations from the current price suggest the embedded assumption is that PayPal's earnings will permanently fall by roughly 45% from today's level and never recover — a scenario not supported by 2025 results, which showed $6.065 billion in operating profit on a rising trajectory.

What's Wobbling

Two items carry amber flags without crossing a hard exit line.

Branded-checkout disclosure gap. The inability to confirm — or refute — branded-checkout volume data for the three months ending March 2026 is the most significant wobble. The Darcy securities class action alleges that management knew of impediments to the checkout strategy and failed to disclose them. If the allegation proves well-founded at a later stage of litigation, the first exit condition (branded-checkout share loss exceeding two percentage points in a single year) becomes material rather than theoretical. The watch period is the next quarterly report: if a second consecutive quarter passes without explicit branded-checkout data, the monitoring cadence should increase.

GAAP operating margin compression. The 2-percentage-point margin contraction in the three months ending March 2026 versus the same period in 2025 is the product of Braintree mix — a structural dynamic that is unlikely to reverse quickly. The full-year floor holds, but the quarterly trend in the wrong direction deserves attention. If the next two quarterly results also show year-on-year contraction, the annualised figure will approach the $5.5 billion exit floor.

Neither wobble has crossed a line. Both warrant monitoring at the next quarterly disclosure.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Branded checkout is structurally displaced. If Apple Pay and artificial-intelligence-agent platforms establish a routing preference that bypasses the PayPal checkout button, take-rate erosion becomes permanent. Medium-probability, high-impact.

  • Management transition causes strategic drift. Three chief executives in five years is a governance weakness. A large acquisition under Lores would redirect the $12.4 billion buyback authorisation toward an asset at a premium, collapsing the per-share compounding thesis.

  • Regulatory enforcement returns. A change of administration could convert the two open Consumer Financial Protection Bureau civil investigative demands into formal enforcement actions restricting the buy-now-pay-later product.

  • Agentic-commerce protocol fragmentation. AI-agent platforms may standardise on payment rails that bypass PayPal's checkout entirely. PayPal's integrations with major AI platforms are defensive positioning; the protocol competition is unresolved.

  • Consumer credit cycle turns. The charge-off improvement to 3.0% looks solid today. A consumer-led recession that lifted charge-offs toward 8–10% would compress margins and approach the exit threshold.

Gaps in the Picture

Branded-checkout volume is undisclosed. The most important driver condition cannot be measured from the quarterly filing for the three months ending March 2026. The Darcy class action (at pleadings stage) adds a legal dimension to the gap. The hold verdicts in §3 for conditions involving this driver rest on the absence of a confirmed breach, not on positive evidence.

Two fair-value anchors coexist. The owner-earnings yield method produces a fair value of $48 — what the business is worth at today's earnings level with no assumed growth. A separate scenario analysis using historical price multiples over the prior four-and-a-half years produces a base-case midpoint of $142, assuming the market eventually re-rates toward its historical average. The $48 governs the standing orders. A future review should settle which anchor takes precedence; the two numbers reflect fundamentally different questions about how the stock should be valued.
