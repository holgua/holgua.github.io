About Bund Linker 2033

The Bund Linker 2033 is a German government bond issued in February 2021 and maturing on 2033-04-15. It was issued by the Federal Republic of Germany — rated AAA by all three major credit agencies — with an original issuance volume of €10.65 billion. The stated coupon is 0.10% per year, but both the coupon payments and the principal at maturity are linked to the German Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices excluding tobacco (a price index maintained by Eurostat and used by the European Central Bank as the standard euro-area inflation gauge). When prices rise, the nominal amounts paid on the bond rise with them, preserving the real purchasing power of the holding. That is the bond's only stated purpose: inflation protection at effectively zero credit risk.

Settlement mechanics reflect this linkage. A buyer today pays the dirty price of €119.67 per €100 of original face value at settlement, not the quoted clean price of €94.63. The clean price of €94.63 is expressed in real terms — the same purchasing-power units as the 2021 issuance date. To get the actual euros that change hands, the clean price is multiplied by the indexation coefficient — currently 1.26416, meaning the principal has grown by about 26.4% in nominal terms since issuance — and accrued interest is then added.

The Case for the Bet

The bet on this bond is structural: it provides euro-denominated inflation protection at effectively zero credit risk, provided three conditions continue to hold:

  1. Real yield at current market price remains above the exit floor. The bond only makes economic sense as a holding if its real yield — the return above inflation built into its price — stays positive enough that EUR cash or a nominal German government bond is not a better alternative. A real yield persistently below −1.0% while EUR cash simultaneously offers a superior real return triggers the compound exit.

What's Wobbling

The one item worth watching is the trajectory of the ECB deposit rate. At 2.25%, EUR cash earns a real yield of approximately 0.23% — giving the linker a 70 basis-point advantage. A sharp ECB rate cut would reduce the cash real yield, widening the linker's advantage. A re-acceleration of inflation, on the other hand, would push break-even inflation higher, narrowing the gap between the linker yield and the cash yield; in an extreme case where break-even inflation exceeded the linker's real yield by more than 1 percentage point, the cash comparison leg of exit condition (1) from the Case for the Bet could begin to apply. Neither scenario is current.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Sustained real yield collapse into exit territory. If the bond's real yield falls below −1.0% and remains there for a full quarter while EUR cash simultaneously offers a better real return, the compound exit fires. This would require a combination of price appreciation — driving the clean price above €108 — and a cash real yield that turns positive enough to exceed the linker's real return.
  • German sovereign credit deterioration. A downgrade below AA− by any two major rating agencies would trigger the credit-exit condition regardless of where the real yield sits. Germany has maintained the highest sovereign credit rating continuously since the post-war era; the event is low-probability but carries full position risk.
  • HICP index methodology change. Eurostat retains the right to alter the methodology of the HICP ex-tobacco index. A substitution of the reference basket or a change in the reference population would sever the inflation-protection link. This is a regulatory and political risk rather than a market risk; it is low in probability but would render the inflation-hedge rationale invalid if it fired.
  • Liquidity under stress. The bond trades in the over-the-counter market for German government securities. In a severe market dislocation, bid-offer spreads can widen and execution at quoted prices may be constrained — particularly if an exit condition fires during a period of elevated volatility, when liquidity is typically thinnest.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
