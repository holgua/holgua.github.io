Recent Development

The FY2026 results — for the year ended 30 April 2026, reported in the 10-K filed 2026-06-08 — confirmed the Elasticsearch platform thesis intact across every driver that can be tracked from a public filing. Net expansion rate held at 112%, meaning the existing customer base is spending 12% more on Elastic products than it was a year ago; the exit floor for that number is 105%. Cloud revenue — the software-as-a-service portion of the business — grew 22% year-on-year to $837 million, reaching 48% of total revenue; the exit floor for annual cloud growth is 10%. The cash generation figure that anchors the valuation rose 51% to $321.8 million, lifting the cash margin to 18.5% from 14.3% a year earlier.

The profitability trajectory also moved in the right direction. The operating loss narrowed by 39% in absolute terms to roughly $33 million, bringing the operating margin to −1.9% from −3.7% in FY2025. At that rate of improvement, the FY2028 target of a positive operating margin is within reach — and exit condition 6 remains live but unbroken. Stock-based compensation fell slightly as a share of revenue to 17.2%, the second consecutive year below the 18% exit threshold; 84 basis points of room sounds thin but has not been breached.

Two small acquisitions — an artificial-intelligence embedding specialist for $43 million and a Kubernetes cost-management tool for $11 million — both came in well below the $500 million exit threshold and required no special capital-accretion disclosure. The competitive picture, covered in external research rather than in the 10-K itself, shows Datadog growing approximately 11 percentage points faster than Elastic on a year-on-year basis, still below the 15-point gap that would start the two-year clock on exit condition 7. All of that is detailed further in the Case for the Bet and the Share Price sections.

About Elastic

Elastic N.V. sells enterprise software built on the Elasticsearch technology it created and open-sourced in 2012. The core product is a search and data-analytics engine — a layer that sits between an organisation's raw data stores and the applications or analysts that need to query them quickly. From that foundation Elastic has expanded into three connected markets: enterprise search and artificial-intelligence retrieval (helping companies build internal search tools and AI assistants), observability (monitoring the performance and health of software infrastructure), and security (ingesting and correlating security events at scale, a category often called SIEM — Security Information and Event Management). Customers use all three on the same Elastic platform and the same underlying data, which is the product's central pitch: one data layer, one operations team, three use cases.

Revenue arrives in two forms. Subscription revenue — about 94% of the total — comes from companies paying for either a hosted, Elastic-managed cloud service (Elastic Cloud) or a self-managed licence for software they run on their own infrastructure. The remaining 6% is professional-services revenue from implementation and support contracts. Elastic Cloud, the company's own managed service running on the major cloud providers' infrastructure, has been growing faster than the self-managed business and now accounts for 48% of total revenue; management has explicitly guided that cloud will continue to grow as a share. The company generated $1.74 billion in total revenue in FY2026 and is headquartered in San Francisco, incorporated in the Netherlands, and listed on the New York Stock Exchange.

The Case for the Bet

The core argument is that Elasticsearch's production-embedded pipelines create genuine switching costs: once a company's security-alert rules, performance dashboards, and search interfaces are built on Elastic, replacing that infrastructure requires rebuilding everything from scratch — a project large enough that most organisations avoid it unless the platform materially fails them. The 112% net expansion rate is the primary empirical test of that claim; customers are spending more, not less, which is what a switching-cost business looks like at the customer level. The cloud pivot adds a second argument: as customers move workloads to managed infrastructure, the economics of the relationship improve (higher gross margin, lower customer burden) and the switching cost, if anything, deepens.

The seven exit conditions below translate those arguments into measurable tests:

  1. Net expansion rate above 105% for each measured quarter. The rate at which existing customers expand their spending is the most direct measure of whether the switching-cost moat is real. A reading below 105% for two consecutive quarters would signal that customers are contracting or churning faster than new usage is being added — the earliest observable signal of moat erosion.

Latest reading: approximately 112% as of 30 April 2026. Customers spending more than $100,000 annually grew 14% year-on-year to over 1,720; customers spending more than $1 million annually stood at over 240. 700 basis points of headroom above the exit floor.

  1. Elastic Cloud annual revenue growth above 10% for each fiscal year. The managed-cloud product is the business's growth engine and the mechanism by which switching costs deepen over time. A deceleration to single-digit growth for a full fiscal year would confirm that the AWS OpenSearch fork — a competing, open-source, AWS-backed alternative — is successfully commoditising the self-managed renewal base and stalling the cloud transition.

Latest reading: total Elastic Cloud grew 22% year-on-year to $837.3 million in FY2026; the annual Elastic Cloud contract segment specifically grew 28%. Cloud reached 48% of total revenue, up from 46% a year earlier. The exit floor of 10% has 12 percentage points of current headroom. Forward contracted revenue (calculated remaining performance obligations) grew 20.4% to approximately $1.2 billion, providing a pipeline signal that is consistent with continued growth above the exit floor.

  1. Cash flow margin above 14% on a full-year view. Cash generation — operating cash flow minus capital expenditure, computed on the raw basis before stock-based compensation deduction — is the valuation anchor. A sustained compression below 14% would indicate that revenue growth is consuming cash rather than generating it, invalidating the platform's economics.

Latest reading: operating cash flow $326.9 million minus capital expenditure $5.1 million equals raw cash flow of $321.8 million, representing an 18.5% margin on $1.74 billion of revenue. 450 basis points above the exit floor. A year earlier the margin was 14.3%, so the improvement is real, not flat.

  1. Stock-based compensation below 18% of revenue for each of two consecutive fiscal years. High stock-based compensation dilutes existing shareholders and signals that a company is paying its employees in equity because it cannot afford to pay them in cash — a concern for a business still running at a GAAP operating loss. Two consecutive years above 18% would indicate that compensation discipline has reversed.

Latest reading: stock-based compensation was $298.4 million in FY2026, representing 17.2% of revenue — the second consecutive year below the 18% threshold. The buffer is thin at 84 basis points. In absolute terms the stock-based compensation figure grew 16% year-on-year, but the revenue denominator grew 17%, so the ratio dipped slightly. A material slowdown in revenue growth without a corresponding reduction in stock awards would close the gap quickly.

  1. No acquisition exceeding $500 million at a revenue multiple above 8× without disclosed expected cash-flow accretion within three years. Large acquisitions at high revenue multiples are the most common way a software company destroys value while growing revenue. The $500 million threshold and the 8× multiple cap make the test observable directly from public filings.

Latest reading: Elastic completed two acquisitions in FY2026 — Conic AI Technology (trading as Jina AI, an artificial-intelligence embedding and reranking specialist) for $43.4 million in October 2025 and Paladin Data (trading as Keep, a Kubernetes cost-management tool) for $10.9 million in May 2025. Combined consideration of $54.3 million is well below the $500 million floor. Exit condition 5 is clear.

  1. GAAP operating margin positive by FY2028. Elastic has been unprofitable on a GAAP basis since listing. The trajectory toward profitability is the thesis for margin expansion; if the business has not crossed into positive GAAP operating margin by FY2028, the profitability convergence argument has failed on the timeline expected.

Latest reading: GAAP operating loss of $33.5 million in FY2026, representing a −1.9% margin. A year earlier the loss was $54.9 million at −3.7%. The 180-basis-point improvement over one year, if sustained, reaches positive territory by FY2028. The condition is active and on track, not yet triggered.

  1. Datadog revenue growth exceeding Elastic's by no more than 15 percentage points for two consecutive fiscal years, AND Elastic retaining Gartner Observability Magic Quadrant Leader status. This condition requires both a sustained competitive growth gap and a Gartner downgrade before the exit fires. The Datadog gap is the leading signal; Gartner is the confirming signal.

Latest reading: external competitive research places the Datadog-to-Elastic revenue growth gap at approximately 11 percentage points, below the 15-point trigger. Elastic confirmed Gartner Observability Leader status in FY2026. Neither condition of exit condition 7 is triggered. The 10-K itself provides no comparative growth data; this reading draws on external competitive analysis rather than the filing.

The Share Price

The valuation method used here converts cash flow into a yield: divide the annual cash flow by the market capitalisation to get a percentage, in the same way a bond's interest payment divided by its price gives a yield. The higher the yield, the cheaper the stock. At $61.86, Elastic's cash flow yield is approximately 5.0% — above the 3.04% level that anchors fair value (the yield implied by the base-case discounted cash-flow intrinsic of roughly $101 per share) and approaching but not yet at the 5.5% buy threshold (implied price approximately $56). In other words, the share price is below fair value but not yet at the level where the Rules call for adding to the position.

The valuation-band position is within the hold band, closer to the buy edge than the trim edge. A price decline to around $56 would push the yield above 5.5% and arm the buy order — that much is straightforward. The more interesting question is why the share price is trading at 39% below the base-case intrinsic when every measurable exit condition is clear, and that question is addressed in the section that follows.

Why the Market Sees It Differently

The price says the business will earn less over time. Our view says the opposite.

The gap is 38.8%: the base-case intrinsic estimate is $101, and the share price is $62. That is not a small rounding difference — it implies the market is pricing in a long-run cash-flow growth rate of approximately 6.4% a year, against the base-case assumption of 15%. Three explanations are consistent with this divergence.

The first is that the bear scenario is dragging the probability-weighted price below the base case. The bear case — priced at $29 per share with a 20% assigned probability — implies a 49% permanent loss from today's price. When a bear case that destructive carries a non-trivial probability, the market's arithmetic, even with a 55% base-case weight, will price the shares below the base intrinsic. The FY2026 10-K results are consistent with the base case and inconsistent with the bear case: 22% cloud revenue growth, 112% net expansion, and an 18.5% cash margin do not look like a business heading to $29 per share. But the risk is not zero, and the market is pricing it.

The second explanation is that the AWS OpenSearch fork is being priced as a larger threat than the current evidence supports. OpenSearch is now under Linux Foundation governance with over 1,400 contributors; if the market believes that free, cloud-native competition will gradually commoditise the self-managed customer base, a 6.4% implied growth rate is not irrational — it is essentially pricing in a world where Elastic Cloud grows slowly and the self-managed book erodes. The 22% cloud growth and the 20.4% increase in contracted forward revenue argue against this reading, but it is a real risk, not a straw man.

The third is that broad-market multiple compression on software stocks — driven by higher interest rates and rotation away from high-growth names — has pushed Elastic's share price down more than its fundamentals warrant. This is the most generous reading of the gap: the company is executing, the market is simply applying a lower multiple, and the gap will close as rates normalise and growth investors return to the sector.

What would have to happen for the market's 6.4% long-run growth view to prove correct? Sustained deceleration in Elastic Cloud below 15%, two or more consecutive quarters of net expansion rate below 108%, and evidence that the FedRAMP federal revenue ramp is failing to materialise. Not one of those signals appears in the FY2026 data; all of them are the conditions that would need to emerge for the bear scenario to prevail.

What's Wobbling

Nothing in the FY2026 10-K has crossed a threshold or broken a condition. The observations below are watch items — things that are moving in a direction that warrants monitoring but have not triggered any rule.

SBC buffer thinning. Stock-based compensation as a share of revenue fell to 17.2% in FY2026, the second consecutive year below the 18% exit threshold. That is the good news. The less comfortable observation is that the absolute dollar amount of stock-based compensation grew 16% year-on-year to $298 million — almost exactly in line with revenue growth of 17%. The ratio is falling only because the denominator is growing faster than the numerator. If revenue growth were to slow to, say, 12% while stock awards continued at current levels, the ratio would start climbing back toward 18%. At 84 basis points of headroom, the buffer deserves attention at each annual filing.

Competitive growth gap with Datadog. Datadog's observability business is growing approximately 11 percentage points faster than Elastic's total revenue on an annualised basis. Exit condition 7 requires that gap to exceed 15 percentage points for two consecutive fiscal years before any action is triggered — and the Gartner Leader downgrade would also need to occur simultaneously. So the condition is not at risk of triggering in the near term. But the direction of travel — a gap that has been widening rather than narrowing — is worth tracking. A second consecutive year of 11-point divergence would not fire the exit, but a year at 16 points would start the clock.

GAAP profitability timeline. The operating loss narrowed materially in FY2026, but the company is still loss-making on a GAAP basis. Exit condition 6 sets a 2028 deadline. At the current trajectory of approximately 180 basis points of improvement per year, the business arrives at breakeven in FY2028 — on track but with little margin for error. A single year of margin deterioration would push the timeline past 2028 and trigger the exit condition.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • AWS OpenSearch commoditises the self-managed base. OpenSearch is free, built on the same codebase, and is now a Linux Foundation project with growing industry support. If enterprise customers conclude that OpenSearch is "good enough" for their non-cloud workloads, the self-managed subscription renewal base could erode faster than Elastic Cloud can replace it — shrinking total revenue and compressing margins simultaneously.
  • Datadog continues to widen its observability lead. Datadog has more salespeople, a larger installed base, and a broader monitoring platform. If the competitive growth gap widens from the current 11 points to more than 15 points for two consecutive years — and Gartner's assessors reach the same conclusion — the competitive-displacement exit condition fires.
  • Revenue slowdown exposes SBC fragility. If total revenue growth decelerates below 14% while stock-based compensation continues to grow, the SBC percentage crosses 18% and the clock starts on exit condition 4. A sharp slowdown to single-digit revenue growth could trigger both the SBC exit condition and the cloud-growth exit condition simultaneously.
  • Capital allocation deteriorates further. The $340 million buyback executed at an average of $76.91 — above today's share price — was a capital-allocation mistake that the remaining $160 million authorisation could either compound or correct. A second large buyback at prices above the base-case intrinsic, or a large acquisition at a high revenue multiple without cash-flow accretion disclosure, would trigger exit condition 5.
  • FedRAMP ramp fails to materialise. The FedRAMP High authorisation, obtained in mid-2026, opens the federal market. If the CISA SIEMaaS contract and GSA OneGov adoption generate less revenue than anticipated, the bull case narrative loses its most distinctive catalyst, and the stock is left defending the base case with a competitive headwind in observability.

The Rules

If the thesis remains intact and no exit condition has been triggered and the share price is in the hold band, hold the position and do not add. Monitor net expansion rate and Elastic Cloud growth at each quarterly filing; confirm stock-based compensation at each annual filing.

If any thesis driver shows sustained deterioration — net expansion rate trending toward 107% or Elastic Cloud growth decelerating below 15% for two consecutive quarters — do not add to the position. Require two consecutive quarters of worsening before considering a reduction; one quarter is not sufficient evidence of a structural break.

If any of the seven written exit conditions fires, exit the full position within 30 days. No averaging down on a thesis break. Exit conditions are: net expansion rate below 105% for two consecutive quarters; Elastic Cloud growth below 10% for a full fiscal year; cash flow margin below 14% on a full-year view; stock-based compensation above 18% of revenue for two consecutive fiscal years; an acquisition exceeding $500 million at more than 8 times trailing revenue without a three-year cash-flow accretion disclosure; GAAP operating margin not positive by FY2028; or Datadog's revenue growth exceeding Elastic's by more than 15 percentage points for two consecutive fiscal years while Elastic's Gartner Observability position falls below Leader.

If the share price falls to around $56 — where the cash flow yield rises above 5.5% — and the thesis is intact, add to the position in tranches. No more than half the target size in a single fill; deploy the remainder only after confirming at the next quarterly filing that conditions have not deteriorated.

If the share price rises to around $140 — where the cash flow yield falls below 2.2% — trim 25% of the position to harvest the premium above fair value. Keep the core holding for continued compounding if the base case plays out.

If the share price rises to approximately $200 — where the cash flow yield falls below 1.5%, putting the market at roughly the bull-case intrinsic value — exit the entire remaining position. At that reading the forward return has inverted and the asymmetry no longer justifies continuing to hold. No re-entry for at least 90 days following an exit at this level.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
