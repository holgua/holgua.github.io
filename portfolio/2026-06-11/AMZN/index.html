Amazon: The Bet Still Works

The Bottom Line

The central bet on Amazon — that its cloud infrastructure business keeps compounding at well above 15% revenue growth while maintaining operating margins above 30% — is working. As of the three months ending in March 2026, the cloud unit grew at 28% year-on-year and printed an operating margin of nearly 38%, both comfortably above the lines at which the bet would be called off. The share price at around $250 sits above the point that the rules define as fair value ($206), so no bargain-priced entry exists today. The one condition worth watching is the pace of capital investment, which is running at a rate that would breach a written exit rule — but only if it continues into 2027 and 2028 without the cloud revenue growth that is currently accompanying it.

The Verdict

The evidence from Amazon's latest quarterly filing confirms the thesis. Every growth driver is performing above its minimum threshold; no exit condition has been triggered.

As a fresh investment at today's price (around $250 per share). The share price is above the fair-value anchor of $206 and well above the level at which the rules call for adding — $165. Patience is the indicated posture: set a price alert at $206 (fair value) and a second at $165 (the cheap edge where the buying rules engage), then confirm the conditions are still working before acting on either.

As an existing investment bought below $250 per share — currently sitting at break-even or better. The thesis is intact and the share price has not crossed any trim or exit line. The rules say to hold and let the compounding continue.

As an existing investment bought roughly between $250 and $300 per share — currently showing a loss of up to about 17%. The thesis is intact. A short-term mark-to-market loss on an intact thesis is not a signal to act; the rules say to hold without averaging down.

As an existing investment bought between $300 and $350 per share — currently showing a loss of between about 17% and 29%. Same thesis state, same rule: hold. The bet has not weakened, and no exit condition has fired. The rules reserve an exit order for specific broken-thesis events, none of which has occurred.

As an existing investment bought above $350 per share — currently showing a loss of roughly 29% or more. This is the most uncomfortable position, but the governing logic is unchanged: the thesis is intact, the cloud division is accelerating, and no written exit condition has been crossed. The rules say to hold — not to realise a loss while the underlying business is performing well. The one exception would be a personal liquidity need unrelated to this analysis.

About Amazon

Amazon makes most of its profit from two businesses that most people associate with a third. The business most people know — the online shop where goods arrive the next day — is Amazon's retail and logistics operation. In the twelve months to the end of March 2026, this generated roughly $742 billion in revenue across its North American and international arms. But profit in that segment is thin: the North American stores operation earned about seven cents of operating profit on every dollar of sales in 2025, and the international operation only turned profitable in 2024.

The genuine profit engine is Amazon Web Services — a business that rents computing power and software tools to other companies and governments via the internet. The cloud division generated $129 billion in revenue in 2025 with an operating margin above 35%, and contributed more than half of Amazon's total operating profit despite being only about 18% of its revenue. Next to it sits a third engine: advertising. When companies pay to have their products appear prominently in Amazon search results, that revenue — $68.6 billion in 2025, growing at roughly 22% per year — drops into Amazon's accounts at very high margins. These two high-margin businesses fund, and in turn benefit from, the vast logistics network that underpins the retail side. The whole structure is designed so that each part reinforces the others: a larger logistics network brings more customers to the shop, more customers attract more third-party sellers, more sellers generate more advertising revenue, and more advertising revenue funds further infrastructure.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that Amazon's cloud and advertising businesses are still early in their compounding cycle — that the investment being made today into computing infrastructure and product breadth will compound into a much larger profit pool over five or more years, and that the current share price does not fully reflect that outcome.

For the bet to hold, five conditions must remain in place:

  1. Cloud revenue growth stays above 15% for any given two-quarter window. The specific threshold is not a sustained average; it is two consecutive quarters below 15% as the warning that infrastructure investment is not being absorbed by commercial demand. The current rate is 28%.

  2. Cloud operating margin stays above 28%. This is the floor below which the economics of the cloud business would suggest that the reinvestment is not earning an adequate return. The current margin is approximately 38%.

  3. Advertising revenue growth stays in double digits. The exit condition is single-digit growth for two full years — a signal that the third profit engine has reached saturation. At 24% growth in the most recent quarter, this condition is satisfied by a wide margin.

  4. The international stores operation continues to extend its profitability. The thesis treats the international business's recent turn to profit as a supporting compounding runway — operating income compounding faster than revenue for several more years. The current trajectory supports that.

  5. Capital expenditure normalises relative to cloud revenue growth. Amazon spent $128 billion on infrastructure in 2025. The exit condition is not the scale of spending itself, but spending above $120 billion in both 2027 and 2028 without the cloud revenue acceleration that would validate it.

The Rules

The rules governing what happens under each combination of thesis state and share price are:

  • If the thesis is confirmed (as it is today): hold the position and continue compounding.
  • If the thesis weakens (one driver deteriorating but no exit condition crossed): do not add. Watch the next two quarterly filings before deciding — one quarter of deteriorating evidence is not enough to act.
  • If the thesis breaks (a written exit condition is crossed): exit the full position within thirty days. No averaging down. Cloud revenue and margin exits require two consecutive quarters of evidence before action; a court-ordered business break-up or unexpected auditor change triggers an immediate exit.
  • If the share price falls to or below the buy edge of $165: confirm the thesis is still intact and no exit condition has been triggered. If confirmed, add to the position in tranches over one to two quarters.
  • If the share price rises to or above the trim edge of $309: trim 25–33% of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding. Re-evaluate position size after the trim.
  • If the share price rises to or above the exit edge of $494: exit the full position. At that price the market is pricing in roughly eleven years of base-case compounding in a single moment; the implied forward return falls below the long-run equity premium, and reallocation makes more sense than continuing to hold. No re-entry within ninety days.

The Conditions Today

Cloud revenue growth. In the three months ending in March 2026, Amazon's cloud division reported $37.6 billion in sales — 28% higher than the same period a year earlier. The stated minimum threshold is 15% growth for two consecutive quarters before the first exit condition would be triggered; the current reading is 13 percentage points above that floor. The passage of most relevance from Amazon's latest quarterly report: "AWS sales increased 28% in Q1 2026 compared to the comparable prior year period." Condition met by a wide margin.

Cloud operating margin. The cloud division generated $14.2 billion in operating profit on $37.6 billion in sales in the three months ending in March 2026, a margin of approximately 37.7%. That is modestly below the 39.5% recorded in the same period a year earlier — the additional depreciation from infrastructure build is landing on the margin line — but it remains nearly ten percentage points above the 28% exit floor. Condition met.

Advertising growth. Amazon's advertising revenue — largely payments by sellers and brands to feature their products prominently in search results and across Amazon's properties — grew 24% year-on-year to $17.2 billion in the three months ending in March 2026. The exit condition is single-digit growth sustained for two full years; the current reading is well into the double digits. Condition met.

International profitability. Amazon's international stores operation earned $1.4 billion on $39.8 billion in revenue in the three months ending in March 2026, an operating margin of roughly 3.6%, up from 3.0% a year earlier on 19% revenue growth. The thesis's claim that this business would extend its profitability trajectory is holding. Condition met.

Capital expenditure trajectory. This is the condition to watch. Amazon spent $43.2 billion on infrastructure in the three months ending in March 2026, up from $24.3 billion in the same period a year earlier. On an annualised basis, that pace exceeds the $120 billion threshold mentioned in the exit conditions — but the written rule requires that pace without the commensurate cloud revenue acceleration that would justify it. Since cloud revenue grew 28% in the same quarter, the exit logic is not triggered today. The condition reads as mixed, not broken.

The Share Price

At $250, the share price sits above the rules' fair-value anchor. The valuation is computed using what might be called the cash profit yield — the ratio of Amazon's cash profits (operating cash flow minus the capital expenditure figure in Amazon's formal accounts) to its total market value. At the current price, that yield is approximately 4.9%, which sits between the fair-value anchor of 6.0% (corresponding to a share price of $206) and the trim trigger of 4.0% (corresponding to a share price of $309).

In plain terms: the share price is in the neutral zone where the rules call for patience rather than action in either direction. It is not a bargain, but it is not in the range that would trigger a sale either.

One important caveat on the numbers. The cash profit figure in the calculation uses a narrow accounting definition of capital expenditure — $6.7 billion in 2025. Amazon's actual cash spending on property and equipment in 2025 was $128 billion, disclosed in the annual report but captured in a different line of the accounts. The yield of 4.9% would fall substantially under a full cash-spending view. This gap is documented and acknowledged; readers should treat the yield figure as a relative positioning measure within the rules framework rather than a precise absolute yield.

The three order levels and the five reader-situation verdicts above are consistent: every cost-basis row currently reads as hold because the share price is within the neutral band and the thesis is intact. The standing orders describe what the rules will execute if the share price moves to those boundary lines.

What's Wobbling

Two of the eight thesis drivers are returning a mixed reading rather than a clear positive.

Capital expenditure pace. As described in The Conditions Today, Amazon's infrastructure spending is running at a pace that would annualise to over $170 billion — well above the $120 billion threshold cited in the exit conditions. The governing rule requires that level of spending and the absence of commensurate cloud revenue acceleration; and AWS is currently delivering 28% growth. So the exit condition is not triggered. But if cloud revenue growth were to slow in the next two quarterly filings while spending remained elevated, the arithmetic of that exit condition would shift materially. That combination — not the spending level alone — is the single most important thing to watch.

Capital deployment discipline. Amazon made no share repurchases in the three months ending in March 2026 despite having $6.1 billion of authorised buyback capacity unused. Instead, the company invested $15 billion in OpenAI preferred shares during the quarter, entered a commitment for a further $35 billion in the same vehicle, committed $5 billion to the AI firm Anthropic and arranged a $20 billion financing facility for it, and agreed to a $10.9 billion merger with Globalstar. The original thesis acknowledged that Amazon has not used buybacks as its primary capital tool and treated reinvestment into above-cost-of-capital opportunities as acceptable behaviour. The aggregate scale of the AI-partner equity commitments — more than $70 billion in a short period — is at the outer edge of what the thesis treats as within tolerance; a second consecutive quarter at similar scale would be worth examining closely.

Neither wobble crosses a written exit condition as of the three months ending in March 2026.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • The cloud business loses ground in the AI era. Microsoft and Google are both investing heavily in comparable cloud infrastructure, backed by their own large-language-model partnerships. Amazon's own annual report notes that artificial intelligence "facilitates competitive entry." A sustained shift of AI workloads away from Amazon's cloud towards competitors would compress both the revenue growth rate and the margin, and the central thesis would weaken.

  • The FTC case produces a structural break-up. The US Federal Trade Commission's monopoly trial — scheduled for the second half of 2026 and focused on Amazon's retail marketplace practices — could in a worst case result in a court order requiring structural separation of parts of the business. This is a low-probability outcome in the near term but would be severe and immediate in its effect; the rules treat it as an immediate exit trigger rather than a two-quarter confirmation event.

  • The infrastructure investment does not convert. The $128 billion capital expenditure in 2025 — the largest single-year infrastructure build in Amazon's history — rests on the assumption that AI-related demand will absorb the capacity being built. If demand growth slows before the build is complete, the company faces either asset impairments or a sustained period of below-cost-of-capital returns on new investments. The value analysis's bear-case scenario — which carries a 25% probability weighting — implies an annualised loss on the investment of roughly 15% from the current share price over three years.

  • Tariffs and cross-border competition pressure the retail business. Amazon's own filings identify Chinese-origin sellers as a material portion of its third-party marketplace revenue and flag tariff policy changes as a forward-looking risk. A significant disruption to cross-border e-commerce economics could compress margins in the retail segment further, while also reducing the advertising revenue that depends on those sellers' presence on the platform.

  • Political alignment proves reversible. The current alignment between Amazon's interests and the US federal government's priorities — visible in cloud contracts and AI policy — is not guaranteed to persist through a change of administration. The cloud division's embedded federal contracts provide insulation, but the retail and regulatory picture remains sensitive to the political cycle.

Gaps in the Picture

Capital expenditure definition. The cash profit yield used in The Share Price section is based on the capital expenditure figure in Amazon's formal accounts, which was $6.7 billion in 2025. Amazon's actual cash spending on property and equipment in 2025 was $128 billion — disclosed in the annual report but captured under a different accounting classification. The yield figure of approximately 4.9% reflects the accounting-based number, not the total cash outflow. Readers should treat the yield as a framework positioning measure rather than a precise cash return figure, and cross-reference the cash flow statement when near a band edge.

Maintenance versus growth capital. The financial statements do not separate capital spending into the portion required to maintain the existing business and the portion directed at growth. The yield calculation treats the accounting-line capital expenditure as maintenance, which is the standard convention when no split is disclosed. A more conservative treatment — counting a larger share of the $128 billion total as maintenance — would reduce the apparent cash profit yield significantly and could shift the valuation position within the band.

Single-quarter readings. The cloud margin figure of 37.7% and the international margin of 3.6% each represent a single quarter's reading. The written exit conditions require two consecutive quarters of deterioration before any action is warranted; one quarter of data confirms a direction but does not establish a trend.
