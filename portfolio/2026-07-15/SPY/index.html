The S&P 500 basket now earns less — in pure yield terms — than a ten-year US Treasury bond, which means anyone holding this fund is accepting a lower raw return than the government is offering on its debt. About the Fund

The SPDR S&P 500 ETF Trust tracks the S&P 500 index — a cap-weighted basket of roughly 500 large US companies selected by S&P Dow Jones Indices and representing approximately 80% of the total US stock market by value. Cap-weighted means the companies with the largest market values (like Nvidia and Apple) have the biggest influence on the fund's return. The fund was launched in January 1993 and is the oldest US-listed exchange-traded fund; it holds shares in proportion to each company's market value and rebalances automatically as prices and index compositions shift.

The fund is structured as a Unit Investment Trust, an older legal wrapper that has operated for more than thirty years. Its annual cost is 0.0945% of assets — roughly 95 cents per year for every $1,000 invested — which sits well below the 0.15% hard ceiling in the rules. The fund currently holds no tracking difference or tracking error figures (both are listed as unavailable in the latest factsheet data and should be verified from the State Street SPDR factsheet directly). The fund does not issue a K-1 tax form. Dividend income is distributed quarterly; capital gain distributions are typically zero given the in-kind redemption structure of the UIT wrapper, though this should be verified from the State Street annual report.

The Case for the Bet

The core proposition is that the S&P 500 basket will deliver more than the risk-free rate (the yield on US government bonds) plus a modest additional return for the extra uncertainty of owning equities, over the holding period. The bet is not that this basket is always a bargain — it explicitly is not — but that over time the basket's earnings yield will exceed the risk-free rate by at least two percentage points. Six conditions define when the bet stops working:

  1. The equity risk premium turns persistently negative for two consecutive quarters. The risk premium is the gap between the basket's earnings yield and the ten-year Treasury yield. When that gap is negative, risk-free government bonds pay more than owning the entire S&P 500 — a signal that the market is priced for optimism rather than value.

Latest reading: The equity risk premium is −0.86% as of 2026-07-15, meaning the basket earnings yield of 3.69% trails the Treasury at 4.545% by 86 basis points. This has been negative every day since 2026-06-16 (twelve consecutive daily snapshots). The exit condition requires two consecutive quarters of persistent negativity; the clock started around 2026-06-16 and is running, but the quarterly threshold has not yet elapsed. This driver is challenging the thesis.

  1. State Street raises the annual expense ratio above 0.15%. The fund's running cost is the most predictable drag on returns. At 0.0945% it leaves about 5.5 basis points of headroom before the threshold — headroom that has historically been wide and stable.

Latest reading: Expense ratio 0.0945% as of 2026-07-15. Headroom: 5.55 basis points below the ceiling. Exit condition not triggered.

  1. Assets under management fall below $100 billion. A fund of this scale should not fail a liquidity test, but the rule exists because extraordinary events (a major institutional redemption wave, a trust restructuring) could in principle impair trading conditions.

Latest reading: Assets under management $781.2 billion as of 2026-07-15 — approximately 7.8 times the floor. Average daily dollar volume around $39.8 billion. Exit condition not triggered.

  1. The top single holding exceeds 13% of the portfolio. Normal index drift in a cap-weighted basket pushes the largest names higher over time. The 13% threshold is calibrated to flag a genuine regime change in concentration — not ordinary drift.

Latest reading: Nvidia (NVDA) at 7.50% is the largest holding as of 2026-07-15, leaving 5.5 percentage points of headroom before the ceiling. Exit condition not triggered.

  1. S&P Dow Jones Indices materially restructures the S&P 500 methodology. The S&P 500's value as a broad-market passive vehicle depends on the index rules staying stable. A fundamental change — say, dropping the profitability screen or shifting to equal-weighting — would alter the mandate the bet was written on.

Latest reading: No automated monitoring signal for a methodology change is available in the pipeline; this condition is monitored externally. No change has been announced. Exit condition not triggered.

  1. The median return on equity of the ten largest holdings collapses below 15%. The earnings yield of the basket rests partly on whether the constituent businesses are genuinely profitable. A collapse in the median quality reading would mean the basket had lost its underlying compounding power.

Latest reading: Median return on equity of the deduped top nine holdings is 37.28% as of 2026-07-15 — well above the 15% floor. The weighted-mean (70.4%) is inflated by Apple and Nvidia's aggressive share-buyback programmes, which shrink book equity and push calculated returns to implausibly high levels; the median is the load-bearing figure. Exit condition not triggered.

What's Wobbling

One condition is actively challenging the bet: the equity risk premium. At −0.86%, the basket earns less than a ten-year US government bond. That is not a marginal shortfall — it means the market is currently pricing in a future where corporate earnings grow fast enough to make today's expensive price look reasonable in hindsight. The bet disagrees: it requires the basket to yield more than risk-free, not less.

The persistence clock matters here. The exit condition as written requires two consecutive quarters of negative equity risk premium, not just twelve days. The first quarter started around 2026-06-16; it has not elapsed. Until it does, the framework's answer is to trim, not exit. The other five conditions are all clean: vehicle costs are stable, the fund is enormous and liquid, Nvidia has not seized the index, the methodology is unchanged, and the quality of the top holdings (median return on equity 37.28%) is solid.

The technology tilt is worth watching even though it does not itself trigger an exit condition. Technology accounts for 38.55% of the basket by weight — a structural feature of cap-weighting driven by the dominant market values of Nvidia, Apple, Microsoft, and Broadcom. A sharp sector rotation or a sustained earnings miss in technology would pull the basket's earnings yield and return on equity in the wrong direction simultaneously. The rules do not have a direct trigger for this, but it is the single most concentrated risk in the vehicle.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • The equity risk premium stays negative for two full quarters. If the basket earnings yield of 3.69% does not recover above the ten-year Treasury (currently 4.545%) before roughly 2026-09-16, exit condition (1) fires and the position is closed.
  • A sharp earnings contraction meets a multiple compression simultaneously. The five-year bear scenario assumes a 13% decline in basket earnings combined with the market re-rating from 27× to 15× earnings. That combination — which mirrors 2000–2002 and 2008–2009 — produces an annualised return of −22.7% over five years. The downside is materially larger than the upside in the base scenario (+1.1% annualised).
  • Technology rotation amplifies the drawdown. At 38.55% of the basket, a sustained de-rating of the technology sector — driven by margin pressure, regulation, or an AI-cycle disappointment — would hit the basket harder than the broad-market bear case implies.
  • S&P 500 index methodology changes. If S&P Dow Jones Indices restructures the constituent selection rules in a way that undermines the broad-market mandate, exit condition (5) fires. This is externally monitored; no signal is currently present.
  • Expense ratio drift. State Street has held SPY's cost at 0.0945% for an extended period, but the 0.15% ceiling is a hard limit. A fee increase with no competitive response would trigger exit condition (2).

The Rules

The action plan has six branches, one for each combination of conditions and price:

  • If the thesis is confirmed (all drivers holding, basket priced inside the bands): hold — basket is reasonably priced and the vehicle is sound.
  • If the thesis is weakened (one or more drivers challenging but no exit condition triggered): do not add. Re-validate after the next quarter. If the equity risk premium stays negative or the median look-through return on equity keeps falling, consider swapping to a comparable index fund or moving to cash per the position-sizing rule.
  • If the thesis is broken (vehicle integrity breached or exit condition triggered): exit. Move proceeds to risk-free or to a holding that meets the value test.
  • If the price falls below the buy band (basket earnings yield reaches 6.25% or higher): add to the position. Basket is meaningfully cheap relative to risk-free.
  • If the price rises above the trim band (basket earnings yield falls to 4.00% or lower): trim 25–33%. Basket is meaningfully expensive relative to risk-free; harvest the premium.
  • If the price rises above the exit band (basket earnings yield falls to 3.00% or lower): exit. Basket is fully priced relative to risk-free. Redeploy proceeds to risk-free or to a holding that meets the value test.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
