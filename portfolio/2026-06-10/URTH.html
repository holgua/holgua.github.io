All World: The Bet Is Wobbling

The Bottom Line

The All World fund is a single, low-cost basket of about 1,300 large and mid-sized companies spread across 23 wealthy countries — a one-line way to own developed-markets equity without picking individual shares. The bet behind holding it is that, over a long stretch of years, the basket's underlying earnings will pay a meaningful premium over what risk-free government bonds pay. As of June 2026 that premium has gone the wrong way: the basket's earnings yield sits just below the yield on ten-year US Treasuries, so the value test the bet rests on is failing on the first measurement. Nothing about the fund itself is broken — the cost is still low, the size is comfortable, and the quality of the underlying companies is genuinely high — so the call is to keep what is held and add nothing more. The one thing to watch is whether the earnings-versus-Treasury gap stays negative for a second quarter in a row.

The Verdict

The overall call is to hold what is already held and wait — neither sell nor buy more — until either the value test improves or one of the explicit lines in the sand is crossed. The fund's plumbing is sound; only the price the basket is trading at, relative to what cash pays, is off. Below, the same call is sketched against five plausible reader situations so anyone can locate themselves on the map.

  • As a fresh holding at today's price (around $200 per share). The rules say wait. The basket is trading rich against risk-free bonds, and the share price sits well above the cheap-edge price of $135 — meaning there is no cushion for a fresh entry. A first standing alert at $169 (the fair-value anchor) and a second at $135 (the cheap edge) marks the prices at which the case to start buying would warrant another look. Until then, cash is the simpler, better-paid alternative.
  • As an existing holding bought below $200 per share — currently sitting on a gain of zero per cent or more. The rules say hold. A gain on the position is not itself a reason to trim; trimming is only triggered when the basket's earnings yield crosses the expensive line, or when one of the fund-integrity lines (cost, size, top-name weight) is breached. None of those triggers have fired, so the position stays as-is.
  • As an existing holding bought roughly between $200 and $250 per share — currently around break-even, give or take a loss of about 20 per cent. The rules say hold. Today's share price sits inside this band. The bet is wobbling but not broken, the fund itself is sound, so the right move is to wait for the next quarterly read on the earnings-versus-Treasury gap before doing anything. No adding, no trimming.
  • As an existing holding bought between $250 and $300 per share — currently showing a loss of about 20 to 33 per cent. The rules say hold. Selling now would lock in a meaningful loss while the underlying fund still meets every operational test. The rules call for re-reading the figures after the next quarter; if the earnings-versus-Treasury gap stays negative, or if the look-through quality starts slipping, the rules then call for swapping into the cheaper European-listed equivalent (annual cost 0.20 per cent versus 0.24 per cent here) or stepping aside into cash. None of that is triggered yet.
  • As an existing holding bought above $300 per share — currently showing a loss of roughly 33 per cent or more. The rules say hold. A deep underwater position at a wobbling — but not broken — bet is the worst possible moment to capitulate; the fund's plumbing is fully intact, the underlying companies are still high-quality compounders, and selling here turns a paper loss into a realised one without any of the structural exit conditions having tripped. Same instructions as the mild-loss case: re-read after the next quarter, swap to the cheaper European version or move to cash if the value test keeps deteriorating, but do nothing reflexively now.

About the Fund

The All World fund — formally the iShares MSCI World ETF, ticker URTH — is a single share that represents a tiny slice of roughly 1,300 large and mid-sized companies, all listed in the world's wealthy markets. About 70 per cent of the basket is American: Nvidia, Apple, Alphabet, Microsoft, Amazon and the other familiar US giants. The other 30 per cent is spread across 22 other developed markets — Japan, the United Kingdom, France, Switzerland, Germany, Canada, and so on. The basket follows the MSCI World index, meaning the weight each company gets is proportional to its market value, and the list is reshuffled mechanically as company sizes change.

There is no manager picking shares. The fund's job is to hold the index, charge a small annual fee for doing so (0.24 per cent — meaning 24 cents a year for every $100 invested), and pass through the dividends. It is held in the portfolio as the broad, default way to own developed-world equity — distinct from a pure American-index fund because it dilutes the US concentration with that 30 per cent ex-US sleeve, which lowers the risk of being entirely captive to one country's market regime.

The Case for the Bet

The bet, stated as a single idea: that owning a broad, cheap basket of the world's largest developed-market companies will, over a long enough holding period, pay a meaningful premium over the yield on safe government bonds. Not every day — not even every year. But across the full holding period, the basket's underlying earnings yield should beat what ten-year US Treasuries pay by at least two percentage points.

For the bet to keep working, six conditions must hold:

  1. The value test eventually clears. The basket's earnings yield — the share of the underlying earnings, expressed as a yield on price — must, over the holding period, exceed the yield on ten-year US Treasuries by at least two percentage points. The bet does not require this to be true every quarter; it requires it to be true on average. The hard floor: if the basket's earnings yield falls below 3 per cent and stays below the Treasury yield for two consecutive quarters, the basket is decisively expensive and the bet is off.
  2. The annual fee stays low. The fund's annual expense ratio must remain at or below 0.35 per cent. It is currently 0.24 per cent. The European-listed equivalent (IWDA, a Dublin-domiciled near-twin) runs at 0.20 per cent, so any further hike here without a competitive response would signal the manager has stopped competing on price.
  3. The fund stays large enough to trade cleanly. Total assets in the fund must stay above $2 billion. They currently sit at about $8.1 billion. Below the $2 billion line, the gap between the price to buy and the price to sell starts to widen meaningfully, eroding the cost advantage of holding the basket through this vehicle.
  4. No single company comes to dominate the basket. The largest single holding must stay below 10 per cent of the basket. The current top name, Nvidia, sits at about 5.7 per cent. A global cap-weighted index with one name at 10 per cent is no longer a developed-markets bet; it is a bet on that one company.
  5. The index methodology stays recognisable. MSCI Inc., the firm that defines the index, must not materially restructure what counts as a developed market, drop major constituents, or change the weighting scheme away from market-value weighting. A structural rewrite of the index undermines the very thing the basket was bought to deliver.
  6. The basket's underlying companies stay high-quality. The median return that the largest companies earn on their book equity — the share of each dollar of accounting capital that comes back as profit — must stay above 15 per cent. Currently it sits at about 37 per cent. A collapse below 15 per cent would mean the basket has stopped being a compounding vehicle.

The Rules

The rules that translate the state of those conditions into action:

  1. If the conditions are all working and the basket is reasonably priced, hold. The fund is sound, the price is sensible, nothing to do.
  2. If one or more conditions are weakening — but not broken — do not add. Re-read the figures after the next quarter. If the earnings-versus-Treasury gap stays negative or the underlying quality keeps slipping, swap into the cheaper European equivalent (annual cost 0.20 per cent), or step aside into cash.
  3. If any condition is decisively broken — the value test fails for two straight quarters at sub-3-per-cent earnings yield, the fee jumps above 0.35 per cent, the fund shrinks below $2 billion, the top name passes 10 per cent, the index is materially rewritten, or look-through quality collapses below 15 per cent — wind down the position. The proceeds move to safe government bonds or to a holding that meets the value test.
  4. If the share price falls below the cheap-edge level — meaning the basket's earnings yield crosses above 6.25 per cent — add to the position. The basket is meaningfully cheap against risk-free at that point.
  5. If the share price rises above the expensive-edge level — meaning the basket's earnings yield falls below 4 per cent — trim a quarter to a third of the position. Harvest some of the premium while keeping the core.

The Conditions Today

Five of the six conditions are working comfortably; one is failing.

The value test is failing. The basket's earnings yield is 4.21 per cent. The ten-year Treasury pays 4.54 per cent. That makes the earnings premium minus 0.32 per cent — meaning, on the snapshot date, the basket pays slightly less in underlying earnings than the safe government bond does, before any reward for taking equity risk. The bet asks for that premium to be at least plus two per cent over the holding period; today it is about 2.3 percentage points short of the bar. This is exactly the failure mode the original written thesis flagged at calibration — the bet was entered with eyes open, with the expectation that the value test would clear over time, not on day one.

The fee is well inside the line. Annual cost is 0.24 per cent — eleven hundredths of a percentage point of headroom before the 0.35 per cent ceiling kicks in. No fee drift.

The fund is comfortably large. Total assets are $8.1 billion, more than four times the $2 billion liquidity floor. Trading spreads should stay tight.

No single name is dominating. Nvidia is the top holding at 5.73 per cent, about four percentage points below the 10 per cent ceiling. Concentration is elevated for a global blend but not in regime-change territory.

The index methodology is unchanged. No signal of a structural rewrite by MSCI Inc. in this snapshot; this condition is monitored externally rather than by automated feed.

The basket's underlying quality is strong. The median return on book equity across the top ten holdings is 37.3 per cent — well above the 20-per-cent comfort line and far from the 15-per-cent collapse threshold. The top two names, Apple and Nvidia, post even higher returns (141 per cent and 114 per cent respectively), but only because aggressive share buy-backs have shrunk their accounting capital to the point that the ratio overstates the picture. The median — the middle name, undistorted by buy-backs at the extremes — is the trustworthy read, and it is healthy.

The Share Price

The fund last traded at $200.38 per share. In the rules' language, that puts it just below the expensive edge of $211, just above the fair-value anchor of $168.84, and well above the cheap-edge level of $135. The basket's earnings yield, at 4.21 per cent, sits inside the band — closer to the expensive line than to fair value. Translated to share price: it would take roughly an 11-dollar rise to push the share price across the expensive line and trigger the trimming rule, or roughly a 65-dollar fall to push it below the cheap-edge and trigger the adding rule. The price is neither cheap nor outright expensive — it is fair-ish, leaning rich.

The Standing Orders

The rules translate the price bands into two standing alerts and two standing orders. Each is the operationalisation of a pre-committed rule — not a new judgement.

Price alerts (informational). - A first alert at $168.84, the fair-value anchor. The crossing means the share price has returned to a neutral reading; no action follows, but the conditions warrant a fresh read. - A second alert at $135.07, the cheap-edge. The crossing arms the buy order below; before adding, the next quarterly check should confirm the conditions are still working.

Standing orders (actionable). - A standing buy order at $135.07. The rules call for adding to the position once the share price closes at or below this line, provided the conditions are still working. The order may be staged across one or two quarters rather than filled in a single fill. - A standing trim order at $211.05. The rules call for trimming a quarter to a third of the position when the share price closes at or above this line, to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.

There is no standing exit order tied to a share-price level. For a passive index basket of this kind, the sell-side of the rulebook is governed by the fund-integrity tests — fee, size, top-name weight, methodology — not by share price. The bet does not give up on the basket because the multiple is high; it gives up if the vehicle itself stops doing its job.

What's Wobbling

One condition is wobbling: the value test. The basket's earnings yield, at 4.21 per cent, is below the ten-year Treasury yield of 4.54 per cent. The required two-percentage-point premium has gone the wrong way — by about 2.3 percentage points.

This wobble does not yet cross any of the lines drawn in The Case for the Bet. The hard floor on the value test asks for two things together: the basket's earnings yield must fall below 3 per cent, and the earnings-versus-Treasury gap must stay negative for two consecutive quarters. Only one of those — the negative gap — is true today, and this is the first measurement. The basket's earnings yield itself, at 4.21 per cent, is still a full 1.2 percentage points above the 3-per-cent trigger. The condition is failing on the snapshot but not on the structural test.

That is why the call is hold-and-wait rather than wind-down. The bet was entered knowing the value test would not clear on day one; it was entered on the proposition that it will clear over the holding period. One more quarter of data is required before the rules promote the wobble into a break.

What Could Break the Fund

The cases in which the bet stops working — and the basket would need to be wound down — are limited and concrete:

  • The earnings-versus-Treasury gap stays negative for a second quarter and the basket's earnings yield falls below 3 per cent. That would mean the basket is being structurally outpriced by risk-free bonds, not just temporarily so.
  • A meaningful re-rating of the underlying companies. If the median return on book equity of the top ten holdings collapses below 15 per cent — for example through a sustained earnings contraction at the Tech-heavy top — the basket would no longer be a compounding vehicle.
  • A regime-change concentration event. If the top name (today Nvidia) climbs past 10 per cent of the basket, the bet stops being a developed-markets bet and becomes a single-name bet.
  • A structural rewrite of the index by MSCI Inc. A reclassification of major countries, a change in weighting methodology, or dropped constituents could undermine the mandate.
  • An operational drift in the fund itself. A fee hike above 0.35 per cent or a drop in fund assets below $2 billion would erode the cost-and-liquidity advantage the basket was bought to deliver.

The most realistic of these, and the one currently moving, is the first: a second quarter of negative earnings-versus-Treasury gap combined with a further multiple expansion that pushes the basket's earnings yield below the 3-per-cent floor.

Gaps in the Picture

A few honest caveats on the underlying figures:

  • The basket's earnings yield is a trailing measure — it uses the past year of earnings, not a forecast. A meaningful re-rating in either direction will not show up until reported earnings catch up.
  • The look-through quality measure (median return on book equity across the top ten) excludes about 99 per cent of the basket. The top ten represent only roughly 28 per cent of the fund by weight; the other 72 per cent is not directly inspected here. The expectation is that the broad mass of mid-cap names follows the same broad pattern, but the median figure is a sample, not a complete census.
  • A long-cycle valuation measure that smooths earnings over a full ten years (often called a cyclically-adjusted view) was not available for the basket from the data source used; only the trailing measure is in evidence. A cyclically-adjusted view would likely make today's price look richer still, not cheaper.
  • The index-methodology condition is monitored externally rather than by automated feed. A change announced by MSCI Inc. between snapshots would not show up here until the next manual check.

None of these gaps changes the overall call. They are flagged so that the figures are read with the right amount of confidence — and the right amount of patience for the next quarterly read.
