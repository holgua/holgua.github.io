Recent Development

Microsoft's annual results for the year ended 30 June 2026 confirm that the operating franchise is tracking the base case. Operating income grew 20.8% year on year to $155 billion, and gross profit rose 16.3% to $225 billion — both well ahead of the respective exit-condition thresholds in the Case for the Bet. Azure sustained its 40% growth rate through the three quarters of FY2026 for which quarterly data is available, and commercial contracted backlog reached $627 billion (up 99% year on year as of March 2026), providing multi-year revenue visibility that is not a forecast but a signed obligation.

The notable development is on the cash generation side. The FY2026 annual filing records capital expenditure of $116 billion — nearly double the $65 billion spent in FY2025 — as Microsoft locks in datacenter capacity for AI workloads. That spending compressed the ratio of free cash flow after stock-based compensation to net income to approximately 0.41 for the year, the first annual reading below the 0.65 threshold that the thesis flags as a warning line. Exit condition 2 requires the ratio to remain below that threshold for two consecutive fiscal years; FY2025 came in at 0.70, so the sequence has not been met. The rule counts annual filings, not quarters, which means the next verdict arrives with the FY2027 10-K in approximately July 2027.

The valuation picture has a two-frame quality that is worth naming plainly. The earnings-yield framework — which anchors fair value to current cash generation — places fair value at roughly $208 per share and the current price of $392 well above it. The scenario model, which projects the business through the capital-expenditure cycle and applies market multiples to expected future earnings, arrives at roughly $632 as the base-case midpoint. Both methods are internally consistent; the gap reflects whether one anchors to today's compressed cash flows or to the three-year trajectory. For more on this, see the Share Price.

About Microsoft

Microsoft makes most of its money selling software and computing infrastructure to large organisations. The largest segment by profit is Intelligent Cloud, which houses Azure — Microsoft's cloud computing platform, where enterprise customers rent processing power, storage, and increasingly artificial-intelligence services rather than building and maintaining their own servers. The second segment is Productivity and Business Processes: the Microsoft 365 suite (the applications most office workers use every day) sold as a subscription, alongside the LinkedIn professional network and the Dynamics 365 enterprise-management software. The third is More Personal Computing, which includes the Windows operating system licensing, Xbox gaming, and Bing search advertising — smaller, more cyclical, and less central to the thesis.

What ties the business together is identity. Nearly every large enterprise in the world runs its employee logins, application permissions, and security policies through Microsoft's Entra ID infrastructure (formerly called Azure Active Directory). That infrastructure sits at the centre of the enterprise computing stack — every cloud application, every device, every access rule depends on it. Migrating away from Microsoft is therefore not a software decision; it is an IT-estate reconstruction project that most organisations are not willing to attempt. This switching cost is the economic foundation of the thesis.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that Microsoft's enterprise-identity lock-in and cloud-infrastructure position will sustain returns well above the cost of capital for the next several years, and that the current surge in capital expenditure is building a structurally wider moat rather than permanently compressing cash returns. The conditions that must keep holding for that bet to work are:

  1. Azure revenue growth stays above 25% per quarter while capital expenditure is above 20% of revenue. Below 25% on a sustained basis, with the infrastructure spending still elevated, is the signature of an AI investment cycle that is not converting into contracted demand — the central risk of the thesis.

Latest reading: Azure grew 40% in the three months ending March 2026, the third consecutive quarter at or near that level. Capital expenditure ran at 37% of revenue in the same quarter — above the 20% threshold, but Azure growth remained far above the 25% exit-condition floor. The compound condition (low growth plus high spending) has not been triggered.

  1. The ratio of free cash flow to net income stays above 0.65 on an annual basis for two consecutive years. Below that level, the AI infrastructure spending is impairing cash generation rather than building for future scale.

Latest reading: The nine-month ratio through March 2026 was approximately 0.48, and the full-year FY2026 ratio on a post-stock-compensation basis came in at 0.41 — below the 0.65 threshold. This is the first annual filing to cross the line. Exit condition 2 requires two consecutive annual filings below threshold; FY2025 was 0.70. The sequence has not yet been met, and the FY2027 annual filing will be the deciding data point.

  1. Consolidated operating profit margin stays above 42% per quarter for two consecutive quarters. Below 42% for two quarters running signals that software revenue is no longer outpacing the cost of the infrastructure build — a structural shift, not a timing effect.

Latest reading: The operating margin was 46.3% in the three months ending March 2026 and 47.4% for the nine months to that date — both more than four percentage points above the exit-condition floor, and both expanding year on year despite the infrastructure surge.

  1. The IRS transfer-pricing dispute does not escalate to Tax Court, and no single quarterly tax charge exceeds $5 billion. An escalation to Tax Court or a settlement announcement at scale would signal the balance-sheet risk moving from contingent to live.

Latest reading: As of the annual filing dated 2026-07-29, the $28.9 billion notice of proposed adjustment remains in administrative appeals. The language in the filing is unchanged from prior quarters; no Tax Court petition has been filed, and no discrete settlement charge has been disclosed.

  1. No formal antitrust complaint seeking structural remedies — such as separating the cloud, artificial-intelligence, or identity businesses — is filed by the Federal Trade Commission. A formal complaint seeking to unbundle those products would directly attack the switching-cost moat.

Latest reading: No formal complaint has been disclosed in the FY2026 annual filing. Antitrust scrutiny is cited as a standard risk factor. The investigation, which began in late 2024, remains in an early phase.

  1. Gross profit margin stays above 65% per quarter for two consecutive quarters. Below 65% for two consecutive quarters signals that AI infrastructure costs are passing through to erode the economics of the software franchise.

Latest reading: The consolidated gross margin was 67.6% in the three months ending March 2026 and 68.2% for the nine months to that date — both more than two percentage points above the floor. The Microsoft Cloud segment ran at 66%, also above the threshold.

  1. Commercial contracted backlog grows at least 30% per year and does not decline in absolute terms from one quarter to the next. A slowdown below 30% or an outright quarterly decline would signal that enterprise commitments are weakening and the revenue pipeline is eroding.

Latest reading: The commercial contracted backlog stood at $627 billion as of March 2026, up 99% year on year, with roughly $157 billion — about a quarter of the total — due to convert into recognised revenue within the next twelve months. The exit-condition floor of 30% growth or absolute decline is not close to being tested.

The Share Price

The current share price is $392. Where it sits relative to the Rules depends on which valuation frame one uses, and on this point the two methods embedded in the analysis reach very different conclusions.

The earnings-yield framework — which asks what fraction of the current market value the business generates in annual cash earnings — places the current yield at about 1.86%. The rule sets fair value at a 3.5% yield, the buy threshold at 5.0%, and the trim threshold at 2.0%. At 1.86%, the share price sits above the trim line: the market has compressed the yield below the threshold at which the pre-committed rules call for harvesting part of the position. On this frame, fair value is approximately $208.

The scenario model takes a different approach: it applies the price multiples at which comparable businesses and Microsoft's own trading history are valued to the business's projected future earnings across the three scenarios. On that method, the base-case midpoint is roughly $632 — meaning the current price of $392 would represent a meaningful discount to what the business might be worth if the base case tracks. The gap between the two frames is methodological, not an error; the cash-yield method anchors to today's compressed cash flows during a capital-expenditure surge, while the scenario model looks through to the trajectory that the $627 billion contracted backlog implies.

For the purpose of the pre-committed rules, the yield framework governs the trim signal. The scenario model governs the sizing decision for what remains after a trim: if the base case tracks, the residual position still has meaningful room to run. The two frames agree that the price is not cheap and that a full exit is not called for; they disagree on how expensive the current price is, and that disagreement explains why the rule calls for a partial reduction rather than a full exit.

What's Wobbling

Two drivers are in mixed territory, neither crossing a hard exit line but both moving in the wrong direction.

The first is the ratio of free cash flow to net income. On a post-stock-compensation basis, the ratio reached 0.41 for the full FY2026 year — below the 0.65 threshold that the thesis identifies as a monitoring floor. The cause is the scale of capital expenditure: $116 billion for the year, roughly double the prior year's figure, as Microsoft builds out datacenter capacity for AI infrastructure. The underlying operating business is generating cash at a healthy clip — operating cash flow rose 26% year on year in the March 2026 quarter — but total capital spending is absorbing a disproportionate share. Exit condition 2 requires two consecutive annual filings below the floor; one year is not a trigger. The direction matters, and the FY2027 filing will resolve it.

The second is capital expenditure as a percentage of revenue. At roughly 35–36% of revenue for the full FY2026 year, capital spending has exceeded the 30% solo bear-trigger level — meaning the infrastructure build is running at a pace that, in isolation, raises questions about whether the returns will justify the outlay. The compound exit condition (which combines the CapEx level with a deceleration in Azure growth below 25%) has not triggered because Azure sustained 40% growth. But the CapEx intensity alone is an elevated reading that warrants watching. The question is whether the $627 billion contracted backlog translates to revenue fast enough to bring the CapEx ratio back below 30% by FY2027–2028.

Neither wobble crosses a hard threshold in isolation today. Together, they define the monitoring agenda for the next twelve months: the FY2027 annual filing is the first genuine test of whether the AI infrastructure build is paying off in cash terms.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Azure growth decelerates materially. If Azure revenue growth falls below 25% for two consecutive quarters while capital expenditure remains above 20% of revenue, the thesis's central proposition — that the AI build is monetising — is falsified. The contracted backlog provides some insulation, but deceleration in the consumption rate would erode confidence in the $627 billion pipeline's conversion speed.
  • Free cash flow compression becomes permanent. If the FY2027 annual filing shows a second consecutive year of the cash-to-net-income ratio below 0.65, exit condition 2 triggers a mandatory review. The bear case does not require Azure collapse — it merely requires that the infrastructure spending fails to produce the contracted backlog conversion at the pace the thesis assumes.
  • The IRS dispute escalates or settles at the high end. The total notice of proposed adjustment stands at $28.9 billion, with penalties and interest potentially adding further. A Tax Court filing or a settlement announcement near the high end would create a material near-term balance-sheet shock, absorbing a significant portion of the current net-cash position.
  • The Federal Trade Commission files a formal complaint seeking structural remedies. A formal action requiring Microsoft to unbundle its cloud, identity, and productivity products — even if ultimately unsuccessful — would create years of regulatory uncertainty and could force product-design choices that weaken the switching-cost moat at the margin.
  • Open-source artificial-intelligence models commoditise Azure's AI differentiation. The core identity and switching-cost moat does not depend on AI, but a significant portion of Azure's current growth rate is tied to AI workload demand. If open-source models reduce the premium of Azure's AI services, the growth rate could decelerate toward the rate implied by the pure infrastructure and identity business, which is lower.

Gaps in the Picture

The earnings methodology uses total capital expenditure rather than maintenance-only spending. This matters because Microsoft's AI infrastructure build is classified as growth capital expenditure — spending to create capacity that does not yet exist, not to maintain existing capacity. Subtracting total capital expenditure from operating cash flow therefore understates normalised owner earnings during the build phase; the net income proxy (which does not subtract capital expenditure at all) overstates it. The earnings-yield anchor used for the trim rule sits between these two extremes and reflects a deliberate methodological choice rather than a calculation error. The full-year FY2026 post-stock-compensation owner earnings of $54.6 billion and the resulting 1.86% yield are the trigger inputs for the trim rule; they reflect the current cost of the AI build, not a permanent normalised earnings floor.

The artificial-intelligence annual recurring revenue figure — cited as $37 billion (up 123% year on year) from the third quarter FY2026 earnings release — is not a GAAP disclosure and does not appear in the annual 10-K filing. There is no way to verify the figure from public filing sources. The base-case trajectory (above $50 billion within four quarters of the disclosure) cannot be confirmed until management discloses an updated number in an earnings release.

The competitive position assessment notes that Microsoft lost exclusive access to OpenAI models in April 2026 and that Alphabet (Google's parent) completed a significant security acquisition in the same period. These are noted as narrowing two previously durable advantages. The full-year impact on Azure AI differentiation will become visible in subsequent quarterly filings.

The Rules

The rules are pre-committed based on thesis state and share-price position. They apply in the order listed; the first rule that fires governs.

  • If the business has executed a written exit condition — specifically: Azure growth below 25% for two consecutive quarters while capital expenditure exceeds 20% of revenue; the cash-to-net-income ratio below 0.65 for two consecutive fiscal years without improvement in Azure artificial-intelligence revenue; operating margin below 42% for two consecutive quarters; the IRS dispute escalated to Tax Court or a single-quarter tax charge above $5 billion; a formal antitrust complaint from the Federal Trade Commission seeking structural remedies; gross margin below 65% for two consecutive quarters; or commercial contracted backlog growth below 30% or an absolute quarterly decline — then exit the full position within 30 days, with no averaging down.
  • If the thesis conditions are weakening without a hard exit having triggered, do not add to the position. Monitor for one additional quarter before reassessing size. Distinguish cyclical timing effects (capital expenditure peaking) from structural competitive displacement. Escalate to exit review if a second consecutive quarter confirms the deteriorating signal.
  • If the share price is below the buy threshold — earnings yield above 5.0% — and no exit condition has triggered, add in tranches: a first tranche below $352 (which corresponds to a 40% margin of safety against the base-case scenario midpoint) and a second below $322. Each tranche represents 25–33% of the intended add-on allocation.
  • If the share price is above the trim threshold — earnings yield below 2.0%, currently the case — trim 20–25% of the position to harvest the premium. Do not trim below 50% of the current holding unless a thesis exit condition has also triggered. Re-evaluate remaining size after the trim.
  • If the share price is above the exit threshold — earnings yield below 1.5%, which corresponds to roughly $1456 at current earnings — exit the full position and redeploy proceeds. No re-entry within 90 days.
  • If all the above conditions remain untriggered — thesis confirmed, share price in the hold band — hold the position. Continue monitoring at each quarterly earnings release.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
