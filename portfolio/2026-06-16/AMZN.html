Amazon: The Bet Still Works

Updated 2026-06-16 from the 10-Q filed 2026-04-30.

The Bottom Line

Amazon's cloud and advertising engines delivered their strongest combined quarter in recent memory, and none of the eight pre-written exit conditions has been triggered. The share price at $250 sits inside the valuation band — above what a fresh buyer would call fair value but well below the level at which the position becomes dangerously expensive. The single condition to watch is whether Amazon's extraordinary spending on data-centre infrastructure — $147 billion in the twelve months to March 2026 — begins to generate proportionately less revenue from its cloud business over the next two annual cycles.

The Verdict

Five situations, starting with the best outcome for someone entering today.

Fresh entry below $140 — no position yet. At $140, the cash-flow yield on Amazon's owner earnings would stand at 7.5%, a level that historically signals a meaningful discount to fair value. The pre-committed instruction is to add the position in tranches across one to two quarters, provided no exit condition has fired and the cloud division is still growing at least 18% a year.

In at below $250 — sitting on a gain. The current price is slightly below the trim edge of $263. The thesis is intact, all core metrics are confirming, and the right action is to hold without change. The trim edge is close enough to warrant setting a price alert.

In at $250–$300 — roughly even or a small loss. All thesis pillars are intact. No exit condition has been triggered. The cloud business just posted its fastest quarterly growth in at least six quarters. The recommended action is to hold, treat the position as fully operational, and wait for either a price drop toward $140 or a continued rise toward $263 before taking any action.

In at $300–$350 — a loss of roughly 17% to 29%. The position is underwater, but the investment case is unchanged. Crystallising a loss at this point would mean selling precisely when the thesis is working. The pre-committed action is to hold and continue compounding.

About Amazon

Amazon began as an online bookseller but has long since become something harder to categorise: a sprawling logistics and technology conglomerate that happens to operate one of the world's largest shopping platforms. The retail and delivery operation — encompassing both the goods Amazon sells directly and the third-party marketplace where outside merchants sell — generated $588 billion in combined sales in the full year to December 2025. That operation is the cash engine and the customer-acquisition flywheel for everything else.

The everything else is where the profit lives. Amazon Web Services, known as AWS, sells computing power, data storage, and software tools to companies that would rather rent digital infrastructure than build it themselves. In 2025 it brought in $128.7 billion in revenue while producing $45.6 billion in operating profit — a profit margin of 35%, roughly five times the margin of the retail division. Alongside AWS, Amazon's advertising business — built on the buying-intent signals generated by hundreds of millions of shoppers — reached $68.6 billion in annual sales at an equally high implied margin. The thesis for owning the shares rests on these two profit engines, not on the underlying retail operation.

The Case for the Bet

The central idea is that Amazon operates the most diversified compounding machine in large-cap technology: a cash-generative retail and logistics core that funds two high-margin profit pools — cloud computing and advertising — whose economics are still well below full maturity. The bet is that these two pools will compound operating profit faster than revenue for at least three to five more years.

Three numbered conditions must hold for the bet to remain valid:

  1. AWS quarterly revenue growth stays above 15% for every rolling pair of quarters. The exit threshold is not a target; it is the floor below which the entire AI-infrastructure investment narrative breaks. The working target is 18–20%. In the first quarter of 2026, AWS grew 28.4% year on year.

  2. AWS operating margin stays above 28% for every rolling pair of quarters. Heavy data-centre investment is compressing margins temporarily — from 39% a year ago to 38% today — but at 38% the division remains nearly ten percentage points above the exit floor. The bet requires that margin stabilises rather than continues its decline.

  3. Amazon's aggregate capital spending does not remain above $120 billion in both 2027 and 2028 without a matching acceleration in AWS revenue. The company spent approximately $147 billion on infrastructure in the twelve months to March 2026. If that level persists two years from now with no corresponding surge in cloud revenues, the economics of the AI bet will have broken down. At present, with AWS revenue growing fast and $364 billion in contracted cloud backlog on the books, the acceleration clause is met.

The Rules

Five pre-committed if-then instructions, set before the position was opened and held constant regardless of market conditions:

  1. If all thesis conditions confirm: Hold without action. Continue compounding the cloud and advertising flywheel.

  2. If one or more conditions are weakening but no exit threshold has been crossed: Do not add to the position. Assess the specific deteriorating driver after the next two quarterly filings — one quarter of data, then confirm with a second.

  3. If a written exit condition is triggered: Exit the full position within 30 days. No averaging down. AWS revenue and margin exits require confirmation across two consecutive quarters; an imposed court remedy or an unexpected auditor change is grounds for immediate exit.

  4. If the price falls below $140: Confirm the thesis is intact first. If confirmed, add to the position in tranches over one to two quarters.

  5. If the price rises above $263: Reduce 25–33% of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding. Re-examine the remaining size after the reduction.

The Conditions Today

AWS revenue growth — confirming strongly. In the first quarter of 2026 (the period covered by the most recent quarterly filing), AWS produced $37.6 billion in revenue, a 28.4% increase over the same quarter a year earlier. That is the fastest quarterly growth rate in at least six quarters, and it stands nearly 10 percentage points above the 18% target and 13 points above the 15% exit floor. Amazon disclosed $364 billion in contracted backlog within AWS — revenue that has been promised by customers but not yet delivered — with roughly half expected to be recognised within the next two years. The condition is met by a wide margin.

AWS operating margin — mixed but safe. The margin on AWS operating profit came in at 37.7% for the first quarter of 2026, compared to 39.4% for the same period a year earlier. The compression of roughly 1.7 percentage points reflects planned investment in new data-centre infrastructure; the company attributed it explicitly to "additional investments to support AWS business growth." At 37.7%, the margin sits nearly ten full percentage points above the 28% exit threshold, making this a watch item rather than a concern today.

Advertising — confirming strongly. Advertising services revenue reached $17.2 billion in the first quarter of 2026, a 23.9% increase year on year. The company cited rising advertising sales as a primary driver of improved operating income in both its North America and International retail segments. The exit condition for this driver requires advertising growth to fall to single digits for two consecutive full years — a scenario that does not appear to be approaching.

International segment profitability — confirming. The International division, which covers all retail and marketplace operations outside North America, produced $1.4 billion in operating profit in the first quarter of 2026, up from $1.0 billion a year earlier. The margin improved from 3.0% to 3.6%. This segment turned profitable only in 2024 after years of losses; its continued expansion adds a third stream of compounding operating income to the thesis.

Capital spending trajectory — mixed, forward watch. Amazon spent approximately $147 billion on property and equipment in the twelve months to March 2026, nearly double the $88 billion spent in the equivalent prior-year period. The exit condition applies to spending in 2027 and 2028, not the current twelve-month period, and it requires that level to persist "without commensurate AWS revenue acceleration." With AWS at 28% growth and a $364 billion backlog, the acceleration clause is currently satisfied. This remains the most important item to monitor in Amazon's 2026 annual results and forward guidance.

FTC antitrust — silent. The US Federal Trade Commission monopoly trial remains ongoing, as disclosed in Amazon's legal proceedings. No court-ordered structural remedy has been imposed. Exit condition three — which would require an immediate exit — has not been triggered. The trial is scheduled to conclude in late 2026; its outcome is the primary binary risk in the entire investment.

Insider selling — no abnormal signals. The quarterly filing disclosed no insider trading arrangements, confirming that Jeff Bezos — who holds approximately 8.8% of the company and $234 billion in shares at current prices — has not filed a Form 144 (an advance notice of planned stock sales) exceeding 40 million shares in a single filing. That threshold, if crossed, would signal abnormal acceleration beyond his established selling programme and would constitute exit condition seven. It has not been crossed.

The Share Price

Amazon uses an owner-earnings yield — essentially the ratio of annual cash generation to the total value the stock market assigns to the company — as its valuation method. A higher yield means the shares are cheaper; a lower yield means they are more expensive. The fair-value yield is set at 6.0%, which equates to a share price of roughly $176. The buy edge — the price below which the yield would be attractive enough to add to the position — is $140. The trim edge — the price above which the position starts to look expensive — is $263. The exit edge, at $421, represents a yield so low (below 3.0%) that the market would be pricing in more than a decade of optimistic growth instantaneously.

At $250, the current owner-earnings yield is 4.2%. The shares sit $74 above fair value and $13 below the trim edge. There is no margin of safety for a fresh buyer at current prices. A buyer purchasing today would need to believe in the base-case scenario — AWS continuing to grow at 18–22% with gradual improvement in overall profit margins — to justify the current valuation. A discounted cash-flow analysis using conservative assumptions places intrinsic value at approximately $227 per share, implying the stock currently trades at roughly an 10% premium to that estimate. Meaningful safety of purchase would only return around $170 (25% below the $227 base-case estimate) or $136 (40% below).

A note on a data discrepancy: the separate fair-value modelling conducted by the Growth and Value analyses implies a base-case price target substantially higher than the config's $176 anchor — the base-case midpoint from that analysis is $569. This divergence does not affect the current verdict (the share price is safely within the valuation band), but it does warrant a review of the yield thresholds before any significant position change.

Alert: price reaches $176 (fair-value anchor). The owner-earnings yield at that price returns to 6.0%, the level defined as fair value. This is the signal to prepare for a possible add; it is not itself an instruction to act.

Alert: price reaches $140 (cheap edge). The yield would cross above 7.5%, signalling that the shares have entered the buy band. The pre-committed instruction at that point is to confirm the thesis is intact and then add in tranches.

Order: add below $140. If the price crosses below $140 and no exit condition has been triggered (specifically: AWS revenue still growing above 18%), add to the position in tranches over one to two quarters.

Order: reduce above $263. If the price crosses above $263, trim 25–33% of the position to harvest the premium while retaining the core holding.

Order: exit above $421. If the price crosses above $421, exit the full position. At that price the market would be pricing in roughly 11 years of base-case compounding at once — a premium that no longer leaves room for error. No re-entry within 90 days to avoid buying back on emotional anchoring to the exit price.

What's Wobbling

Capital spending is at an extreme level. Amazon spent approximately $147 billion on physical infrastructure in the twelve months to March 2026. For context, the company's operating cash flow over the same period was $148.5 billion — meaning the business essentially directed everything it earned toward building new data centres and related equipment. The management-definition measure of free cash flow (the money left after all capital spending) compressed to just $1.2 billion for the rolling twelve months. This is not a written exit condition today, but it represents the central execution risk: if the AI workload demand that is supposed to fill this infrastructure disappoints, the write-offs and revenue shortfalls could be severe. None of the exit thresholds for this risk have been crossed.

Tariff and trade-policy exposure. Amazon's marketplace contains a large proportion of Chinese-based sellers, and the company's own supply chain for consumer goods has significant China exposure. The 10-Q filing from April 2026 flagged tariff policy changes as a risk to operating results multiple times. Despite this, North America operating income grew 41% in the first quarter of 2026, which suggests the exposure is being absorbed rather than amplified. The exit condition for advertising growth — which would be affected by advertiser pullback from Chinese sellers — requires single-digit growth for two consecutive full years. At 24% growth in the most recent quarter, that condition is nowhere near being triggered.

No share buybacks since 2022. Amazon has not repurchased a single share since the first quarter of 2022, leaving $6.1 billion of a $10 billion authorisation unused for more than four years. During that time the stock fell as low as roughly $85 (in late 2022), which would have been an exceptional buyback opportunity. Instead, management issued $37 billion of new bonds in March 2026 to fund AI infrastructure spending. This is consistent with the original thesis, which characterised Amazon's capital allocation as "acceptable but not excellent," but it remains a point of friction for shareholders who believe the capital could have been deployed more effectively.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • The AI capex cycle does not convert to revenue. If Amazon's $147 billion in annual data-centre spending produces data centres that sit underutilised — because AI workload growth slows, or because Microsoft Azure and Google Cloud capture a disproportionate share of new AI workloads — Amazon's free cash flow could remain compressed for years and the resulting asset impairments could be severe. This is the primary bear scenario, representing roughly a 25% probability. The confirming early-warning signal would be AWS quarterly revenue growth printing below 18% for two consecutive quarters.
  • The FTC monopoly trial produces a structural separation order. A court-ordered requirement to separate the AWS cloud business from the retail marketplace would be an immediate exit trigger. It would also represent a severe impairment: AWS and the retail business are worth significantly more together (AWS profits fund retail investment; retail traffic feeds AWS customer acquisition) than separately. The probability is low but the impact is extreme; this is the source of the "tail risk below $100" warning in the bear-case analysis.
  • AWS operating margins continue their decline. The current compression from 39% to 38% is attributed to planned investment and is considered normal. If margins drift below 35% over several consecutive quarters — particularly if the cause is competitive pricing pressure from Microsoft or Google rather than planned reinvestment — the economic logic of the investment thesis would be under serious pressure. No exit condition is triggered at 35%, but conviction would diminish.
  • Advertising revenue slows sharply. Advertising is the underappreciated part of the thesis: $68.6 billion at +22% annual growth, monetising buying-intent data that no other advertising platform can replicate. A sustained deceleration to below 10% annual growth for two full years — potentially caused by advertiser pullback, AI-driven changes to product-search behaviour, or a regulatory crackdown on data use — would trigger exit condition five.
  • Political alignment reverses after a government transition. Amazon has cultivated close ties with the current US administration, and those ties have generated tangible economic benefits: government cloud contracts, favourable tax treatment, and a degree of protection from the most aggressive FTC enforcement postures. If the political environment shifts, Amazon's federal cloud business would face more competitive re-bid pressure and the FTC trial could accelerate. This is a cyclical rather than permanent risk, but it is worth monitoring across election cycles.

Gaps in the Picture

The valuation method in use — owner-earnings yield — applies figures extracted directly from Amazon's formal filings using the standardised financial reporting taxonomy that public companies use when filing with US regulators. Specifically, it uses operating cash flow minus the capital expenditure line as reported under US GAAP accounting rules. That figure ($132.8 billion in 2025) differs substantially from the "management free cash flow" number that Amazon highlights in its own investor communications ($11.2 billion in 2025), because Amazon's preferred definition subtracts not only the standard capital expenditure line but also spending on equipment finance leases and other infrastructure obligations. Neither figure is wrong; they measure different things. The standardised-data-based number likely overstates the cash truly available for shareholders by treating lease obligations as off-balance-sheet, while management's preferred number may temporarily understate normalised cash generation during a peak-spending cycle. Both are disclosed clearly; the divergence is a known feature of how Amazon finances its infrastructure rather than a quality concern.

The fair-value anchor (a 6.0% yield equating to $176 per share) was set based on a Buffett-style discounted-cash-flow framework anchoring to a 10% required return. The separate scenario analysis conducted by the growth and value research arms of this framework implies a base-case price target of $569 per share — more than three times the $176 anchor. This divergence has been flagged as a known inconsistency. The valuation band still functions correctly at current prices ($250 sitting within the $140–$421 range), but investors should be aware that the "fair value" printed here is a conservative floor rather than the central estimate produced by more detailed modelling.

Amazon does not separately disclose how much of its capital spending is for maintaining existing assets versus adding new capacity. The owner-earnings calculations in this framework use depreciation as a proxy for maintenance spending, which is a traceable but imperfect approximation. For a company doubling its data-centre capacity annually, the true maintenance fraction of spending is likely substantially lower than the depreciation charge, which means the underlying earnings power is probably higher than the conservative owner-earnings figure suggests.
