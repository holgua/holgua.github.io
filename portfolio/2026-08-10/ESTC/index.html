The Bottom Line

Data as of 2026-08-10 · Latest filing 2026-06-08.

The bet on Elastic — that its search-and-observability platform creates switching costs deep enough to keep customers and grow them — is working. The share price at $62 sits in the lower half of the hold band, deeply below fair value at $101. The long-run expected return at this price is about 20% a year — an exceptional equity premium above the risk-free rate. The one condition worth watching is whether stock-based pay, which consumed 17% of revenue in the latest financial year, stays below its 18% threshold as revenue growth moderates.

How the Score Adds Up

Elastic scores 77.24 out of 100, ranking first among 20 active holdings in the portfolio — the only holding where the probability-weighted return clears the hurdle rate by this margin.

score   = quality-weight × (expected return − fx/tax drag − hurdle rate)   = 1.00 × (83.60% − 1.46% − 4.91%)   = 1.00 × +77.23%   = 77.24

Moat weight: 1.00. Elastic sits in the middle moat tier — the weight is one, so it leaves the score unchanged. The case for a moat rests on production-embedded pipelines: once an organisation's log analytics, application monitoring, or security tooling is built on Elasticsearch at scale, migrating it elsewhere requires re-ingesting petabytes of data, rebuilding alerting logic, and retraining operations teams. The 112% net expansion rate (customers spending more each year than the year before) is the primary empirical evidence that this lock-in is real. What keeps the tier from being the highest is the existence of the AWS OpenSearch fork, now maintained under the Linux Foundation with over 1,400 contributors, which puts a ceiling on how much pricing power Elastic can exert over self-managed deployments.

Expected return: 83.60%. This figure is the probability-weighted average across the three scenarios — bear, base, and bull. The arithmetic is: 20% probability × bear return of +2.2%, plus 55% probability × base return of +50.1%, plus 25% probability × bull return of +222.6%, which comes to about +83.6%. The spread is wide by design: a mature, all-clear outcome and a serious deterioration outcome coexist, and the weighting between them is the central judgement call.

Hurdle rate: 4.91%. This is the minimum return required to justify holding the position rather than simply owning a portfolio of inflation-linked German government bonds. It is constructed from three pieces: 0.91% real yield on those linker bonds, 2.00% inflation, and 2.00% as a premium for taking company-specific risk instead of holding a government bond. An additional 1.46% is deducted to account for currency drag (the holding is priced in US dollars inside an otherwise euro-denominated portfolio). A return that clears 4.91% after that drag is doing what it is supposed to do.

Elastic's score of 77.24 reflects a position where the base case alone — no bull-case outcome needed — clears the hurdle rate by roughly 45 percentage points.

Recent Development

Elastic's annual report for the financial year ended 2026-04-30 confirmed every measurable exit condition clear. The most important numbers in order: the net expansion rate held at 112% (the threshold below which the switching-cost thesis would be questioned is 105%, and two consecutive quarters below that level are required before the exit condition fires); Elastic Cloud revenue grew 22% across the full year to $837.3M, reaching 48% of total revenue; and the free cash flow margin came in at 18.5% on $321.8M of cash generated, comfortably above the 14% floor.

GAAP profitability — actual accounting profit rather than the adjusted figures the company prefers to highlight — is not yet positive. The operating loss narrowed 39% in the year, from $54.9M to $33.5M, putting the margin at −1.9%. At a roughly 180-basis-point annual improvement pace, positive GAAP operating margins by 2028 remain within reach, though the trajectory leaves no room for a bad year. Two small acquisitions in the period — an AI-embedding specialist called Jina AI for $43.4M and a Kubernetes cost-management tool called Keep for $10.9M — both fell well within the $500M acquisition-discipline threshold.

The one driver the annual report could not settle is the competitive gap against Datadog, the fastest-growing observability platform. The annual filing names Datadog as a competitor but provides no comparative growth data. External analysis places the gap at roughly 11 percentage points — Datadog grew at around 26% annually while Elastic grew at 17% — which is below the 15-percentage-point threshold that would trigger the competitive exit condition. For more on the market's view of this divergence, see Why the Market Sees It Differently. For the valuation picture this creates, see The Share Price.

About Elastic

Elastic N.V. builds and sells the Elasticsearch technology stack — a set of software tools that organisations use to search, monitor, and protect their data in real time. The three practical applications are: enterprise search (finding documents, products, or records across large datasets quickly); observability (watching whether software systems are running correctly, and diagnosing problems when they are not); and security operations (detecting threats and responding to incidents). All three run on the same underlying data layer, which means a customer who adopts Elasticsearch for one purpose can expand to another without rebuilding their data infrastructure from scratch.

Revenue is almost entirely from software subscriptions. As of the latest annual report, Elastic generated $1,739.3M in total revenue, of which $1,634.5M came from subscriptions and the remaining $104.9M from professional services. Within subscriptions, the split between on-premise software licences and Elastic's own cloud-hosted service matters for the growth story: cloud revenue — where Elastic hosts the infrastructure and charges per consumption — grew 22% in the year and now represents 48% of total subscription revenue, up from 43% two years earlier. Cloud revenue is stickier and tends to grow with usage, whereas self-managed licences are more vulnerable to the open-source alternatives.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that Elastic's production-embedded platform creates switching costs durable enough to keep customers expanding their usage over time, while the shift toward cloud hosting compounds revenue per customer and the margin structure improves as the business scales. Seven specific conditions must hold for this to remain true:

  1. Customer expansion rate above 105% for two consecutive quarters. Elastic measures net expansion rate — the ratio of this year's revenue from last year's customers to last year's revenue from those same customers. A rate above 100% means customers are spending more on average, which is the primary sign that the switching-cost lock-in is working.

Latest reading: approximately 112% as of 2026-04-30. Customer count grew from roughly 21,500 to 24,000 in the year; customers spending more than $100,000 annually grew 14% to over 1,720. The expansion rate has held well above the 105% threshold.

  1. Elastic Cloud annual growth above 10%. If the cloud migration stalls or reverses, it would suggest AWS OpenSearch or another open-source alternative is displacing Elastic on price. A full fiscal year below 10% cloud growth would confirm that displacement.

Latest reading: +22% for total Elastic Cloud to $837.3M as of 2026-04-30 (annual Elastic Cloud specifically grew 28%). The exit threshold of 10% was cleared by a wide margin.

  1. Free cash flow margin above 14% on a full-year view. This floor defines the minimum cash-generating capability the business must maintain to remain viable as a standalone entity. Free cash flow is operating cash receipts minus the cash spent on property and equipment — a measure of cash the business actually produces before paying for acquisitions or returning capital.

Latest reading: 18.5% as of 2026-04-30 ($321.8M on $1,739.3M revenue). The margin improved roughly 420 basis points year-on-year, leaving 450 basis points of headroom above the 14% floor.

  1. Stock-based pay below 18% of revenue for two consecutive years. Software companies pay employees partly in shares rather than cash, which is a real economic cost even though it does not reduce cash flow. When this pay exceeds 18% of revenue for two consecutive years, it signals that compensation discipline has reversed.

Latest reading: 17.16% of revenue as of 2026-04-30 ($298.4M of share-based pay on $1,739.3M revenue). This is the second consecutive year below 18%, clearing exit condition 4. The buffer is thin at 84 basis points and depends on revenue continuing to grow faster than absolute pay levels.

  1. No acquisition above $500M without disclosed cash-flow accretion within three years. Large acquisitions at high prices can destroy capital even when the strategic logic is sound. This condition is objectively measurable from official filings — any deal above $500M at more than eight times the target's annual revenue must come with a disclosed path to positive cash-flow contribution within three years.

Latest reading: two acquisitions in the year ended 2026-04-30. Jina AI (an AI embedding and re-ranking specialist) acquired for $43.4M in October 2025; Keep (a Kubernetes cost-management tool) acquired for $10.9M in May 2025. Both are well below the $500M threshold.

  1. Positive accounting operating margin by 2028. The gap between Elastic's cash generation and its accounting losses is large because share-based pay is counted as an operating expense under accounting rules. The accounting operating loss has been narrowing; exit condition 6 requires it to turn positive by the financial year ending April 2028.

Latest reading: −1.9% accounting operating margin as of 2026-04-30 (improved from −3.7% the previous year). The 39% narrowing of the absolute loss in one year keeps the 2028 target within reach, but the trajectory requires continued revenue growth.

  1. Datadog growth gap below 15 percentage points for two consecutive years, and Elastic remains a Gartner Observability leader. This condition requires both triggers simultaneously — the growth gap alone is insufficient. Datadog is the fastest-growing observability platform and the primary competitive threat in Elastic's most contested segment.

Latest reading: external analysis as of 2026-04-30 places the Datadog growth gap at approximately 11 percentage points (Datadog at roughly 26% annual growth, Elastic at 17%). The 15-percentage-point threshold has not been crossed. Elastic confirmed its Gartner Observability Leader status in 2026. Neither arm of exit condition 7 is triggered.

The Share Price

At $62, the share price is deeply below what the business appears to be worth. The valuation method used here translates cash generation into a yield — dividing the $321.8M in annual free cash flow by the total market value of all shares. At $62, that yield is approximately 4.97%. The rule says a yield above 5.50% puts the share in buy territory; a yield below 2.20% puts it in trim territory. Today's 4.97% sits in the hold band, in the lower half — closer to the buy edge than to the fair-value midpoint, which corresponds to a yield of 3.04% and a share price of roughly $101.

That $101 fair value anchors to a discounted cash flow model — a calculation that asks what today's cash flows are worth if they grow at 15% annually for five years and then stabilise, discounted back to today at a 10% rate. The market price at $62 implies the market expects a much lower growth rate, approximately 6.4% annually, over the long run.

One note for context: a separate analysis using peer company multiples and Elastic's own trading history produces a higher central fair value of roughly $215. Both methods agree the stock is below fair value; they disagree significantly on the magnitude. The more conservative $101 anchor governs the standing orders in The Standing Orders and the TL;DR band above.

Why the Market Sees It Differently

The price says the business will earn less over time. The assessment here says the opposite. The gap is striking — the market price of $62 sits 38.8% below the $101 fair value estimate, implying the market believes Elastic's free cash flow will grow at roughly 6.4% annually over the long run, against the base-case assumption of 15%.

Three explanations are consistent with the evidence. First, the bear scenario — priced at roughly 20% probability — implies Elastic's cash flow grows at only 9% annually, which by itself drags the probability-weighted fair value below $101; a market that weights the bear case more heavily than the base case would rationally price the stock closer to $62. Second, the AWS OpenSearch fork, now maintained by over 1,400 outside contributors under the Linux Foundation, may be absorbing more self-managed renewal business than the annual filing discloses — the 22% cloud growth figure is disclosed, but the self-managed renewal rate is not separately broken out in a way that would confirm or deny this. Third, the broad compression of price multiples for software companies whose accounting losses remain negative has pushed down valuations across the sector, regardless of underlying cash generation.

The filing evidence most directly relevant to the first hypothesis — that the bear case deserves more than 20% weight — would be a deceleration in cloud growth or a drop in the net expansion rate. Neither materialised in the latest annual report. Cloud grew 22% and the expansion rate held at 112%. On the current evidence, the market's implied growth expectation of 6.4% is not supported by the operating data. That could change; the next quarterly report, covering the three months ending July 2026, is the nearest point at which either hypothesis could be confirmed or challenged.

The Standing Orders

Two alerts. When the share price falls to $101, the business is trading at fair value — a shift in conditions or expectations at that level warrants attention. When the share price falls to $56, the share enters buy territory under the cash-flow yield rule.

Standing orders. If the thesis is intact — all seven conditions above holding — and the price reaches or falls below $56, the standing order is to add to the position, in staged tranches of no more than 50% of the intended position in any single move. The caution about staged entry reflects the bear case: a permanent loss of roughly 49% from current prices, at 20% probability, is large enough to require thesis confirmation between tranches rather than a single deployment.

If the thesis is intact and the price rises to $140, the order is to trim 25% of the position. At $140, the cash-flow yield falls to 2.20% — the trim edge — and the share has moved roughly 35% above the $101 fair-value anchor; harvesting some of that premium while keeping the core position is the rule at that level.

No exit-price rule is currently set below $140 on the valuation dimension alone. A separate full-exit order exists if any of the seven exit conditions fire, independent of price.

What's Wobbling

The one driver that warrants monitoring rather than clean reassurance is stock-based pay. At 17.16% of revenue in the latest year, it cleared the 18% threshold — but only by 84 basis points, the thinnest margin in the tracked history. The pay level itself grew 16% in absolute terms; what kept the percentage declining is that revenue grew faster, at 17%. If revenue growth moderates meaningfully in one year while pay levels do not adjust downward, this threshold could be crossed without any change in the underlying compensation structure. The exit condition requires two consecutive fiscal years above 18% before it fires, which provides one year of warning, but the margin for error is now narrow.

The Datadog competitive gap is the other active watch item, even though it did not cross any threshold in the latest annual filing. The annual report is silent on comparative growth figures — the gap estimate of roughly 11 percentage points comes from external analysis rather than disclosed data. The condition that matters is two consecutive fiscal years of a gap exceeding 15 percentage points, combined with a Gartner Observability ranking below Leader status. On current evidence neither arm is triggered. But the gap is directionally moving in the wrong direction, and the external data on which the assessment depends is less certain than the internally disclosed drivers.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • AWS OpenSearch closes the capability gap. The Linux Foundation fork now has over 1,400 contributors and is receiving active investment from Amazon Web Services. If it reaches feature parity with Elastic Cloud on key observability and vector-search capabilities, Elastic's self-managed renewal base faces a direct price-competitive alternative. The annual filing discloses cloud growth at 22% but does not separately report self-managed renewal dynamics, so this risk is only partially observable.
  • The bear scenario materialises. In the bear case, free cash flow grows at 9% rather than 15%, producing a value per share of roughly $29 — a permanent loss of approximately 49% from today's price. This is not a remote tail; it carries 20% probability in the scenario model. The primary triggers would be Elastic Cloud growth decelerating sharply or the net expansion rate falling below 105%.
  • AI infrastructure spend consolidates on hyperscaler-native tools. Enterprises building AI-search and observability infrastructure may choose the managed AI services from their primary cloud provider — Microsoft Azure AI Search, Google Vertex AI Search, or Amazon Kendra — over Elastic's offering. The AI-led consolidation risk is structural rather than cyclical and would affect the Search & AI segment first.
  • Revenue growth slows before accounting profitability is achieved. The path to a positive accounting operating margin by 2028 depends on revenue growing faster than total operating costs. A single year of meaningfully lower revenue growth, combined with a rigid cost structure from multi-year employment contracts and stock-based pay, could push the 2028 milestone out and force a reassessment of exit condition 6.
  • A large acquisition at an unfavourable price. Management has $1.37 billion in liquid assets and $575 million in senior notes due in 2029. The acquisition-discipline condition requires any deal above $500 million to come with a disclosed accretion timeline. A deal that technically meets the letter of this condition while destroying economic value — by using an inflated revenue multiple or overstating synergies — would be difficult to detect in real time from public filings alone.

The one-percentage-point year-on-year movement is therefore an estimate rather than a directly disclosed comparison.

The competitive-position driver — specifically Datadog's revenue growth rate and Elastic's Gartner Observability ranking — cannot be read from Elastic's own filings. The annual report names Datadog as a competitor but provides no comparative growth data. The Rules

If all seven conditions in The Case for the Bet are intact and the price is within the hold band, hold the position without action. Monitor the net expansion rate and cloud revenue growth at each quarterly earnings release; confirm the stock-based-pay trajectory at each annual filing.

If one or more drivers are weakening — for example, the net expansion rate trending toward 107% or cloud growth decelerating below 15% for two consecutive quarters — do not add to the position. Require two consecutive quarters of deterioration before treating a signal as a structural break rather than noise. Re-run the thesis validation before any sizing decision.

If any of the seven written exit conditions fires — the net expansion rate falling below 105% for two consecutive quarters; cloud growth below 10% for a full year; free cash flow margin below 14%; stock-based pay above 18% of revenue for two consecutive years; an acquisition above $500M at more than 8× revenue without a disclosed accretion timeline; the accounting operating margin not positive by 2028; or the Datadog growth gap exceeding 15 percentage points for two consecutive years while Elastic falls below Gartner Observability Leader status — exit the full position within 30 days. No averaging down on a thesis break.

If the share price falls to the buy edge (cash-flow yield above 5.50%, approximately $56 per share on the current earnings basis), and the thesis is intact, add to the position in tranches. No more than 50% of the intended full position in a single move, given the bear-case severity of roughly −49% at 20% probability. Confirm the thesis is intact between tranches before deploying further capital.

If the share price rises to the trim edge (cash-flow yield below 2.20%, approximately $140 per share), trim 25% of the position. The premium above fair value at that level warrants harvesting; the core position is kept for further compounding if the base case plays out.

If the share price rises to approximately $200 per share (cash-flow yield below 1.50%), exit the entire remaining position. At that price, the market would be pricing the bull-case outcome in full, and the asymmetry of return would have inverted. No re-entry within 90 days to guard against anchoring back to the prior cost basis.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
