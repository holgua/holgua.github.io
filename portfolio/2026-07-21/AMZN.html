Amazon: The Bet Still Works

The Bottom Line

Data as of 2026-07-20 · Latest filing 2026-04-30.

The bet is working. Amazon's cloud computing division (AWS) posted 28% revenue growth in the first quarter of 2026, the advertising business grew 24%, and the international retail segment turned durably profitable — all three thesis conditions confirming that the central flywheel is intact. At $250, the share price sits in the upper half of the hold band, well above fair value by the yield-based measure used here, earning a healthy equity premium. The one condition to watch is the pace of capital spending: Amazon committed roughly $54.8 billion in new equipment in a single quarter, and whether that investment returns adequate earnings by 2027–2028 remains the load-bearing open question.

Recent Development

The first quarter of 2026 was the filing window that mattered most for the central bet, and the verdict is straightforwardly positive on the revenue side. AWS grew 28.4% year on year to $37.6 billion in the three months ending in March 2026, well above the thesis expectation of 18–20% sustained growth and miles from the 15% exit threshold that would arm the first written exit condition. The committed customer backlog reached $364 billion — a five-and-a-half-year pipeline of contracted revenue — which makes a sudden deceleration in the next two quarters the wrong frame for thinking about risk.

The supporting thesis legs also advanced. Advertising services grew 23.9% year on year to $17.2 billion, and the international retail segment posted operating income of $1.4 billion at a 3.58% margin, up from 3.03% a year earlier and from a loss position two years ago. The three-engine structure the thesis describes — cloud, advertising, and international retail — is producing in all three cylinders simultaneously.

The complication is the capital spending line. Amazon reported $54.8 billion in net additions to property, plant, and equipment in the single quarter, a 99.5% increase year on year, with the bulk flowing into AWS infrastructure. Amazon's own preferred measure of free cash flow collapsed to $1.2 billion on a trailing twelve-month basis because capex is absorbing nearly all operating cash generation. The thesis flag on this point — exit condition (4), which watches whether capital spending remains above $120 billion in both 2027 and 2028 without commensurate AWS revenue acceleration — has not triggered, but the clock is running. For how the share price reads this set of facts, see below.

About Amazon

Amazon makes its money in three distinct businesses that share infrastructure and a customer base but operate on very different economics. The largest by revenue is North American retail — the online shop and fulfilment network where goods are sold directly by Amazon or by third-party merchants who pay for listing, shipping, and advertising. International retail runs the same model across Europe, Asia, and Latin America, and turned operationally profitable on a sustained basis in 2025. These two segments together account for the majority of Amazon's $717 billion in annual revenue.

The engine that generates most of the profit is Amazon Web Services — the cloud computing platform that rents computing power, storage, and software tools to businesses and governments worldwide. AWS earned an operating profit of 35% in the 2025 financial year on roughly $107 billion of annual revenue, and that margin pool funds the continued investment in the retail and logistics network that would otherwise be financially difficult to sustain at scale. The third profit engine is advertising: brands pay Amazon to place sponsored products in front of shoppers who are already in a buying frame of mind, generating $68.6 billion in revenue in 2025 at margins closer to the software business than the logistics one.

The three businesses reinforce each other through Prime membership, which sits at the centre as a loyalty subscription — it subsidises fast delivery, bundles video streaming and other benefits, and keeps customers shopping on Amazon rather than elsewhere. The more Prime customers shop, the more useful the advertising platform becomes; the more the advertising platform earns, the more Amazon can invest in delivery speed; the faster delivery becomes, the more customers choose Prime. AWS provides the computing infrastructure for all of it.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that Amazon's two high-margin compounding engines — cloud computing and advertising — are not yet close to their earnings ceiling, and that the capital spending currently suppressing cash flow will prove to have been the right investment at the right time. Four specific conditions must keep holding:

  1. AWS revenue growth stays above 15% in every twelve-month window. Growth below that level for two consecutive quarters would signal that the cloud migration thesis has stalled — that AI workloads are not arriving fast enough to justify the infrastructure build, or that competitors have captured enough share to bend the growth curve downward. The exit threshold is conservative by design: it sits well below the 18–20% thesis expectation and is meant to catch structural deceleration, not a single soft quarter.

Latest reading: 28.4% year-on-year growth in the three months ending 2026-03-31, with $364 billion in committed long-term customer backlog and a weighted-average remaining contract life of 5.5 years. Exit condition (1) is not remotely approached.

  1. AWS operating margin stays above 28% in every twelve-month window. An AWS margin below 28% for two consecutive quarters — absent a clearly explained one-off charge — would signal that reinvestment economics have broken: that the cost of building and running the infrastructure exceeds what the business can earn from it at scale.

Latest reading: 37.7% in the three months ending 2026-03-31, down roughly 180 basis points (that is, 1.8 percentage points) from the 39.5% posted in the same period a year earlier. The compression reflects infrastructure spending, not pricing pressure or volume loss. The exit threshold of 28% is 970 basis points below the current reading. Exit condition (2) is not triggered.

  1. Advertising revenue keeps growing. A deceleration to single-digit growth for two consecutive years would signal that the third compounding engine — the intent-based advertising platform built on Amazon's shopping audience — has reached saturation or lost competitiveness to social media alternatives.

Latest reading: $17.2 billion in the three months ending 2026-03-31, up 23.9% year on year. The growth rate is well above the single-digit warning level, and the advertising business is growing faster than the overall company. Exit condition (5) is not approached.

  1. Capital spending normalises relative to AWS revenue by 2027–2028. If consolidated capital spending remains above $120 billion in both 2027 and 2028 without a matching acceleration in AWS revenue, that would signal that the AI infrastructure investment has overshot demand — that Amazon built capacity the market does not yet need and may not need at the pace assumed.

Latest reading: Net property, plant, and equipment additions of $54.8 billion in the three months ending 2026-03-31, a 99.5% increase year on year. Management stated that capital spending will continue to increase in 2026. The condition being watched is the 2027–2028 period, not the current quarter; it is not triggered today but requires close monitoring through 2026 results.

The Share Price

At $250, Amazon's share price sits in the upper half of the hold band — above the fair-value anchor implied by the yield method used here, but not yet above the trim line. In plain terms: the business is working well, but the market has already priced in much of the good news.

The yield measure works like a price-to-earnings ratio in reverse. The 6% yield that defines fair value corresponds to a share price of $176. At $250, the yield is 4.2% — still inside the hold band (the trim signal fires below 4.0%), but closer to the expensive edge than the cheap one.

The gap between $176 and $250 is genuinely striking and deserves transparency rather than smoothing over. The scenario-based valuation model — which discounts three-year forward cash flow projections under bear, base, and bull assumptions — produces a base-case fair value of $368, not $176. The two methods start from different premises: the yield method asks what a static, conservatively-measured owner earnings stream is worth at a 6% discount rate; the scenario model asks what the business is likely to be worth in three years given plausible growth outcomes. Neither is wrong. The yield method is the conservative anchor; the scenario model captures the growth optionality the market is evidently paying for. The mismatch between the two is itself the most useful piece of information: at $250, the price is above the conservative anchor but well below the growth-optionality estimate. The trim signal uses the conservative anchor.

Why the Market Sees It Differently

The market is pricing the business as if it will earn more, over time, than the yield-based fair-value calculation implies. The gap is 42% — at $250, the market sits 42% above our conservative fair-value estimate of $176.

In terms of growth assumptions, the gap is surprisingly small: our base growth rate is 8% per year; the implied growth rate embedded in the current price is 8.9%. A 0.9 percentage-point difference in expected growth is not a dramatic disagreement about the business's future. What the gap really reflects is a difference in how those growth assumptions are capitalised — in what multiple the market is willing to apply to current earnings.

Three hypotheses explain a market willing to pay a premium over the conservative yield anchor. The first is that the market regards Amazon's current owner earnings as a trough figure during the investment cycle, not a run-rate, and expects cash flow per share to be materially higher once the capital spending cycle normalises in 2027–2028. The second is that the market assigns a lower required return to Amazon than the conservative 6% yield threshold — treating it more like a bond-plus-growth vehicle than a typical equity. The third is that the market attributes premium value to optionality in Kuiper satellite internet, healthcare, and other emerging business lines not yet visible in the yield measure.

The first hypothesis has the most support from the current filing. Q1 2026 consolidated revenue grew 16.6% year on year, the business is clearly investing for a larger future, and historical investment cycles at Amazon have tended to generate above-cost returns when the build phase completed. The second and third hypotheses are less testable from the data. For the gap to close toward our estimate, either the share price would need to fall or the business would need to generate owner earnings large enough to push the yield above 6% at the current price — which would require roughly $150 billion in owner earnings at today's market capitalisation, compared to $113 billion today.

What's Wobbling

Two drift warnings appear in the current re-validation, both informational rather than action-triggering.

The first concerns the relationship between the two valuation methods described in the share price section. The scenario-based fair-value model produces a base-case anchor of $465 — well above both the yield-based estimate ($176) and the current price ($250). This divergence suggests the conservative yield threshold may be calibrated to a framework that underweights Amazon's growth trajectory. The analyst should decide whether the 6.0% yield threshold remains the right anchor or whether the scenario model's output should inform a threshold adjustment. The thesis re-validation flags this as a calibration question, not an exit trigger.

The second wobble is the AWS margin compression. At 37.7% in the first quarter of 2026, down roughly 180 basis points from a year earlier, AWS margin is still far above the 28% exit threshold — but the direction of travel bears watching. The compression is explicitly attributed to infrastructure investment rather than pricing pressure, which is the more benign explanation. If it persists for another two consecutive quarters at a level approaching 30%, the second written exit condition begins to close in.

The capital spending trajectory is the background condition behind both wobbles. Everything the thesis is counting on — margin normalisation, AWS return on capital, the compounding flywheel — depends on the $200 billion-plus investment cycle generating adequate returns by 2027–2028. The filing confirms it is underway; the outcome is not yet confirmed.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • AWS growth slows sharply and durably. If Azure AI Studio and Google's cloud platform capture a meaningfully larger share of AI-native workloads, AWS's 28% growth rate could decelerate to the 12% range within two years. At that rate, combined with a market multiple contraction, the share would trade close to the bear-case estimate of $170 — a loss of roughly 32% from today's price.
  • The $200 billion capital commitment generates inadequate returns. Amazon has committed more capital to AI infrastructure than its operating cash flow can cover in a single year. If by 2028 the return on that investment is below the cost of capital, the earnings case for the current share price collapses — not because the business is bad, but because the price assumed a better outcome.
  • A court-ordered structural separation of the marketplace. The Federal Trade Commission's monopoly case against Amazon is scheduled for trial in 2027. An order requiring Amazon to choose between operating a marketplace and competing in it with its own retail business would permanently impair the Prime flywheel — the self-reinforcing loop between third-party sellers, fast delivery, and subscriber loyalty that funds the advertising platform.
  • Advertising saturates or loses share. The advertising business currently grows at 22–24% per year. If it decelerates to single digits for two consecutive years — because shopping intent migrates to TikTok Shop or social media platforms, or because tariff disruption reduces the Chinese seller cohort that relies on Amazon advertising — the third compounding engine stalls. This fires exit condition (5).
  • The tariff and de-minimis policy shift permanently disrupts the third-party marketplace. A significant fraction of Amazon's marketplace goods and third-party advertising revenue comes from Chinese sellers who rely on low-cost cross-border shipping. Sustained tariff increases or the elimination of de-minimis customs exemptions could reduce this seller cohort materially, compressing marketplace economics in a way that takes years to reverse.

Gaps in the Picture

The owner earnings figure used in the valuation yield ($113 billion for the 2025 financial year) is computed as operating cash flow minus capital expenditures as reported in Amazon's standardised financial filings. This definition excludes operating and finance lease obligations, which are a major portion of Amazon's actual capital deployment. Amazon's own preferred measure of free cash flow — which includes lease payments — was $1.2 billion on a trailing twelve-month basis at the end of the first quarter of 2026. Neither figure is the "true" owner earnings: the standardised filing figure overstates near-term cash availability; the management-definition figure understates the underlying earnings power of the business by treating growth investment as maintenance spending. Readers should interpret the valuation band positions with this tension in mind.

The fair-value central estimate from the separate scenario-based model ($465) diverges from the yield-based fair-value anchor ($176) by a wide margin. Both are internally consistent; they answer different questions. The scenario model asks what the business is worth if growth continues; the yield method asks what the business is worth if it stopped growing. The analyst has not yet resolved which framework should govern the trim threshold. Until that decision is made, the $263 trim edge should be read as a conservative signal, not a ceiling.

The Rules

If the thesis is confirmed — AWS growing above 15%, margin above 28%, advertising compounding, no exit condition triggered — hold without action and continue monitoring quarterly.

If the thesis is weakening — one driver deteriorating without yet triggering a written exit condition — do not add to the position. Re-run the validation on the specific deteriorating driver after the next two quarterly filings before deciding. A single soft quarter is not sufficient; two consecutive quarters of a driver moving in the wrong direction is the signal.

If the thesis is broken — any written exit condition has fired — exit the full position within 30 days. No averaging down. AWS revenue or margin exits require two consecutive quarters of breach before action; an order imposing a structural business separation or an unexpected change of auditor is an immediate exit with no waiting period.

If the share price falls to or below $140 — the yield rises to 7.5% or above — and the thesis is confirmed intact with AWS growth at or above 18%, add to the position in tranches over one to two quarters. Do not add if any exit condition is close to triggering.

If the share price rises to or above $263 — the yield falls below 4.0% — trim 25–33% of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding. Re-evaluate the remaining position size after the trim.

If the share price rises to or above $421 — the yield falls below 3.0% — exit the full position. At that level the market is pricing in roughly 11 years of base-case compounding delivered immediately, and the implied forward return falls below the long-term equity premium. Redeploy proceeds at the next buy-edge crossing or into a more attractively priced alternative. No re-entry within 90 days.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
