Recent Development

The FY2025 full-year results, filed in February 2026, confirmed that Unilever's underlying business is in better shape than its headline revenue number suggests. Reported revenue fell 3.8% to EUR 50.5 billion compared with the year before — but the decline is structural, not competitive. It reflects the exit of the Ice Cream division (the Magnum and Wall's brands), which completed its separation in December 2025. The organic business, measured by the "underlying sales growth" metric that strips out portfolio changes, accelerated through the year: Unilever's Power Brands grew underlying sales at 4.3% across the full year and at 5.0% in the three months ending March 2026, with volume contributing 4.0 percentage points of that growth — the most encouraging signal, because volume-led growth is more durable than price-led growth.

The structural health of the business also improved. Operating profit margin reached 17.9% — the highest in at least five years — and the return earned on invested capital rose to 14.7%, comfortably above the estimated cost of capital of 8–9%. Owner earnings (operating cash flow minus an estimate of the capital the business needs to maintain its competitive position) came in at EUR 6.85 billion, EUR 1.35 billion above the exit floor of EUR 5.5 billion written into the rules. The accruals ratio — a measure of whether reported profits are backed by actual cash — stands at +0.016, close to zero and consistent with a long track record of genuine cash conversion.

The one reading that remains mixed is balance-sheet leverage. Net debt stands at approximately USD 23.3 billion, producing a debt-to-equity ratio of around 161%. The thesis anticipated this: the McCormick Foods transaction — under which Unilever agreed in March 2026 to sell the Hellmann's, Knorr, and Marmite brands for USD 44.8 billion — provides a clear deleveraging path once the deal closes, expected in mid-2027. The detail that matters for the Bottom Line is that the cash-flow-to-revenue ratio sits at 16.5%, well above the 12% watch level at which leverage would begin to constrain the business.

About Unilever

Unilever is one of the world's largest makers of everyday consumer goods — the products that sit in bathroom cabinets, kitchen cupboards, and laundry rooms in households across 190 countries. Its portfolio spans personal care (Dove soap and shampoo, Axe and Lynx deodorants, TRESemmé hair products), nutrition (Hellmann's mayonnaise, Knorr soups and stock cubes, Marmite yeast extract, until its sale), beauty and wellness (Dermalogica, Hourglass, Pond's skincare), and home care (Persil and Omo laundry detergents, Domestos cleaners). The business earns money by selling branded goods through supermarkets, pharmacies, convenience stores, and — increasingly — direct digital channels, at a price premium over unbranded alternatives that reflects decades of consumer trust built through advertising and consistent product quality.

The strategic direction under chief executive Fernando Fernandez (in post from March 2025, continuing the Growth Action Plan launched in 2023 by his predecessor Hein Schumacher) is to concentrate Unilever's capital and management attention on fewer, stronger brands in higher-margin categories. The Ice Cream division — home to Magnum, Ben & Jerry's, and Wall's — completed its spin-off as an independent company in December 2025. The sale of the Nutrition portfolio (Hellmann's, Knorr, Marmite) to a joint venture with McCormick & Company was signed in March 2026 and, pending regulatory clearance, is expected to close in mid-2027. What remains after those transactions is a business focused on Beauty & Wellbeing, Personal Care, and Home Care — all categories where Unilever holds a structural advantage through its brand portfolio and distribution network, and where margins are meaningfully higher than in the departed segments.

The Case for the Bet

The bet rests on a proposition: a business with durable brand advantages and a distribution network built over decades, earning a return on capital well above its cost of capital, compounding steadily across emerging markets where the middle class is growing, and simplifying its portfolio toward its strongest segments. The conditions that must keep holding for the bet to work are:

  1. Operating profit margin stays above 14% for every fiscal year. This is the floor below which the brand advantage would have to be considered damaged, not temporarily under pressure. Five consecutive years between 16.6% and 17.9%, including the most severe commodity cost inflation in a decade, establishes that the moat is real and durable. Two consecutive years below 14% would signal something structural, not cyclical.

Latest reading: 17.9% in the year ending 31 December 2025 — a five-year high. Operating profit came in at EUR 9.04 billion on revenue of EUR 50.5 billion, up from 16.8% the year before. No concern.

  1. Return on invested capital stays above 10%. The business earns a meaningful spread over its estimated cost of capital (8–9%) — at 14.7% in FY2025, that spread is about 5–6 percentage points. Two consecutive years below 10% would mean brand equity is no longer generating economic value: the business would be earning less than it costs to finance.

Latest reading: 14.7% in FY2025, recovered from prior years' commodity-cost pressure. Comfortably above threshold.

  1. Owner earnings stay above EUR 5.5 billion for every fiscal year. Owner earnings — operating cash flow (the cash the business generates before capital spending) minus an estimate of the capital needed to maintain the competitive position (approximately EUR 1.5 billion, around 3% of revenue) — is the clearest measure of what the business actually produces for its owners after keeping the machinery running. EUR 5.5 billion is the bear-case floor: below it for two consecutive years, and the thesis income assumption has broken.

Latest reading: EUR 6.85 billion in FY2025 (operating cash flow EUR 8.35 billion minus estimated maintenance capital EUR 1.5 billion). EUR 1.35 billion above the exit floor. The three-year average is approximately EUR 7.6 billion — consistent with the valuation basis used.

  1. Goodwill write-downs stay within limits. Goodwill — the premium paid over book value in acquisitions, sitting on the balance sheet as an asset — can be written down when management concludes a past acquisition has not worked out. A write-down of more than EUR 3 billion in any single year, without a concurrent disposal announcement, would signal that management is belatedly recognising past mistakes under pressure.

Latest reading: Goodwill fell EUR 4.6 billion in FY2025, from EUR 22.3 billion to EUR 17.7 billion — a large number, but explained entirely by the Ice Cream separation, which was announced and is a deliberate strategic exit rather than a surprise impairment. The carve-out condition is met; exit condition 4 has not fired.

  1. Earnings quality stays clean. The accruals ratio — computed as reported net income minus operating cash flow, divided by total assets — measures whether profits are backed by real cash flows or by accounting judgements. A ratio above +0.10 for two consecutive years would signal a deterioration in earnings quality.

Latest reading: +0.016 in FY2025, close to zero and in line with a long track record (FY2024: -0.047; FY2023: -0.039). No concern.

  1. The McCormick Foods transaction completes close to its agreed terms. A collapse of the deal, or a repricing of more than 20% downward from the agreed USD 44.8 billion, would signal execution failure and leave capital stranded in the Nutrition brands at a lower strategic quality than the continuing portfolio.

Latest reading: The transaction was signed in March 2026. Regulatory review by the US Federal Trade Commission was under way as of July 2026, with no enforcement action reported. Tracking on schedule.

  1. Power Brand underlying sales growth stays above 1.5% per period. "Underlying sales growth" strips out the effect of acquisitions, disposals, and currency moves to show how the core branded business is growing. The 1.5% floor is the minimum at which the price premium over unbranded alternatives can be considered intact. Two consecutive periods below that level would suggest that private-label competition is eroding the brand moat faster than emerging-market growth can compensate.

Latest reading: Power Brand underlying sales growth of 4.3% for the full year ending December 2025, accelerating to 5.0% in the three months ending March 2026 (with volume contributing 4.0 percentage points). Comfortably above the exit threshold in all recorded periods.

The Share Price

Unilever's shares trade at 4,671p. The earnings yield on normalised annual profits — the ratio of what the business produces in cash to the market's total price for the whole enterprise — sits at 6.43%. The fair-value yield, the level at which the business is priced neither cheaply nor expensively relative to what it earns, is 6.5%. The current reading of 6.43% is essentially at that level: the share price sits in the middle of the hold band, approximately at fair value.

The practical implication is that the business is priced to deliver approximately what it earns — roughly 10% a year including assumed steady-state growth — without any particular margin of safety. At today's price, fair value is being paid for a high-quality business. There is no discount to exploit and no premium to harvest. The numbers confirm the thesis is intact; they offer no catalyst in either direction.

What's Wobbling

The only driver that is not fully confirming the thesis is balance-sheet leverage. Net debt stands at approximately USD 23.3 billion, which puts the ratio of debt to equity at around 161% — elevated by any standard. The cash-flow-to-revenue ratio of 16.5% is still comfortably above the 12% watch level set in the rules, so the leverage has not yet constrained the business's ability to operate. But until the McCormick Foods transaction closes and the proceeds reduce the debt pile, leverage remains a source of vulnerability: a prolonged economic downturn, an unexpected regulatory block on the transaction, or a sharp rise in refinancing costs could tighten the constraint materially.

Importantly, elevated leverage was anticipated when the thesis was written — it is not a new development or a surprise. The transaction pipeline was the planned deleveraging mechanism. The relevant question for the Case for the Bet is whether any of the seven exit conditions has moved closer to being triggered. As of the latest filing, not one has. The leverage reading is a reason to watch, not a reason to act.

There is also a secondary wobble in the valuation data: the fair-value estimate produced by the peer-and-history multiple model (a separate analytical approach that looks at what the market has historically been willing to pay per unit of earnings or book value) implies an owner-earnings yield of around 4.2%, compared with the 6.5% figure used as the primary valuation anchor. The two methods disagree by 35 percentage points on what counts as fair value. The primary method — yield on owner earnings — is authoritative for the standing orders; the divergence between methods is a signal that the valuation picture is more uncertain than normal and deserves investigation at the next annual cycle.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Emerging-market currency collapse. Approximately 60% of Unilever's revenue comes from emerging markets. A broad depreciation of the Indian rupee, Indonesian rupiah, or Brazilian real against the euro and pound translates directly into lower revenue and earnings in reporting currency, even if the underlying businesses are healthy. The bear case models a 15% basket depreciation; a sharper or more sustained move would push owner earnings below the exit floor faster than the three-year window used in the scenario analysis.
  • Private-label brands taking sustained share in Europe. The core risk to the brand moat is that supermarket own-label products — cheaper alternatives to Dove, Hellmann's, and Persil — gain enough shelf space and consumer acceptance that Unilever's pricing premium erodes permanently. A sustained reading of Power Brand underlying sales growth below 1.5% would confirm that the moat is losing ground.
  • Operating margin compression to 15.5%. The bear case assumes margins fall from their current 17.9% to 15.5%, driven by a combination of commodity cost pressure (raw materials account for the majority of cost of goods sold), pricing limitations in a private-label-competitive environment, and investment in brand support. At 15.5%, the business is still profitable but is no longer earning a convincing spread over its cost of capital.
  • McCormick transaction failure or repricing. A collapse of the deal, an antitrust block, or a forced repricing of more than 20% downward from the USD 44.8 billion agreed value would leave capital stranded in Nutrition brands, undermine the portfolio simplification strategy, and eliminate the primary planned deleveraging mechanism — leaving balance-sheet leverage elevated for longer than anticipated.
  • Regulatory or reputational action on food ingredients. The US regulatory environment for food products, particularly those classified as ultra-processed, has become more active. An enforcement action directly naming Unilever's brands, or a mandatory reformulation requirement on a high-volume product, would impose costs and potentially shrink the addressable market for the Nutrition segment before it is fully disposed of.

The Rules

The rules governing what happens under each combination of conditions and share price are set in advance and are not subject to revision based on short-term market moves. They read as follows:

  • If all conditions are holding and the price is within the normal band: hold the position. The primary expected return in the base case is the dividend yield of approximately 3.71%. The rules call for holding for income and for optionality on the portfolio simplification re-rating as the McCormick transaction closes.
  • If any condition begins to deteriorate without crossing an exit threshold: do not add to the position. Monitor the specific deteriorating driver for one additional quarter before reassessing. Key dates: quarterly trading statements (for underlying sales growth and margin commentary), the FY2025 annual filing (for return on capital, accruals ratio, goodwill), and continuous monitoring of McCormick regulatory progress and any US policy actions naming Unilever.
  • If any exit condition fires: exit the full position immediately on confirmation from filed data or a press release. Exit is unconditional on conditions 1 (operating margin), 2 (return on capital), and 7 (Power Brand underlying sales growth below 1.5%) — these signal permanent damage to the moat. No averaging down after an exit condition has been confirmed.
  • If the share price falls to 3,754p or below (owner-earnings yield at or above 8.0%): confirm all conditions are intact, then add in two tranches over one or two quarters. The additional constraint is that operating profit margin must be at or above 16% at the time of purchase — a falling price accompanied by margin deterioration changes the risk profile materially.
  • If the share price rises to 6,006p or above (owner-earnings yield at or below 5.0%): trim a quarter to a third of the position. The trim is mechanical — the price has reached a level where the yield no longer adequately compensates for the risk of holding a single company.
  • If the share price rises to 8,580p or above (owner-earnings yield at or below 4.5%): exit the full position. At that yield, the market is pricing in the bull scenario as near-certain; the forward risk-to-reward balance no longer justifies holding. No re-entry for at least 90 days.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
