About DT

Dynatrace sells observability software to large enterprises: the tools that tell engineers, in real time, what is happening across thousands of interdependent services, databases, cloud containers, and user sessions — and, increasingly, why. Its central product is a software agent, OneAgent, that deploys automatically across an organisation's technology stack and feeds everything it measures into a proprietary data lakehouse called Grail. On top of that lakehouse sits an AI-assisted query and alerting layer called Davis, which the company markets under the Dynatrace Platform Subscription licence.

The business model is subscription-based and almost entirely recurring. Customers sign annual or multi-year contracts, and because OneAgent becomes deeply embedded in production systems during deployment, replacing it requires a multi-year re-instrumentation programme across the entire technology estate. That friction creates the switching costs that anchor the economics. The platform earned $1,930 million in subscription revenue in fiscal year 2026 — roughly 96 cents of every revenue dollar — and has compounded total revenue at around 21% over the past four fiscal years.

The Case for the Bet

The bet rests on one central idea: Dynatrace has built a software platform that is genuinely difficult to displace, and the market is currently pricing in growth assumptions that are more pessimistic than even the bear-case outlook. The conditions below must keep holding for that idea to remain credible.

  1. Net revenue retention stays at or above 107%. This metric measures what existing customers spend in a given year compared with the year before — a reading above 100% means the installed base is expanding on its own, before any new customer is counted. The kill signal is two consecutive quarters below 107%; a single quarter below that line is a watch item, not an exit trigger.

Latest reading: Dollar-based net retention rate (constant currency) was 110% for fiscal year 2026, unchanged from 2025 and slightly below 2024's 111%. Three consecutive years at or above 110%; the exit threshold is clear of the current reading by a wide margin.

  1. Annual recurring revenue growth (constant currency) stays above 13%. This is the top-line signal for whether the platform is winning enough new business to replace the organic expansion from the installed base. Two consecutive quarters below 13% would confirm a structural deceleration.

Latest reading: Annual recurring revenue reached $2,054 million as of March 2026, 18% higher year on year. The annual filing does not break out a separate constant-currency figure for the full ARR total (the constant-currency presentation applies only to net retention); 18% reported is nonetheless far above the 13% threshold even after any plausible currency adjustment.

  1. Free cash flow margin stays above 20% for any full fiscal year. This is the acid test of whether the moat translates into durable profit generation rather than accounting constructs. A drop below 20% for a full year — not a quarter — would be the trigger.

Latest reading: Free cash flow was $529 million on $2,018 million of revenue — a 26.2% margin, up from 25.4% in fiscal year 2025. The margin is a full six percentage points above the exit threshold, and has been above 20% for every fiscal year in the data series.

  1. No open-market equity sales by the chief executive or chief financial officer. Sales of $5 million or more within any 90-day window would indicate that management's private assessment of the business diverges sharply from the investment thesis.

Latest reading: No open-market equity sales by either the chief executive or chief financial officer in the twelve months to March 2026. The company repurchased $479 million of shares at the corporate level during the same period, with a new $1 billion authorisation launched in February 2026.

  1. Platform-subscription penetration (Dynatrace Platform Subscription as a proportion of annual recurring revenue) stays above 72%. This migration metric signals whether existing customers are adopting the new consumption-based licence model. Stalling below 72% for three consecutive quarters — particularly if management ceases disclosing the figure — would indicate the expansion strategy is failing.

Latest reading: The annual filing does not disclose platform-subscription penetration as a numeric percentage. Management describes the model in detail and expresses intent to drive adoption, but the point-in-time figure is only available in quarterly earnings releases. The last disclosed figure (around 70% as of late 2025) is stale. This is the most significant blind spot in the current data set.

  1. No confirmed enterprise customer displacement by Cisco/Splunk or Palo Alto/Chronosphere. These two merged security-and-observability platforms represent the bundled-suite threat: large customers choosing one consolidated vendor rather than a best-of-breed observability specialist. The trigger requires named customer wins, not merely acquisition announcements.

Latest reading: Palo Alto Networks confirmed the acquisition of Chronosphere in 2026 — the preparatory signal noted in the thesis. No named Dynatrace customer displacement has been disclosed by either Cisco or Palo Alto. Net retention at 110% is the primary counter-evidence that displacement is not occurring at scale.

  1. Net cash stays above $600 million. Cash materially below that line — without a disclosed rationale such as an acquisition — would signal balance-sheet deterioration inconsistent with the thesis.

Latest reading: Cash and equivalents stood at $1,097 million as of 31 March 2026, with no outstanding borrowings under the credit facility. Net cash is well above the $600 million floor by any reasonable definition; the thesis figure of $823 million cited at the time of writing was an earlier snapshot.

The Share Price

At $41.42, Dynatrace sits in the middle of the hold band — the range between the price at which a new purchase would offer a meaningful margin of safety and the price at which trimming becomes the right call. That places the shares at fair value by the yield-based measure used here: free cash flow of $530 million against a market value of roughly $12 billion works out to a yield of 4.4%, essentially equal to the fair-value anchor of the same 4.4%.

The buy edge sits at $31 — the price at which that free cash flow yield would rise to 5.9%, a level the model treats as offering sufficient excess return over the holding's cost of capital. The trim edge sits at $55 — where the yield would fall to 3.3%, close enough to the base-case intrinsic-value estimate of $58 that the remaining upside would require bull-case execution to justify a full position.

The exit edge sits at $101 — the price at which the market would be pricing in roughly six years of compounding at today's growth rate, implying a forward return below the long-run equity premium. That level is 144% above the current price and functions as a hard ceiling beyond which the standing rules call for closing the position regardless of thesis state.

What's Wobbling

Two conditions are producing mixed signals rather than clean confirmations.

Palo Alto/Chronosphere acquisition. The bundled-suite threat cited in the thesis has moved from hypothetical to confirmed preparation. Palo Alto Networks disclosed the Chronosphere acquisition in 2026, creating a directly competing observability platform within a major security vendor. Exit condition six requires proof of named Dynatrace customer losses, not merely competitive positioning — that evidence has not appeared. Net retention holding at 110% is the strongest available counter-signal. The watch item is Palo Alto's go-to-market announcements for the combined platform, expected at upcoming industry events and quarterly earnings calls.

Subscription gross margin compression. Subscription gross margin declined one percentage point to 85%, driven by higher cloud-hosting costs as the platform scales toward a more software-as-a-service delivery model. Total gross margin expanded from 81% to 82%, which confirms that the platform's pricing lever is intact at the product level. Exit condition three references free cash flow margin (26.2%, well above the 20% floor), not subscription gross margin; the compression does not cross any written line. It is, however, a cost trend that warrants monitoring as the subscription base grows.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Observability commoditises faster than expected. Datadog's product-development pace, the rapid adoption of open-source agents such as OpenTelemetry, and the emergence of eBPF-native startups all apply downward pressure on the pricing power that sustains 110% net retention. If generic alternatives reach feature parity with OneAgent at lower cost, net retention could compress materially within two or three years.
  • DPS conversion stalls. If the platform-subscription migration stalls below 72% of annual recurring revenue — and particularly if management stops disclosing the penetration figure — the expansion engine that drives natural revenue growth within the existing customer base would be impaired. This is the condition with the least current data.
  • Large-language-model-native monitoring emerges as a substitute. Dynatrace's filing explicitly acknowledges the risk from "generic LLMs, generative AI, and general-purpose agents" addressing enterprise monitoring needs without a specialised platform. If enterprise technology teams route observability questions to general-purpose AI assistants rather than dedicated platforms, the total addressable market could shrink.
  • Capital allocation deteriorates. A drop in net cash below $600 million without a disclosed acquisition rationale, or open-market equity sales by senior management, would signal a divergence between management's private assessment and the thesis. Neither has occurred, but both require quarterly monitoring.
  • Macro-driven IT budget cuts. Observability is a discretionary line item in enterprise technology budgets during a spending freeze. A severe macroeconomic contraction could compress net retention across the installed base regardless of product quality, temporarily breaking the 110% signal without any underlying loss of competitive position.

Gaps in the Picture

Platform-subscription penetration is not disclosed in annual filings. The most important expansion metric — what proportion of annual recurring revenue has migrated to the Dynatrace Platform Subscription consumption model — is disclosed only in quarterly earnings commentary and not in the annual filing reviewed here. The last available figure (approximately 70% as of late 2025) is stale by at least two quarters. A complete picture requires monitoring the quarterly earnings releases.

Constant-currency annual recurring revenue growth rate. The annual filing discloses 18% total-ARR growth but does not separately present a constant-currency figure for the full ARR total (the constant-currency adjustment applies to net retention only). The 13% exit threshold was designed against a constant-currency measure; at 18% reported, any plausible currency effect still clears the threshold by a wide margin, but the exact constant-currency reading is unavailable from this source.

Risk-free rate sourced from SPY market data snapshot. The hurdle rate calculation uses the 10-year government bond yield read from the broader portfolio's SPY market data file (snapshot dated 2026-07-06) rather than from a DT-specific field, which is absent. This is a structural convenience rather than a data error; the rate would need to shift materially to affect the conclusion.

Fair-value drift between methods. The yield-based fair value ($41) and the scenario-weighted discounted cash flow midpoint ($158, from the base-case scenario analysis) diverge by roughly 75%. The yield method anchors to today's free cash flow; the scenario method prices in fifteen-plus years of compounding. The two approaches measure different things. Both are documented; neither has been reconciled into a single preferred figure.

The Rules

If the thesis is confirmed — all conditions working and no exit triggered — the rule is to maintain the full position, continue monitoring the quarterly metrics, and consider adding modestly on dips below $38 while net retention holds at or above 107%.

If the thesis is weakening — net retention declining toward 107% or annual recurring revenue growth decelerating toward 14% — the rule is to reduce to a half position within thirty days of the trigger signal, then monitor for two additional quarters before deciding whether to exit fully or rebuild. Weakening signals include net retention between 107% and 109% for one quarter, annual recurring revenue growth between 13% and 15% for one quarter, or platform-subscription penetration stalling below 72%.

If the thesis is broken — net retention below 107% for two consecutive quarters, annual recurring revenue growth below 13% for two consecutive quarters, free cash flow margin below 20% for a full fiscal year, chief executive or chief financial officer open-market equity sales of $5 million or more, or confirmed displacement of named enterprise customers by Cisco/Splunk or Palo Alto/Chronosphere — the rule is to exit fully within one quarter of confirmed break. Averaging down on a broken thesis is explicitly excluded.

If the price falls below the buy edge — free cash flow yield above 5.9%, implying a share price near $31 — the rule is to accumulate toward a full position size. At that level the implied return premium over the cost of capital is exceptional, and the management buyback at $37.71 provides evidence that the business itself regards prices near that level as deeply undervalued.

If the price rises above the trim edge — free cash flow yield falling below 3.3%, implying a share price near $55 or above — the rule is to trim toward a half-to-two-thirds position. The exit rule does not apply unless the thesis is simultaneously breaking; a high-quality compounder with durable net retention and strong cash generation merits continued partial ownership through multiple expansions.
