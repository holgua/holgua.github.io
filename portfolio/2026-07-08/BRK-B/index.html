The Bottom Line

Data as of 2026-07-06 · Latest filing 2026-05-04.

The bet is working. Berkshire Hathaway's compounding engine continued to perform through the first quarter of 2026: book value grew, operating earnings rose, the railroad delivered its best recent earnings quarter, and a major legal overhang at the energy subsidiary was cut sharply by an appeals court ruling — the conditions are working across all four written exit criteria. Recent Development

The headline from Berkshire's quarterly report for the three months ending in March 2026 is that book value per share — the single most important number for this bet — kept growing. Shareholders' equity rose a further $9.8 billion to $727.2 billion during the quarter, implying a per-share book value of approximately $337 at current share counts. That follows a full-year 2025 increase of 10.5 per cent, which is the primary compounding signal the thesis watches.

The other big development was legal rather than financial. In April 2026, an Oregon appeals court reversed the initial-phase wildfire liability verdict against Berkshire's power utility subsidiary, PacifiCorp — a ruling that sharply reduces the worst-case legal exposure that had been hanging over the energy business. The energy unit also agreed to sell its Washington state power operations to Portland General Electric for $1.9 billion in cash, simplifying its regulatory footprint. Neither event is earnings-moving in isolation, but together they substantially reduce the tail risk that had been the most prominent wobble in prior quarters.

The railroad, Berkshire's freight-rail business known as BNSF, delivered pre-tax earnings of $1.82 billion in the quarter — up 13.5 per cent from $1.60 billion in the same period a year earlier — driven by higher average revenue per car and lower fuel costs. That is the best recent quarterly earnings growth for that segment. The one step back was at Berkshire's car-insurance business, GEICO, where the combined ratio (a measure of underwriting profitability where lower is better; a ratio above 100 per cent means the business is paying out more than it collects) rose from 79.8 per cent to 87.3 per cent, as claims frequency and severity both climbed from unusually low levels in the prior-year quarter. GEICO remains profitable, but the year-on-year comparison looks worse than the underlying trend warrants. That question lands in The Share Price section's assessment of where things stand today.

About Berkshire

Berkshire Hathaway is an insurance-led holding company that owns a large collection of wholly-owned businesses — most prominently, Berkshire's car-insurance business, GEICO; the BNSF freight railroad across the western United States; Berkshire Hathaway Energy, a regulated utility group operating in six US states; Precision Castparts, a manufacturer of aerospace components; and a manufacturing and services portfolio spanning consumer brands, industrial products, and building materials. Alongside these wholly-owned operations, Berkshire also holds a roughly $298 billion portfolio of publicly traded shares, led by positions in Apple, Bank of America, and American Express.

The business model that ties all of this together is the insurance float. When customers pay premiums to GEICO, General Re, or Berkshire's other insurers, that money sits with Berkshire until claims are paid — sometimes years later. This pool of investable money, which stood at approximately $176.9 billion at the end of March 2026, costs Berkshire little or nothing to hold (and sometimes has a negative cost, meaning the underwriting profit exceeds the time value of money). That float funds the investments. The compounding from deploying float and retained earnings into high-returning businesses and securities over decades is the source of Berkshire's long-run returns — and the central question this bet asks is whether Gregory Abel, who became chief executive on 2026-01-01, can keep that engine running as well as Warren Buffett did.

The Case for the Bet

The bet on Berkshire is that the management culture Buffett built — decentralised, owner-operator, capital-disciplined — survives the transition to Abel and continues to compound shareholders' equity at rates a passive index cannot match. Four written conditions define when that bet is off.

  1. Book value per share growth trails the MSCI World total return for five or more consecutive years. This is the primary signal that the compounding edge has been lost. Latest reading: book value grew 10.5 per cent in the full year 2025, and a further $9.8 billion in the first quarter of 2026, reaching $727.2 billion. No five-year underperformance sequence is in sight. This condition is comfortably not triggered.

  2. A large acquisition that reveals Abel lacks Buffett's capital discipline. Specifically, a single transaction exceeding $20 billion at a price above 1.5 times the book value of the target, or one that subsequently requires a goodwill write-down exceeding $5 billion. Latest reading: Abel's first acquisition as chief executive, OxyChem (Occidental Petroleum's chemicals business) at roughly $9.5 billion, was sub-threshold in size and priced rationally. No qualifying acquisition has been announced. This condition is not triggered.

  3. A structural culture change that signals the decentralised model is being abandoned. This includes introduction of a regular dividend, a break-up of the conglomerate, or an activist capitulation. Latest reading: no such development has occurred. Greg Abel continues as chief executive with no reported leadership changes, and the quarter contained no succession-related disclosure. Silent this quarter — not a concern in isolation, but a condition that warrants watching with each new filing.

  4. The price-to-book ratio (the share price divided by the per-share book value of the business) exceeds 2.0 times. Above that level, the valuation fully prices in optimistic compounding assumptions and the opportunity cost versus a passive index becomes clearly negative. Latest reading: the current ratio stands at approximately 1.52 times, using year-end 2025 equity. Using the more recent first-quarter 2026 equity of $727.2 billion, the ratio is approximately 1.50 times. Both readings are comfortably below the 2.0-times exit condition.

The Share Price

The share price of $504 corresponds to a price-to-book ratio of approximately 1.52 times — using the most recently published annual book value figure. Against the fair-value anchor of 1.45 times (the level that reflects a quality franchise with a moderate discount for the Abel transition uncertainty), the share price is modestly above that anchor in absolute terms.

In band terms, the buy edge sits at $415 (a price-to-book of 1.25 times, in line with Buffett's historical buyback floor) and the trim edge at $581 (1.75 times, where the valuation starts pricing in aggressive compounding assumptions). At $504, the share price sits in the upper half of the hold band — closer to the trim edge than to the buy edge, but not close to triggering a trim. The valuation is consistent with fair value when measured against the fair-value anchor directly.

Buybacks during the first quarter were relatively modest at roughly $235 million — 33 Class A shares and approximately 431,000 Class B shares — which management described as relatively minor amounts. This is consistent with Abel's stated practice of repurchasing shares only when price is clearly below intrinsic value by a meaningful margin. At current prices near the fair-value anchor, discipline rather than opportunism governs.

What's Wobbling

Berkshire's car-insurance business, GEICO, is the one condition that showed mixed signals in the quarter. The combined ratio — the ratio of claims and expenses to premiums collected — rose to 87.3 per cent in the three months ending in March 2026 from 79.8 per cent in the same period a year earlier. That 7.5 percentage-point worsening was driven by higher claims frequency (claims coming in more often, up roughly 5 to 7 per cent) and higher claims severity (the cost per claim climbing, up roughly 12 to 14 per cent).

Two things put this in context. First, the first quarter of 2025 was an unusually clean quarter with near-zero catastrophe losses — an outlier period rather than a normal baseline. Second, GEICO's combined ratio of 87.3 per cent still means the underwriting business is solidly profitable: the ratio needs to breach 100 per cent before the business is losing money on its insurance operations, and 87.3 per cent is well below that line. The aggregate underwriting result across all of Berkshire's insurance operations actually improved year on year — the other parts of the group (the reinsurance business known as BHRG and the primary insurance unit) more than offset the GEICO step-down. The exit condition for GEICO in this bet is framed as a market-share and competitive concern, not a single-quarter combined-ratio print. Policies-in-force data — the metric most relevant to that question — are not disclosed in a quarterly filing; the annual letter is the right place to check that number.

Share repurchases were similarly mixed: modest amounts at near-fair-value prices, consistent with discipline rather than a policy change.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Abel deploys the cash pile poorly. Berkshire held roughly $369 billion in cash and Treasury bills at the end of 2025. That is the biggest optionality in the investment case — and also its biggest risk. One large acquisition at an inflated price or without a clear moat would be a direct hit to the thesis's second exit condition. The first Abel-era deal at scale will be the defining test.
  • BNSF loses its western-rail duopoly. A merger between Union Pacific and Norfolk Southern, if approved by regulators, would create a combined rail entity large enough to end the comfortable western-rail duopoly that has supported BNSF's pricing power for decades. The regulatory decision is pending in 2027; the outcome is genuinely uncertain.
  • A catastrophic insurance loss event. A single large catastrophe — a hurricane, wildfire, or earthquake of unusual severity — could produce an underwriting loss exceeding $15 billion and temporarily reverse the book-value compounding that the bet relies on. Insurance has fat tails; this is the occupational hazard of the business model.
  • GEICO continues to lose competitive ground to Progressive. Progressive's telematics-based pricing technology has been outcompeting GEICO's direct-response model for several years. Ajit Jain confirmed telematics parity was achieved in 2025, but regaining lost market rank is harder than holding it.
  • BHE wildfire liabilities re-escalate. The Oregon court reversal in April 2026 materially reduced the worst-case exposure, but the cumulative wildfire accrual still stands at $2.9 billion with $577 million unpaid. Further litigation on remaining phases could produce new accruals above the monitoring threshold.

Gaps in the Picture

The share count used to compute the current price-to-book ratio is implied from the market capitalisation divided by the share price — the underlying filing data does not separately report diluted shares outstanding in a way the extraction pipeline can reliably parse for Berkshire's multi-class share structure. The 2,162.7 million Class B equivalent shares used here are cross-checked against the disclosed market capitalisation and are consistent with management's per-share book value disclosures, but the number is mechanically derived rather than directly extracted from the filing. The impact is small and the direction of any error is bounded, but the figure carries that caveat.

A second caveat relates to the 1,500:1 Class A to Class B conversion ratio applied throughout. This ratio is the standard public-record figure, but users should verify it against the current filing disclosure before acting on any per-share figures.
