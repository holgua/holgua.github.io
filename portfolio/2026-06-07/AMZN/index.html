Amazon: The Bet Is Still Working

The Bottom Line

The bet on Amazon as a long-term compounding machine is working as the case requires. Cloud growth has actually accelerated, advertising is compounding in the low twenties, and the international retail leg is widening its profit margin. The pace of spending on artificial-intelligence infrastructure has stepped up sharply, but the cloud sales it is meant to produce stepped up alongside it — so the rule that guards against the spending running away from the cloud business has not been crossed. The share price near $250 sits above the fair-value anchor and well above the bargain line, which means no obvious reason to buy fresh and no reason to harvest.

The Verdict

The latest reading puts Amazon firmly in "still working" territory. That translates differently depending on the price paid for the shares.

  • As a fresh investment at today's price (around $250 per share). Wait. The current share price is above the fair-value anchor of about $206 and well above the bargain price of roughly $165 that would justify a new purchase. The rules call for setting price alerts at $206 and $165 and waiting for the lower line.
  • As an existing investment bought below $250 per share — currently sitting on a gain. Hold. The compounding engines (cloud and advertising) are still running as the case requires. Adding shares calls for either a bargain-range price or a clear improvement in the spending question — neither is present today.
  • As an existing investment bought roughly between $250 and $300 per share — currently around break-even or a mild loss of up to 17 percent. Hold. Adding shares is off the table at today's price; harvesting is not warranted on a still-working case. The position stays put.
  • As an existing investment bought between $300 and $350 per share — currently showing a loss of roughly 17 to 29 percent. Hold. A mild-loss position on a still-working case is exactly the situation the rules are designed to sit through. Cloud is accelerating; the case has not weakened.
  • As an existing investment bought above $350 per share — currently showing a loss of about 29 percent or more. Hold. A deep-loss position paired with a still-working bet does not call for selling. Capitulating while cloud growth is accelerating and no condition has broken would lock in a loss against improving fundamentals.

About Amazon

Amazon is three businesses welded together by a single capital allocator.

The first is the online retail operation — the website most people know, plus the marketplace where independent sellers list goods that Amazon ships through its own fulfilment network. This is the cash engine: $426 billion in sales in 2025, throwing off about seven cents of operating profit on every sales dollar, up from roughly two cents three years earlier. Subscriptions to Prime — the membership programme that bundles delivery, video, music, grocery, and pharmacy benefits — add roughly $50 billion of high-stickiness revenue.

The second is Amazon Web Services, the cloud-computing arm that rents out data-centre capacity to other companies. This is the small-but-disproportionate profit pool: $129 billion of revenue in 2025 — only about a fifth of the total — but 35 cents of operating profit on every revenue dollar, contributing more than half of company-wide operating income. The most recent quarter showed cloud sales growing 28 percent year on year, faster than at any point in the previous two years.

The third is advertising. Amazon sells sponsored placements on its own retail pages, on Prime Video, and across its other surfaces. It is a $69 billion business growing in the low-twenties percentage range, and most of the revenue drops to the bottom line because the platform — the website and the audience — already exists.

The international side of the retail business is part of the first leg but worth flagging separately: it turned profitable only in 2024, and the operating margin has been climbing since. That climb is part of the case for staying invested.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that Amazon is the most diversified compounding machine in mega-cap technology — a retail engine that funds two younger, higher-margin businesses (cloud and advertising) whose economics still have years of room to run.

For the bet to keep working, the following conditions must keep holding:

  1. Cloud growth and cloud margins stay healthy. Cloud revenue should grow at least 15 percent year on year, ideally 18 to 20 percent or better. The cloud segment should keep earning at least 28 cents of operating profit on every revenue dollar, with a target above 30 cents.
  2. Advertising stays in double-digit growth. Advertising should not slip into single-digit growth for two consecutive years. Saturation of the third compounding engine would shorten the runway.
  3. International retail keeps widening its profit margin. The international leg, profitable only since 2024, should keep extending that gain rather than reverting to break-even.
  4. The artificial-intelligence spending pays off. Total spending on property and equipment should not stay above $120 billion per year in both 2027 and 2028 unless cloud revenue is accelerating to match. The spending and the cloud sales should keep moving together.
  5. No court-ordered break-up. The unresolved antitrust trial brought by the Federal Trade Commission should not produce a structural remedy requiring Amazon to separate the cloud arm or the marketplace from the rest of the company.
  6. Accounting and governance stay clean. No surprise change of auditor (Ernst & Young has held the engagement since 1996), no disclosed internal-control failure, and no unusually large or unscheduled share sales by Jeff Bezos that fall outside the established pre-arranged trading plan.
  7. Capital allocation stays acceptable. Management may continue to favour reinvestment over buybacks, but should avoid value-destructive acquisitions and remain disciplined about price when acquiring or investing in other companies.

The Rules

The rules that govern what action follows from each combination of conditions and share price are fixed:

  • If all conditions are intact. Hold. No action. Let the cloud-and-advertising flywheel compound.
  • If any one condition is wobbling but none has clearly broken. Do not add to the position. Watch the deteriorating condition through one more quarterly filing, then a second, before reconsidering.
  • If any one condition has clearly broken. Sell the full position within thirty days. No averaging down. The cloud-revenue and cloud-margin conditions require two consecutive quarters of confirmation before triggering a sale; a court-ordered break-up or a sudden auditor change is grounds for immediate exit.
  • If the share price has dropped into the bargain range and the conditions are still intact. Add to the position in stages over one or two quarters.
  • If the share price has run into the expensive range. Trim a quarter to a third of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding, then reconsider sizing.

The Conditions Today

The most recent quarterly filing — covering the three months ending in March 2026 — reads condition by condition as follows.

Cloud growth. Amazon Web Services posted revenue of about $37.6 billion for the quarter, up 28 percent from a year earlier. That is above the case's target band of 18 to 20 percent and roughly 13 percentage points above the line that would trigger concern. The filing attributes the acceleration to increased customer usage, partly offset by lower contracted pricing on long-term customer agreements. A separate disclosure notes that the existing $38 billion multi-year commercial arrangement with OpenAI has been expanded by $100 billion over eight years.

Cloud margins. Operating profit on cloud revenue was about $14.2 billion on $37.6 billion of sales — 38 cents on the revenue dollar. That is below the 40-cent reading a year earlier (depreciation roughly doubled as new infrastructure came online), but still about 10 percentage points above the line that would trigger concern.

Advertising growth. Advertising revenue ran at $17.2 billion for the quarter, up about 24 percent from a year earlier. That is consistent with the low-twenties pace and well clear of single-digit territory.

International retail. International segment operating margin rose to about 3.6 percent, up from 3.0 percent a year earlier, on 19 percent revenue growth. The "just-turned-profitable" thesis is extending rather than fading.

Artificial-intelligence spending. Cash spent on property and equipment in the quarter was $43.2 billion, up from $24.3 billion in the same quarter a year earlier. Trailing twelve-month spending stood at $151 billion. Annualised, that pace is well above the $120 billion line in the spending condition — but the rule explicitly requires the spending to be running ahead without matching cloud sales acceleration. Cloud sales just accelerated from 17 percent to 28 percent year on year, so the spending and the cloud growth are still moving together. The condition is not breached this quarter, but the spending pace is the watch item for the next two filings.

Antitrust trial. The filing discloses ongoing antitrust litigation including matters brought by the Federal Trade Commission, but reports no structural remedy, settlement, or material adverse ruling. The trial date previously slated for the autumn of 2026 is not separately addressed in this filing.

Accounting and governance. No restatements, no internal-control issues, and no auditor change disclosed. Jeff Bezos continues to sell shares under pre-arranged trading plans at the established cadence; no single filing exceeds the line in the rules.

Capital allocation. No share buybacks were executed in the quarter, against $6.1 billion still available under the existing repurchase authorisation. Meanwhile, $15 billion was invested in OpenAI preferred stock with a further $35 billion equity commitment letter; a $5 billion investment in Anthropic and a $20 billion related financing facility were entered into shortly after the quarter; and a $10.9 billion all-cash merger agreement to acquire Globalstar was signed. The pattern is consistent with the case's framing — reinvestment is the preferred tool — but the absolute scale of those commitments inside one quarter is worth flagging.

The Share Price

At about $250 per share, Amazon currently generates about 4.9 cents of free cash flow per dollar of market value. The rules anchor a fair price at 6.0 cents per dollar — roughly $206 per share — and call the shares clearly cheap when the figure rises to 7.5 cents per dollar (a price around $165) or clearly expensive when it falls to 4.0 cents (a price around $309). A separate exit line sits at 2.7 cents per dollar (a price around $494).

The current 4.9-cent reading sits inside the acceptable range, but the share price is roughly 21 percent above the fair-value anchor. There is no margin of safety for a fresh purchase and no premium that would justify harvesting. Put plainly: today's price is leaning rich, not yet expensive.

The Standing Orders

The rules translate the share-price bands into three standing orders and two price alerts. Each is the operationalisation of a pre-committed rule, not a fresh judgement.

Price alerts (informational).
- A first alert at $206, the fair-value anchor. The crossing means the share price has returned to a neutral reading; no action follows, but a fresh read of the conditions is warranted.
- A second alert at $165, the cheap edge. The crossing arms the buy order below; before adding, the latest re-validation should confirm the conditions are still working.

Standing orders (actionable).
- A standing buy order at $165. The rules call for adding in stages once the share price closes at or below this line and the conditions are still working. The order may be staged across one or two quarters rather than filled in a single fill.
- A standing trim order at $309. The rules call for trimming a quarter to a third of the position when the share price closes at or above this line, to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.
- A standing exit order at $494. The rules call for closing the full position when the share price closes at or above this line. The market is pricing in roughly 11 years of base-case compounding instantly at that reading; the implied forward return falls below acceptable levels, and reallocation beats continuing to hold.

The three order levels do not contradict the cost-basis verdicts above: those verdicts describe today's reading; the standing orders describe what the rules will execute if the share price moves to each line.

What's Wobbling

Two open questions cloud an otherwise clean reading.

The first is the pace of artificial-intelligence spending. At an annualised rate well above $150 billion, the spending is running materially ahead of the $120 billion line in the rules. The reason the condition is not breached is that the rules require the spending to be running away from cloud sales — and cloud sales accelerated to 28 percent in the same quarter the spending stepped up. The pairing logic still holds. But this is the most important variable on the file: a single quarter of cloud-growth deceleration with the spending still at this pace would tip the reading from "watch" to "wobble."

The second is the cluster of large equity commitments inside one quarter — the OpenAI investment plus the OpenAI commitment letter plus the Anthropic stake plus the related financing facility plus the Globalstar merger — totalling well over $80 billion in absolute commitments. The case explicitly accepts reinvestment in place of buybacks and has historically tolerated this pattern. But the scale concentrated in one quarter is at the edge of the case's stated tolerance for "above-cost-of-capital reinvestment." Two consecutive quarters of equity commitments of this magnitude in non-cloud assets would be the trigger to look harder.

Neither pressure crosses a written line this quarter. The case is squarely in the "working" state.

What Could Still Go Wrong

The case for Amazon could still break in a small number of structurally distinct ways:

  • The cloud business loses momentum. Microsoft Azure and Google Cloud are competing aggressively for artificial-intelligence workloads. If cloud revenue growth slips below 15 percent for two consecutive quarters, or the cloud operating margin compresses below 28 percent for two consecutive quarters, the first condition breaks and the rules call for selling.
  • The Federal Trade Commission wins a structural remedy. A court-ordered separation of the cloud arm or the marketplace from the rest of the company would force a fundamental restructuring. Probability over the next two years is low but the impact would be high.
  • The artificial-intelligence spending fails to convert. If the spending stays above $120 billion in both 2027 and 2028 while cloud sales decelerate, the buy-out implies stranded capacity, asset impairments, and structural margin pressure.
  • Tariff and marketplace pressure deepens. The filing flags forward-looking risks from tariff policy changes and notes that China-based sellers account for significant portions of marketplace and advertising revenue. Cross-border discounters and shifts in consumer search behaviour toward newer platforms could compress the retail leg over time.
  • A capital-allocation misstep. A large value-destructive acquisition or a sequence of expensive minority equity investments that do not earn a return on capital would shift the case from "reinvesting at high incremental returns" to "spending without discipline."

The worst plausible drawdown — combining a cloud-growth deceleration with a Federal Trade Commission structural remedy and a partial reversal of the Anthropic mark-up — could plausibly be a 30 to 40 percent fall in the share price over twelve to eighteen months. Permanent capital impairment is unlikely given the cash flows the business currently throws off and the strength of the cloud and marketplace positions; a multi-year period of holding underwater capital while the case proves itself is the more realistic downside.

Gaps in the Picture

A few things in the underlying figures are worth flagging plainly.

The "free cash flow" used to anchor the share-price reading is calculated from operating cash flow minus a narrow accounting line for capital spending. The strict accounting line for capital spending was small in 2025; the company's own definition of capital spending — which captures the full artificial-intelligence infrastructure build — was much larger. The reading used here is the more generous of the two. On the company's stricter definition, the same anchor calculation would put the shares well into expensive territory rather than just above fair value. Whether the artificial-intelligence spending should count as "growth" or "maintenance" capital is the central judgement embedded in the reading, and it is unresolved.

The cloud-margin reading of 38 percent for the quarter is below the 40 percent reading from a year earlier. The narrative is straightforward (depreciation stepped up as new infrastructure came online), but a second quarter of the same pattern would warrant a closer look at whether the spending is producing revenue fast enough to cover its own depreciation.

The Federal Trade Commission trial date previously expected for the autumn of 2026 is not addressed in this quarterly filing. The verdict in the meantime is "monitor, do not act" — but any meaningful update between filings would compress the timeline.

The cluster of large equity commitments inside one quarter is consistent with the case's framing but pushes against its edge. Two more quarters at the same pace would warrant a fresh look at the capital-allocation condition rather than letting it ride.
