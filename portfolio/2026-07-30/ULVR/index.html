Recent Development

The most significant development from Unilever's 2025 annual filing is a combination that rarely arrives together: operating margin at the top of its five-year range (17.9%), while the revenue base is meaningfully smaller. That combination is the signature of a company shedding lower-margin activity rather than losing market share. The reported revenue decline of 3.8% reflects the disposal of the Ice Cream division — Magnum, Ben & Jerry's, Cornetto — which was demerged in December 2025. Strip out the departed segment, and the underlying picture is one of acceleration: the brands that remain in the portfolio (Dove, Hellmann's, Domestos, Axe, Knorr) grew underlying sales at 4.3% in the full year 2025 and 5.0% in the first quarter of 2026, with volume — the more durable form of growth — turning positive at 4.0% in that quarter. Emerging-market organic growth ran at 5.7% in early 2026, with India alone at 7%.

The one wobble in the data is net debt, which sits at approximately USD 23.3 billion. Cash balances declined from EUR 6.1 billion to EUR 3.9 billion in the year, partly reflecting demerger costs and the early phases of the McCormick Foods transaction preparation. That transaction — the planned sale of Hellmann's, Knorr, and Marmite to McCormick for USD 44.8 billion — remains on track, and its proceeds are the mechanism through which Unilever's debt load is expected to shrink materially after mid-2027. The antitrust review is ongoing across three jurisdictions.

For the valuation picture and what the price means against the established bands, see the Share Price.

About Unilever

Unilever is a British-Dutch consumer goods company whose products reach approximately one-third of the world's households every day. The business makes soap, shampoo, conditioner, deodorant, laundry detergent, condiments, and tea. After the December 2025 demerger of its ice cream operations and the pending sale of its Nutrition brands (Hellmann's, Knorr, Marmite) to McCormick Foods, the remaining portfolio is concentrated in four segments: Beauty & Wellbeing, Personal Care, Home Care, and a slimmer Nutrition division. The largest brands are Dove (soap, skin care, hair care), Axe and Lynx (deodorant), Domestos (bleach), Sunsilk and TRESemmé (hair care), Seventh Generation (cleaning), and the Vaseline and Ponds skincare ranges. Revenue in 2025 was approximately EUR 50.5 billion.

The economics of the business rest on two mutually reinforcing assets. The first is brand equity: consumers in most markets reach for a Dove bar or a bottle of Domestos without stopping to compare labels, and that habitual loyalty allows Unilever to charge a small but persistent premium over unbranded alternatives. In the financial year 2022, when raw-material costs surged by tens of billions of euros, Unilever raised prices sharply — and the operating margin held at 17.9%, exactly where it had been in 2021. That is the empirical evidence for pricing power. The second asset is distribution: Unilever's logistics network across more than 190 countries, built over decades and deeply embedded in local retailer relationships and cold-chain infrastructure, is not replicable quickly or cheaply by a competitor starting from scratch. Roughly 60% of revenue comes from emerging markets, and that footprint is the primary structural tailwind for long-run growth as middle-class purchasing power in those markets expands. Return on invested capital was 14.7% in 2025 — well above the estimated cost of capital of 8 to 9% per year.

The Case for the Bet

The central idea is straightforward: Unilever owns brands that consumers repurchase automatically, distributed through a network that competitors cannot build from scratch, generating cash profits well above the cost of the capital that funds them. The question is whether that combination stays intact over the next decade. The following conditions define what "intact" means in observable numbers:

  1. Operating margin holds above 14% — the moat-integrity test. A business that earns 16 to 17 cents of operating profit on every euro of sales, through commodity shocks and portfolio disruption alike, is demonstrating that the brand premium is real. A sustained collapse toward 14% would signal that private-label alternatives are winning and the premium is eroding permanently. Two consecutive years below the line constitutes a confirmed breach.

Latest reading: 17.9% operating margin in 2025, the highest in five years. The margin band since 2021 has been 16.6 to 17.9% — a range of just 1.3 percentage points across an extreme commodity cycle. Exit condition 1 (below 14% for two consecutive years) is not within reach.

  1. Owner earnings stay above EUR 5.5 billion — the cash-generation floor. Owner earnings here means operating cash flow minus an estimated EUR 1.5 billion of maintenance capital spending each year (Unilever's capital spending is not individually tagged in its official filings, so this figure is an estimate consistent with the scale of its physical assets). The EUR 5.5 billion floor corresponds to the bear case in the valuation model; a reading below it for two consecutive years would signal the business is generating substantially less cash than any reasonable scenario anticipates.

Latest reading: Operating cash flow was EUR 8.35 billion in 2025; owner earnings were therefore approximately EUR 6.85 billion — EUR 1.35 billion above the exit floor. The three-year average is approximately EUR 7.6 billion. The accruals ratio (a measure of whether reported profits are backed by actual cash receipts) was +0.016 — effectively zero, confirming that cash earnings are as good as reported earnings.

  1. Power Brand underlying sales growth stays above 1.5% — the pricing-power signal. Unilever tracks sales growth across its "Power Brands" — the roughly 30 names that make up the bulk of revenue. Growth below 1.5% for two consecutive reporting periods would mean those brands are losing ground to supermarket own-labels or competitors faster than the emerging-market tailwind can compensate. This is the most important early-warning signal for moat erosion in developed markets.

Latest reading: Power Brand underlying sales growth was 4.3% for the full year 2025 and 5.0% in the first quarter of 2026, with volume growth turning positive at 4.0% in that quarter. No period in the recorded data has shown growth below the 1.5% exit threshold.

  1. McCormick Foods transaction completes without material repricing. The planned USD 44.8 billion sale of the Hellmann's, Knorr, and Marmite brands to McCormick is the single most consequential event in Unilever's near-term execution story. A collapse or a repricing of more than 20% downward would strand capital in lower-quality assets, eliminate the primary deleveraging path, and signal a failure of strategic credibility.

Latest reading: The transaction was signed in March 2026. Antitrust review is under way across three jurisdictions (UK, EU, US). No enforcement action has been reported as of July 2026. The transaction remains on track.

  1. Return on invested capital stays above 10% — the economic-value test. Return on invested capital measures whether the business is earning more on the capital it employs than that capital costs. Unilever's estimated cost of capital is 8 to 9%; a reading above 10% therefore confirms the business is creating economic value. Two consecutive years below 10% would signal the spread has collapsed.

Latest reading: Return on invested capital was 14.7% in 2025, recovering from a trough of 11.5% in 2024 (which was distorted by goodwill impairments during restructuring). The 14.7% reading is the strongest in the three-year observable window and well above both the 10% exit threshold and the 8 to 9% estimated cost of capital.

The Share Price

Unilever's shares trade at 4,671p (GBX 4,671, or roughly £46.71 per share on the London Stock Exchange), as of the latest data. The valuation method applied is an earnings yield based on normalised annual cash generation (operating cash flow minus estimated maintenance capital expenditure) divided by the total market value of the business. A higher yield means the shares are cheaper; a lower yield means they are more expensive.

At 4,671p, that earnings yield is approximately 6.43%. The fair-value yield — the level at which the shares are neither cheap nor expensive — has been set at 6.5%. At 6.43%, the current yield sits just fractionally below the fair-value level. That puts the share price at fair value: the market is priced approximately for the base case, with no particular margin of safety and no obvious excess.

The buy edge — the price below which the valuation is cheap enough to warrant adding — corresponds to a yield of 8.0%, implying a share price of roughly 3,754p, about 20% below where the shares trade today. The trim edge (where the shares become meaningfully expensive) corresponds to a yield of 5.0%, implying a share price around 6,006p, roughly 29% above today's level. An exit level of approximately 8,580p (a yield of 4.5%) marks the point where the market would be pricing in the bull case with near-certainty, eliminating the asymmetry.

In short: at today's price, the shares are at fair value — the business quality is high, but no bargain is being offered.

What's Wobbling

There is one driver currently flagged as mixed: balance-sheet leverage. Net debt stands at approximately USD 23.3 billion, representing a debt-to-equity ratio of roughly 161%. This is not a new development — it is a pre-acknowledged element of the thesis, and no exit condition is triggered by leverage alone. The watch signal (operating cash flow falling below 12% of revenue) has not fired: operating cash flow was EUR 8.35 billion in 2025 on revenue of EUR 50.5 billion, a ratio of 16.5%, comfortably above the 12% threshold. The deleveraging path runs through the McCormick transaction: USD 44.8 billion in proceeds, expected mid-2027, would transform the balance sheet. The risk is that the deal is delayed, blocked, or repriced — which is exit condition 6 in the Case for the Bet.

There is also a note in the valuation data about a divergence between the fair-value band model and the earnings-yield method. The fair-value band model — which uses a multi-method blend including peer multiples — produces a central estimate implying a lower yield than the 6.5% set as fair value. This divergence does not affect the current hold verdict: the earnings-yield method is the authoritative mechanism here, and at 6.43% that method says the shares are at fair value. But it is worth examining the fair-value band model before the next annual cycle to see whether the methods can be better reconciled.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • EM currency devaluation. Approximately 60% of revenue comes from emerging markets. A broad 15% decline in the EM currency basket against the euro — as modelled in the bear scenario — would compress reported revenue materially and reduce owner earnings toward USD 4.3 billion, well below the EUR 5.5 billion exit floor once converted. This is the highest-likelihood risk in the dataset.
  • Private-label acceleration. Supermarket own-label products are structurally gaining share in European food categories. If the Home Care and Nutrition brands (pre-McCormick disposal) lose pricing power faster than EM volume growth can compensate, Power Brand underlying sales growth could dip toward the 1.5% exit threshold. The current reading of 5.0% provides meaningful headroom, but one sharp quarter of market-share loss could change the picture.
  • McCormick transaction failure or repricing. A regulatory block from any of the three reviewing jurisdictions, or a forced repricing of more than 20% downward, would be a clean trigger of exit condition 6. It would also eliminate the primary mechanism for debt reduction, stranding Unilever with elevated leverage and a lower-quality portfolio.
  • Capital allocation relapse. Under the previous strategic cycle, Unilever came close to acquiring GlaxoSmithKline's consumer division for more than EUR 50 billion — a deal that, had it completed, would have destroyed value at scale. The current team is unwinding that era's excesses. A return to large-scale premium M&A would re-open that risk.
  • Operating margin below 14% for two years. This is the structural moat signal. It has not come close to firing — the five-year floor is 16.6% — but the combination of EM currency pressure, commodity re-inflation, and private-label competition in foods could theoretically compress margins over multiple years if all three arrived simultaneously.

Gaps in the Picture

The most significant gap in this cycle's data is that Unilever's annual filing is submitted in a structured format (ESEF/iXBRL) that failed to yield usable narrative text. The financial numbers — revenue, operating income, cash flow, goodwill — are confirmed from the structured data. The management commentary, the risk-factor narrative, and the strategic discussion from the annual report are not available through the standard pipeline. For qualitative verification — particularly the tone of commentary on the McCormick transaction and on emerging-market growth prospects — the 2025 Annual Report PDF is the correct source and can be retrieved from Unilever's investor-relations website.

Capital expenditure is also untagged in Unilever's ESEF filing, which means the maintenance CapEx figure (estimated at EUR 1.5 billion, or approximately 3% of revenue) is an analytical estimate rather than a reported number. This matters because owner earnings — the primary valuation metric — is operating cash flow minus that estimate. The estimate is consistent with the scale of Unilever's physical asset base and industry norms for asset-moderate consumer goods businesses, but it cannot be confirmed from the filing itself.

Additionally, the risk-free rate needed to compare Unilever's expected return to an equity risk premium was not available in the data this cycle. The dimension of the valuation that assesses whether the expected return exceeds the risk-free rate by a meaningful margin was therefore omitted from the bottom-line calculation.

The Rules

The rules below translate each state of the evaluation into a pre-specified action. There are six states; each maps to exactly one rule.

  • If the thesis is confirmed (yield between 5.0 and 8.0%, operating margin above 14%, return on invested capital above 10%, Power Brand underlying sales growth above 1.5%, and McCormick transaction on track): hold. The dividend — currently approximately 3.71% — is the primary expected return in the base case. Hold for income and optionality on a post-restructuring re-rating.
  • If the thesis is weakened (any driver deteriorating but not yet at an exit condition): do not add. Run a re-validation against the specific deteriorating driver. Monitor for one additional quarter before reassessing position size. Key monitoring dates: quarterly trading statements (Power Brand underlying sales growth, emerging-market organic growth, operating margin) and the annual filing (return on invested capital, accruals ratio, goodwill carrying value, operating cash flow to revenue ratio). Next scheduled key date: H1 2026 results, expected August 2026.
  • If the thesis is broken (any one of the seven exit conditions confirmed from filed data or press releases): exit the full position. The seven conditions are: (1) operating margin below 14% for two consecutive fiscal years; (2) return on invested capital below 10% for two consecutive fiscal years; (3) owner earnings below EUR 5.5 billion for two consecutive fiscal years; (4) goodwill impairment above EUR 3 billion in any single year without a concurrent disposal; (5) accruals ratio above +0.10 for two consecutive fiscal years; (6) McCormick Foods transaction collapsing or repriced more than 20% downward; (7) Power Brand underlying sales growth below 1.5% for two consecutive trading-statement periods. Conditions 1, 2, and 7 are unconditional — no averaging down after confirmation.
  • If the price falls below the buy edge (yield reaches 8.0% or above, implying approximately 3,754p or below — roughly a 20% decline from current levels): confirm all five conditions above are intact before adding. Add in two tranches over one to two quarters. Do not add if operating margin is below 16% in the most recent reported period.
  • If the price rises above the trim edge (yield falls below 5.0%, implying approximately 6,006p or above): trim 25 to 33% of the position. The trim is mechanical and does not require any deterioration in the underlying business.
  • If the price rises above the exit edge (yield falls below 4.5%, implying approximately 8,580p or above): exit the full position. At that yield the market is pricing the bull case as near-certain; risk/reward asymmetry has been eliminated. No re-entry within 90 days.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
