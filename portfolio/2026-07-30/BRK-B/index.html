The Bottom Line

Data as of 2026-07-30 · Latest filing 2026-05-04.

The bet is wobbling. The core compounding engine — Berkshire's insurance float, its freight-rail franchise, and its operating-earnings trajectory — continues to work, but Berkshire's car-insurance business, GEICO, delivered a sharp deterioration in its loss ratio in the three months ending March 2026, directly challenging one of the pillars the bet rests on. The share price sits in the upper half of the hold band, at fair value. The condition to watch before the next filing is whether GEICO's combined ratio (the measure of claims costs plus expenses as a share of premiums collected) continues to worsen or begins to reverse.

Recent Development

The most significant development in Berkshire's latest quarterly filing — the three months ending 31 March 2026 — was a marked deterioration in GEICO's car insurance results. GEICO's combined ratio (claims costs plus operating expenses, expressed as a share of premiums earned) rose to 87.3% from 79.8% in the same period a year earlier. Within that, the loss ratio — the share of premiums consumed by claims — jumped nearly five percentage points to 73.9%, driven by higher claim frequencies and by claim costs rising faster than premiums. Premium written growth came in at just 1.5%, a pace that suggests GEICO is not keeping up with competitors in new-policy volumes.

The rest of the business, in contrast, was largely on track. Berkshire's shareholders' equity (an approximation of the book value on which the bet's valuation rests) grew from roughly $717 billion at the end of 2025 to $727 billion by the end of March 2026 — an increase of $9.8 billion in a single quarter, consistent with the 10.5% full-year growth recorded in 2025. The float — the insurance premiums collected but not yet paid out in claims, which Berkshire invests at its own discretion — reached $176.9 billion, up roughly $500 million from the year-end figure, and the cost of holding it remained negative (meaning underwriting profits more than covered any carrying cost). The freight-rail business, BNSF, posted earnings growth of 13.4% in the quarter, with the ratio of operating costs to revenues falling to 65.6%.

In short, the quarterly filing confirmed that the compounding engine is running, but one of its key components — the competitive position of Berkshire's car-insurance franchise — has hit a patch of turbulence that needs watching before drawing conclusions. The question of whether that turbulence is temporary industry-wide cost inflation or something more structural is what drives the wobbling verdict. Further discussion in the Share Price section.

About Berkshire

Berkshire Hathaway is a holding company — not a traditional operating company — that owns a collection of wholly-owned businesses, a large portfolio of publicly traded shares, and a permanent pool of insurance capital that serves as the structural engine of the enterprise. The insurance operations (principally GEICO for personal auto, Berkshire Hathaway Reinsurance Group for large complex risks, and Berkshire Hathaway Primary for commercial lines) collect premiums upfront and pay claims later. The gap between receipts and payments at any given time — the float — stood at approximately $177 billion at the end of the first quarter of 2026. Berkshire invests that float freely, earning returns that effectively lower its cost of capital to near zero when underwriting is profitable.

Beyond insurance, Berkshire owns the freight-rail operator BNSF (32,500 route miles across the western United States, a network that could not be economically rebuilt from scratch), the energy and utility group Berkshire Hathaway Energy (regulated electricity and gas operations across multiple US states plus a renewable-energy portfolio), and a broad range of manufacturing, service, and retail businesses. It also holds a substantial portfolio of publicly traded shares — centred on a large position in Apple but diversified across several other companies — that together exceeded $400 billion in market value as of the latest filing. Greg Abel became chief executive at the start of 2026, succeeding Warren Buffett, who stepped back to the non-executive chairman role. The transition did not alter the business structure or the capital-allocation principles; it changed the person responsible for applying them.

The Case for the Bet

The central bet is that Berkshire's management culture, now led by Greg Abel after more than six decades under Warren Buffett, will continue to compound the capital entrusted to it — through underwriting discipline, intelligent allocation of the float, and selective acquisition of businesses at fair prices — at rates that exceed what a passive investment in a global index would deliver over a long horizon. Four specific conditions must hold for the bet to remain valid:

  1. Book value per share must compound at least as fast as the global equity market over rolling five-year windows. Book value per share (shareholders' equity divided by the number of equivalent shares outstanding) is the primary scorecard for the compounding bet. If it trails a passive global benchmark for five or more consecutive years, the compounding edge that justifies holding a single concentrated position rather than an index has likely disappeared.

Latest reading: Berkshire's shareholders' equity grew 10.5% in 2025 (from $649 billion to $717 billion) and added a further $9.8 billion in the three months to 31 March 2026, bringing it to $727 billion. This rate of book-value growth has comfortably exceeded the return of a passive global benchmark in recent years — the first condition reads as confirmed.

  1. No large acquisition that signals a breakdown in capital-allocation discipline. The specific trigger is a single transaction exceeding $20 billion at a reported price above 1.5 times the book value of the acquired business, or any acquisition that subsequently requires a goodwill write-down exceeding $5 billion. Either would be evidence that Abel lacks the discipline that defined Berkshire's capital-allocation record under Buffett.

Latest reading: No qualifying transaction has been announced or confirmed under Abel's leadership as of the filing date. The condition has not fired; it remains a forward watch item.

  1. No structural change that abandons the decentralised owner-operator model. A regular dividend, a break-up into separately listed subsidiaries, or capitulation to activist shareholders seeking a faster return of capital would all signal that the culture has shifted away from the model that has compounded Berkshire's value for decades.

Latest reading: The quarterly filing contains no dividend announcement, no restructuring plan, and no indication of activist pressure changing strategy. The condition reads as confirmed.

  1. The price-to-book ratio must stay below 2.0 times. Above that level, the valuation fully prices in optimistic compounding assumptions and the opportunity cost of holding Berkshire rather than a passive index becomes clearly negative. The exit is primarily a valuation discipline, not a thesis-breaking signal.

Latest reading: The price-to-book ratio at the time of the last filing stood at approximately 1.50 times, well inside the 2.0 times boundary. The condition has not fired.

The Share Price

The share price of $497 sits in the upper portion of the hold band — above the fair-value anchor of $482 but below the trim edge of $582. In the price-to-book terms that govern this valuation, Berkshire is trading at a ratio of approximately 1.50 times the book value recorded at the end of 2025, against a fair-value anchor set at 1.45 times. The market is pricing in approximately 3.2% more than the established fair-value estimate, which by the standards of the hold band is a modest premium that carries no action. The trim signal fires only above $582 (1.75 times book); the buy signal would fire only below $416 (1.25 times book). At today's price, the share is neither cheap enough to justify adding nor expensive enough to warrant harvesting the position.

Worth noting for context: the conservative discounted cash-flow estimate of the operating businesses alone — stripping out the public equity portfolio and applying a 3% long-term growth assumption — implies roughly $367 per share. The market price of $497 is higher because investors are rationally attributing additional value to the portfolio of publicly traded shares Berkshire holds on top of its operating businesses. That attribution is not unreasonable, but it does mean the current price embeds some growth expectations that the operating businesses alone do not yet fully justify.

What's Wobbling

The primary wobble is GEICO. Its combined ratio rose to 87.3% in the three months to March 2026, from 79.8% in the same period a year earlier — a 7.5-percentage-point jump that reflects both higher claim frequencies (bodily injury claims rising 5 to 7 per cent) and higher average claim costs (bodily injury severity up 12 to 14 per cent). The expense ratio also rose, from 10.8% to 13.4%, driven by higher policy-acquisition costs. Premium written growth of 1.5% is slow for an insurer trying to maintain market share against a competitor that has been gaining ground through more precise, data-driven underwriting. Not one of the four written exit conditions has been crossed — the GEICO problem is a thesis-weakening signal, not a thesis-breaking one. But it is the condition to watch at the next quarterly filing (the three months ending June 2026).

A secondary watch item is the modest pace of share repurchases. Berkshire bought back $235 million of its own shares in the first quarter of 2026 — a trivial sum relative to its $1 trillion market capitalisation. Management's stated policy is to repurchase only when the price is below intrinsic value, conservatively estimated by the chief executive. The slowdown in repurchases, following years when Berkshire was buying back billions per quarter, may signal that management views the current price as near or at intrinsic value. That reading is broadly consistent with the price-to-book valuation that also places the share near fair value.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • GEICO loses further competitive ground to data-driven rivals. Progressive's telematics approach — pricing each driver on their own measured behaviour rather than actuarial category averages — has consistently taken market share from conventional insurers. If GEICO cannot respond with comparable technology, its loss ratio could keep rising even as the broader accident-cost environment stabilises, permanently compressing underwriting margins.
  • Greg Abel makes a large acquisition that destroys value. Berkshire sits on a large cash reserve. The first major independent acquisition by the new chief executive is the single biggest governance risk: a deal priced above 1.5 times the target's book value, followed by a goodwill write-down, would be direct evidence that the capital-allocation culture has shifted from Buffett's discipline.
  • Berkshire's energy subsidiary faces new wildfire liabilities or regulatory reversal. A 2026 Oregon court ruling reversed a large wildfire verdict against PacifiCorp — a material positive — but further fires, new litigation, or adverse regulatory decisions could revive the liability. The phase-out of wind and solar production tax credits under the 2025 tax legislation also structurally reduces the after-tax returns on the energy subsidiary's $38 billion renewable investment programme.
  • The equity portfolio draws down materially. A large position in Apple accounts for a significant share of Berkshire's publicly traded portfolio. A 50% decline in Apple's value alone would remove tens of billions from book value and knock several percentage points off the price-to-book ratio, without any operating failure at Berkshire itself.
  • Coal-driven freight volumes keep falling at BNSF. BNSF's rail network generates strong returns, but a meaningful share of its revenue historically came from hauling coal to power plants. As coal use in electricity generation continues to decline, BNSF must substitute growth in agricultural, industrial, and intermodal freight to hold its earnings trajectory.

Gaps in the Picture

The share count used in the valuation calculation is inferred from Berkshire's market capitalisation divided by the share price (approximately 2,157 million equivalent class-B shares as of the last filing), because Berkshire's dual-class share structure — Class A and Class B shares with a 1,500-to-1 conversion ratio — is not cleanly reported in the standard company-facts data pulled by automated extraction. Management disclosed directly that 1,437,903 Class A equivalent shares were outstanding as of 31 March 2026, which is consistent with the inferred figure but should be verified against the management-disclosed book value per share for each re-validation.

The book value per share used in the price-to-book calculation ($332.62 per B-equivalent share) is derived from the year-end 2025 equity figure; the Q1 2026 filing confirms equity grew to $727.2 billion, implying a slightly higher per-share figure ($337 by the management-disclosed Class A equivalent count). The valuation model uses the year-end 2025 anchor as its base, consistent with the annual-reporting cadence of the price-to-book methodology.

The long-run total return estimate of approximately 4% per year is derived from the yield on Berkshire's book value (roughly 4.2% owner earnings yield at today's price, per the value analysis) plus a 3% long-term compounding assumption. Because Berkshire uses price-to-book rather than owner earnings yield as its primary valuation method, the Dimension B return-sentence is omitted from the Bottom Line — a price-to-book ratio is not a yield in the mechanical sense required for that comparison, so the figure is carried instead into the TL;DR return line.

The Rules

The action plan for Berkshire has six branches, each triggered by a different combination of thesis state and price position:

  • If thesis is confirmed and conditions are intact: hold — no action. Let it compound.
  • If thesis is weakening (as it currently is, on GEICO): do not add to the position. Run a fresh re-validation on the specific deteriorating driver. Decide after the next quarterly filing.
  • If thesis is broken (any written exit condition fires): exit the full position within 30 days. No averaging down.
  • If the price falls below the buy edge ($416): confirm the thesis is intact first. If confirmed, add to the position in tranches.
  • If the price rises above the trim edge ($582): trim 25 to 33 per cent of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.
  • If the price rises above the exit edge ($649): exit the full position. At that reading, the market is pricing in roughly 10 years of base-case compounding instantly; redeploy the proceeds at the next buy-edge crossing or into an alternative with a more attractive expected return. Re-entry is deferred by 90 days.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
