World Equal Weight: The Bet Is Wobbling

The Bottom Line

The bet on this equal-weight global stocks fund — that holding roughly 1,500 developed-market names at the same tiny percentage each beats the more familiar mega-cap-heavy version of the same index — is still alive but thinner than it used to be. The basket's earnings yield is currently 4.8 cents per dollar of stock, only about a quarter of a percentage point above what United States 10-year government debt pays for no risk. One quality measure of the basket is showing weakness, though the underlying data is corrupted and cannot yet be trusted. The fund's mechanics — size, fees, and the equal-weight discipline itself — are all working as designed.

The Verdict

The latest reading puts the fund in "still working, but the margin is thin" territory. That translates differently depending on the price paid for the units.

  • As a fresh investment at today's price (around €5.74 per unit). Wait. The basket is fairly priced rather than cheap, and the cushion over government bonds is so thin that paying full freight for a fresh purchase is hard to justify. The rules call for setting a price alert near €5 — the level that would mark the units clearly cheap — and waiting.
  • As an existing investment bought below €5 per unit — currently sitting on a gain of about 15 percent or more. Hold. The cushion over the cap-weighted alternative has narrowed, but the structural equal-weight discipline is intact and the basket is still fairly priced.
  • As an existing investment bought roughly between €5 and €6 per unit — currently anywhere between a small loss of 4 percent and a gain of about 15 percent. Hold. Adding fresh capital here means paying close to full freight for an equity premium that has thinned to almost nothing.
  • As an existing investment bought between €6 and €7 per unit — currently showing a loss of roughly 4 to 18 percent. Hold. A mild-loss position waits one more quarter before reconsidering. Selling at a loss while the basket is still fairly priced and the equal-weight mechanics still work would be premature.
  • As an existing investment bought above €7 per unit — currently showing a loss of about 18 percent or more. Hold. Crystallising a structural loss while the rebalance discipline is doing exactly what it was bought to do — pulling the basket back toward equal weights and away from whichever names ran up — would lock in the wrong outcome.

About the Fund

This is the Invesco MSCI World Equal Weighted UCITS ETF (Xetra ticker MWEQ), an exchange-traded fund domiciled in Ireland and traded mainly on the German Xetra exchange. It tracks the MSCI World Equal Weighted index, a version of MSCI's developed-markets global stock index that gives every constituent the same weight rather than weighting by company size.

The plain-English consequence is large. In the more familiar cap-weighted version of the MSCI World index, a handful of mega-cap United States technology names — Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, and a few others — dominate. In this equal-weight version, every one of the roughly 1,500 holdings gets approximately 0.07 percent of the basket on the rebalance day, and the discipline is reset every three months. The result is a basket tilted toward smaller and mid-sized companies, with more weight on non-United States markets (Japan, the United Kingdom, continental Europe) and less weight on whichever names have just had the biggest run.

The trade-offs are real. Quarterly rebalancing turns over roughly 30 percent of the basket each year and creates a small but permanent friction cost. The fund uses sampling rather than holding every name, which produces modest tracking error. And the average company quality of an equal-weight basket of 1,500 names is structurally lower than the top-10 of the cap-weighted version, because the cap-weighted top-10 is by definition a list of the world's biggest, highest-margin businesses.

The fund charges 0.20 percent per year, holds about $1.43 billion in assets, and is roughly 21 months old.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is structural rather than tactical. By weighting every company in MSCI World equally, the fund deliberately dilutes the mega-cap concentration that has driven cap-weighted indices over the past decade. The expectation is that the resulting basket has a higher earnings yield — the amount of company profits behind each dollar invested — than the cap-weighted version, because owning a slice of every small and mid-sized name is mathematically cheaper than owning a slice of the biggest names alone. The earnings-yield premium over the cap-weighted version should be large enough to justify the fund's slightly higher fee (0.20 percent against 0.12 percent for a typical cap-weighted global ETF).

For the bet to keep working, the following conditions must keep holding:

  1. The basket's earnings yield stays above the danger line. The basket's earnings yield — the inverse of its average price-to-earnings ratio — should not fall below 3 cents per dollar invested, and the cushion over a 10-year United States Treasury bond should not be negative for two consecutive quarters. Either failure means the basket has become expensive while losing its main edge.
  2. The annual fee stays competitive. Invesco should not raise the expense ratio above 0.30 percent. Equal-weight funds run higher costs than cap-weighted peers because of the rebalance turnover, but 0.30 percent is the ceiling beyond which the equal-weight premium no longer pays for itself.
  3. The fund stays large enough to survive. Assets in the fund should not drop below $500 million. Below that level, smaller UCITS funds face elevated risk of closure — and Invesco has shut sub-scale products before. The fund is still young (less than two years old) and this floor is its survival flag.
  4. Equal-weighting actually stays equal. No single holding should drift above 0.20 percent of the basket between rebalances. Equal-weighting pins every name to roughly 0.07 percent on rebalance day; a drift to three times that weight would signal a methodology change, a rebalance failure, or extreme single-name price moves the sampling has not corrected.
  5. MSCI does not change the rules. The MSCI Equal Weighted index methodology should not be materially restructured — for example, a shift to factor-tilted weights, the removal of the quarterly rebalance, or a change of the parent universe.
  6. The visible top of the basket keeps a respectable quality. The median return on equity (a measure of how much profit each dollar of company net worth generates) of the deduped top-10 holdings should not collapse below 12 percent. The threshold is lower than for the cap-weighted version because equal-weighting pulls in smaller, lower-quality names by design, but below 12 percent means even the visible top has lost quality.

The Rules

The rules that govern what action follows from each combination of conditions and basket price are fixed:

  • If all conditions are intact. Hold. The basket is reasonably priced, the fund's mechanics are sound, and the equal-weight earnings-yield premium over the cap-weighted version is intact.
  • If any one condition is wobbling but none has clearly broken. Do not add. Re-check after the next quarter. If the equal-weight earnings-yield premium over the cap-weighted version compresses below 0.5 percentage points for two consecutive quarters, the diversification benefit no longer justifies the higher fee — consider consolidating into the cap-weighted alternative (URTH).
  • If any one condition has clearly broken. Sell the position. Fund mechanics breached, or the basket is decisively expensive against bonds. Move proceeds either to the cap-weighted global core (URTH or IWDA) or to risk-free assets.
  • If the basket price has dropped into the bargain range. Add to the position. The equal-weight basket is meaningfully cheap against risk-free; the structural premium should make this rare and decisive.
  • If the basket price has run into the expensive range. Trim 25 to 33 percent of the position to harvest the premium, then redeploy on the next rebalance.

What's Wobbling

Two readings sit between "fine" and "concerning," and one is being flagged mechanically as crossing a line.

The first wobble is the equity risk premium. A cushion of 0.25 percentage points over a risk-free Treasury is below the line at which a passive equity bet pays for itself in a satisfying way. The mechanical reading places it in a mild middle band; the substantive message is the same: the basket is not paying meaningfully for risk-taking right now. This is not a sell trigger, but it is a reason not to add fresh capital.

The second wobble is the equal-weight premium over the cap-weighted version of the same index. At 0.68 percentage points it sits above the 0.5-point threshold the rules use as the switch-to-cheaper-fund signal, but the cushion is shrinking. If it compresses further over the next two quarters, the diversification benefit will not justify the extra 0.08 percentage points of fee.

The third — and the one that mechanically crosses a written line — is the top-10 quality reading at a median return on equity of 8 percent. The threshold sits at 12 percent, and the mechanical math says the line has been crossed. But the data has known errors (one obviously broken constituent value, one missing) and cannot be trusted at face value. The case for treating this as an exit signal is not yet there. The right next step is to confirm the figure against an independent source, not to act on it.

What Could Break the Fund

The case for this fund could break in a small number of structurally distinct ways:

  • The fund shrinks below the survival floor. A sustained drawdown to assets under management below $500 million — perhaps half the current level — would put the fund inside the band where UCITS sub-scale products have historically been shut by their sponsors. Invesco has done this before with other funds.
  • The annual fee rises above 0.30 percent. A fee hike to absorb the cost of higher turnover, or to fund product redesign, would eliminate the rationale for choosing equal-weight over a cap-weighted global core.
  • MSCI restructures the index. A change of the weighting rule (toward a factor or quality tilt), the removal of the quarterly rebalance, or a narrower universe would mean the underlying product has changed without the holder choosing.
  • The equal-weight premium over the cap-weighted version collapses. If the earnings-yield gap between equal-weight and cap-weighted MSCI World compresses below 0.5 percentage points for two consecutive quarters, the diversification benefit no longer pays for the fee. The mechanical answer is to switch into the cheaper cap-weighted alternative.
  • The basket becomes decisively expensive against risk-free. A move to an earnings yield below 3 percent — at a moment when bonds yield more — would mean the basket is mispriced and the bet should be wound down in favour of cash or government debt.

The most realistic permanent risk for the fund is small-fund closure: not an investment loss in the basket, but a forced sale at an unfavourable moment if Invesco decides the product is not viable. The current size sits well above that risk, but the fund is young and not yet through the durability window.
