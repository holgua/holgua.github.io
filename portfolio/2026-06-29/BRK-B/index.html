Recent Development

The three months ending in March 2026 were broadly constructive for the thesis. Book value rose a further $9.8 billion to $727.2 billion, keeping the compounding trend intact. Operating earnings came in at $12,367 million before tax versus $11,615 million in the same period a year earlier — a 6.5% increase. BNSF, Berkshire's freight railroad, delivered its strongest quarterly earnings growth in recent memory, with revenues up 4.8% to roughly $6.0 billion and pre-tax earnings up 13.4% to $1.82 billion. Outside the operating businesses, Berkshire's energy subsidiary received a significant legal reprieve: an Oregon appeals court reversed a wildfire-damages verdict against its subsidiary PacifiCorp, materially reducing what had been the most acute financial tail risk in the portfolio.

The one counter-current came from GEICO, Berkshire's car-insurance business. Its combined ratio — the percentage of every premium dollar consumed by claims and expenses, where a figure below 100% means underwriting profit — rose to 87.3% from 79.8% in the same quarter a year earlier. That prior-year reading was unusually clean, with almost no catastrophe losses; the comparison therefore overstates the deterioration. At 87.3%, GEICO is still profitably underwriting by a comfortable margin. What the reading cannot tell us is whether GEICO is losing ground to competitors with more sophisticated pricing technology — a question that quarterly filings alone cannot fully answer.

On capital allocation, Berkshire repurchased approximately $235 million of its own shares in the quarter at an average price close to the current trading level. Management described the volume as "relatively minor," which is consistent with its stated policy of buying only when shares are priced clearly below intrinsic value. The share price sits at fair value, so modest repurchase activity is the expected behaviour, not a departure from policy.

About Berkshire

Berkshire Hathaway is an insurance-led holding company that combines several distinct businesses under one roof. The largest is its collection of insurance operations, led by GEICO for personal car cover, General Re for reinsurance, and Berkshire Hathaway Reinsurance Group for large and specialty risks. Together, these businesses hold roughly $177 billion in insurance float — premiums that have been collected but not yet paid out as claims. Berkshire invests that float across a large portfolio of stocks and private businesses, historically at returns well above the cost of borrowing in conventional markets. Because the float is held at near-zero or even negative cost — meaning underwriting profits have offset the notional interest cost of the premiums — it functions as a structural subsidy to everything else Berkshire does with its capital.

Outside insurance, the two largest owned businesses are BNSF Railway, a 32,500-mile freight network spanning the western United States, and Berkshire Hathaway Energy, a regulated electricity and gas utility serving customers across several states. Both are capital-intensive, regulated, and durable: railroads and utility networks are not replaced by technology, and their franchises cannot be replicated by new entrants at any reasonable cost. Berkshire also owns a sprawling collection of manufacturing, retail, and service companies — Precision Castparts (aerospace components), Pilot Flying J (truck stops), and dozens of smaller businesses — alongside a $298 billion portfolio of publicly traded shares dominated by positions in Apple, American Express, Bank of America, and Coca-Cola.

The model has been the same for six decades: collect insurance premiums, hold the float at low cost, and compound shareholders' equity by reinvesting earnings into high-returning businesses and securities. Warren Buffett handed the chief executive role to Gregory Abel on 1 January 2026. Abel built Berkshire Hathaway Energy from a small Iowa utility into one of the largest regulated utility groups in North America. The question the market has been chewing on since the announcement is whether Abel will prove as disciplined an allocator of capital as his predecessor — a question that will only be answered one transaction at a time.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that Berkshire's management culture — decentralised, owner-operator, emphatically no-dividend — will keep compounding shareholders' equity at a rate above what a broadly diversified index would deliver, by virtue of the low-cost insurance float and the careful deployment of retained earnings.

For that bet to remain intact, four conditions must keep holding:

  1. Book value per share grows at least as fast as the MSCI World index over any five-year rolling window. If it trails for five or more consecutive years, the compounding edge is gone.
  2. Management stays disciplined on acquisitions. Any single transaction above $20 billion at a consideration above 1.5 times the target's book value — or any deal that subsequently requires a goodwill write-down above $5 billion — would signal that the capital-allocation culture has not survived the Buffett-to-Abel transition.
  3. No structural culture change. A regular dividend, a break-up, or a capitulation to activist pressure would signal that the decentralised owner-operator model has been abandoned.
  4. The price-to-book ratio stays below 2.0 times. At that multiple, the market is pricing in optimistic compounding in full, and the opportunity cost relative to a passive index becomes clearly negative.

What's Wobbling

GEICO's combined ratio rose from 79.8% to 87.3% in the three months ending in March 2026. As a stand-alone number, 87.3% is still comfortably profitable — every dollar of premiums left 12.7 cents in underwriting profit. The year-on-year change looks more alarming than it is: the prior-year period had near-zero catastrophe losses and was an outlier, not a representative baseline. Higher claims frequency and severity in the most recent quarter reflect broader insurance market conditions, not a GEICO-specific breakdown.

What cannot be confirmed from a quarterly filing is whether GEICO is losing ground in the market-share competition with Progressive Insurance. Progressive surpassed State Farm to become the largest US personal auto insurer earlier in 2026, while GEICO held the third position — a rank it did not occupy five years ago. Policies-in-force data, the most direct evidence on market share, is not separately disclosed in the quarterly report. Management confirmed telematics pricing parity in 2025, but parity is not the same as erasing accumulated data and subscriber advantages that have been building at Progressive for years. This is the one thread in the thesis that warrants close attention at each filing.

Share repurchases were modest at roughly $235 million. At a price-to-book ratio near 1.45 times — essentially fair value by the analytical framework — this is exactly what the rules would predict: disciplined restraint rather than volume buying.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Abel deploys the cash at the wrong price. The $369 billion cash and T-bill stockpile is more than a third of Berkshire's current market value. If Abel's first large acquisition exceeds $20 billion at a price above 1.5 times the target's book value, or if it subsequently requires a write-down above $5 billion, the bet on capital-discipline continuity is broken. One bad deal of sufficient size is all it would take.
  • GEICO structural erosion compounds. The loss of market rank from second to third is already a fact. If Progressive's data and technology advantages keep widening and GEICO's combined ratio exceeds 100% for two consecutive quarters, the long-run underwriting edge comes into question — and the insurance float's cost advantage depends on that underwriting discipline.
  • BNSF's western-rail duopoly ends. The proposed merger of Union Pacific and Norfolk Southern, if approved by US rail regulators in 2027, would create a combined entity with roughly 40% of US rail capacity. That ends the comfortable two-player western market BNSF has operated in for decades, and pricing leverage follows market structure closely.
  • BHE renewable investment returns fall. Legislation enacted in July 2025 accelerated the phase-out of production tax credits for new clean-energy projects starting construction after December 2025. Berkshire Hathaway Energy received over $5.5 billion in such credits between 2022 and 2024. The credits are gone for new projects, and BHE's capital programme must now rely on regulated-utility returns rather than the enhanced economics those credits provided.
  • Equity portfolio concentration bites. Roughly 65% of the $298 billion equity portfolio is concentrated in five names. A 30% drawdown in the portfolio — the bear-case assumption — would produce roughly a $90 billion mark-to-market loss. Against $717 billion in shareholders' equity that is survivable, but it would dominate GAAP earnings and potentially rattle investors unfamiliar with the accounting.

Gaps in the Picture

Two data-quality caveats from the underlying analysis carry forward.

The share count used in the valuation is implied from market capitalisation divided by the current share price, rather than extracted directly from the regulatory filing. This is because Berkshire's multi-class share structure — Class A and Class B, convertible at a 1,500-to-1 ratio — is not cleanly surfaced by the standard financial data extraction process. The implied figure of 2,150 million Class B equivalents is cross-checked against the per-share book value management discloses directly in the filing, but that cross-check is manual rather than automated.

GEICO's policies-in-force trend — the most direct evidence for or against the market-share thesis — is not disclosed in quarterly filings. It appears in Berkshire's annual letter and annual report. A fully current read of the competitive position requires waiting for the full-year filing.

The Rules

Six pre-committed rules govern what the evaluation calls for at any combination of thesis status and share price. They are stated as conditions, not instructions.

  • If the thesis is intact: hold the position, take no action, and allow it to compound.
  • If the thesis is weakening: do not add to the position; run a fresh thesis-validation exercise focused on the specific driver that is deteriorating; defer any decision until the next quarterly filing.
  • If the thesis is broken: exit the full position within 30 days; no averaging down.
  • If the share price falls below the buy band (below $417): confirm the thesis is intact first; if confirmed, add to the position.
  • If the share price rises above the trim band (above $584): trim 25–33% of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.
  • If the share price rises above the exit level (above $651): exit the full position. The market is pricing in roughly ten years of base-case compounding instantly; redeploy proceeds at the next cheap-edge crossing or into a cheaper alternative. No re-entry within 90 days.
