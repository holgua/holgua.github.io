About Elastic

Elastic N.V. sells software for searching, monitoring, and securing data at enterprise scale. Its core product, Elasticsearch, is the most widely deployed open-source search and analytics engine in the world — used to power everything from e-commerce search boxes to security-threat hunting across server logs. The business organises itself around three use cases: Search and AI (helping businesses build intelligent search into their applications), Observability (monitoring the health of software systems by ingesting logs, metrics, and traces), and Security (detecting threats by analysing events across an organisation's technology estate). All three use cases run on the same underlying Elasticsearch data layer, which is the commercial and strategic heart of the company.

Revenue arrives in two forms. Subscriptions — roughly 94% of the total — are sold either as cloud-hosted services through Elastic's own infrastructure (Elastic Cloud, now 48% of total subscription revenue) or as licences that customers run on their own servers. Services, the remaining 6%, cover professional implementation help. In the year ending April 2026, total revenue was $1,739.3 million, up 17% from the prior year. Free cash flow was $321.8 million. The company is not yet profitable on its standard accounting basis, though the operating loss has been shrinking steadily and management has guided for break-even by the year ending April 2028.

The Case for the Bet

The central argument is that once a large organisation has built its search, monitoring, or security workflows on top of Elasticsearch, the cost of migrating to a different platform is high enough to keep it in place for years. That stickiness — combined with a tendency for successful deployments to expand into additional use cases — should allow the business to compound revenue and cash flow at a rate that justifies a holding position. The conditions that must keep holding:

  1. Net expansion rate (the ratio of revenue from existing customers year over year) stays above 105%. A rate above 100% means existing customers are spending more in aggregate; below 105% for two consecutive quarters would signal that customers are churning or shrinking faster than new ones are joining — the first sign the switching-cost argument is failing.

Latest reading: approximately 112% as of 30 April 2026. The number of customers spending more than $100,000 per year grew 14% to over 1,720; the number spending more than $1 million per year exceeds 240. Both figures are consistent with an expanding, not contracting, customer base.

  1. Elastic Cloud revenue (the hosted SaaS product) grows at least 10% a year. Sustained growth below 10% would suggest that the open-source fork maintained by Amazon Web Services has successfully commoditised the self-managed installation base and is absorbing demand that would otherwise convert to Elastic's cloud product.

Latest reading: total Elastic Cloud revenue grew 22% to $837.3 million in the year ending April 2026. The higher-margin annual cloud contracts within that grew 28%. The cloud share of total revenue rose to 48% from 46% the prior year. Exit condition 2 is well clear.

  1. Free cash flow margin stays above 14% on a full-year view. A sustained compression below 14% — without an identifiable one-off cause — would indicate that the business's economics are deteriorating structurally rather than improving.

Latest reading: 18.5% in the year ending April 2026 (free cash flow of $321.8 million on $1,739.3 million of revenue), 450 basis points above the threshold. The prior-year margin was approximately 17.7%, so the trend is in the right direction.

  1. Stock-based compensation stays below 18% of revenue for each fiscal year. Two consecutive years above 18% would indicate that the business is diluting shareholders faster than it is compounding value — compensation discipline has reversed.

Latest reading: 17.16% in the year ending April 2026, down from 17.38% the prior year. The buffer is thin at 84 basis points. In absolute terms, stock-based compensation grew $40.7 million, kept in check only because revenue grew faster. A pause in revenue growth would compress the buffer further.

  1. Acquisitions stay below $500 million, priced at no more than 8 times trailing revenue, with expected cash-flow payback within 3 years disclosed publicly. This condition operationalises the requirement that the company deploy capital only where the economics are visible and proportionate.

Latest reading: two tuck-in acquisitions completed in the year ending April 2026 — an AI-embedding technology firm for $43.4 million (October 2025) and a Kubernetes cost-management business for $10.9 million (May 2025). Both are far below the $500 million threshold. Exit condition 5 is clear.

  1. The standard accounting operating margin reaches break-even by the year ending April 2028. This is a time-bound test of whether the profitability convergence trajectory is real: if it is not positive by then, the argument that the business is on a predictable path to sustainable profitability is materially weakened.

Latest reading: operating loss of −1.9% in the year ending April 2026, improved from −3.7% the prior year. At roughly 180 basis points of improvement per year, a positive margin by April 2028 is achievable. The condition is active but not yet triggered.

  1. Datadog's annual revenue growth does not exceed Elastic's by more than 15 percentage points for two consecutive fiscal years, and Elastic retains its Gartner Observability Magic Quadrant leader status. Both conditions must be met simultaneously to trigger this exit. The combined requirement means a competitive displacement must be both rapid (sustained growth gap) and externally validated (Gartner downgrade) before action is warranted.

Latest reading: the annual report itself does not publish Datadog's growth rate. The most recent external competition analysis places the gap at approximately 11 percentage points — below the 15-point trigger. Elastic confirmed Gartner Observability Leader status in the year ending April 2026. Exit condition 7 is not triggered; the driver is classified as silent because the 10-K provides no comparative data.

The Share Price

The share price of $61.86 sits below the fair-value anchor of $101, which is derived from an earnings yield of approximately 3% on the company's audited free cash flow of $321.8 million. At the current price, the yield works out to just under 5% — well inside the hold band, approaching but not yet crossing the buy threshold of 5.5% (which corresponds to a share price of roughly $56).

In other words, the market is pricing the business at 61 cents on the dollar relative to what the base-case analysis says it is worth. That gap is not a buy signal yet — the buy threshold sets a deliberate margin of safety — but it does mean the share price is closer to the buy edge than to fair value. The current position, in the terminology of the Standing Orders, is in the lower half of the hold band.

The valuation is marked by a meaningful disagreement between two independently computed fair-value estimates: the discounted cash-flow approach used as the primary anchor puts the base-case intrinsic at $98.73, while a peer-multiple approach drawing on sector comparables (Datadog, Dynatrace, Cisco) produces a base-case estimate closer to $212. Both agree the stock is below fair value at $61.86; they disagree on the magnitude. The more conservative cash-flow anchor governs the Standing Orders.

Why the Market Sees It Differently

The price says the business will earn less over time. Our view says the opposite.

The gap is 38.8% — at $61.86, the market is pricing Elastic as if its free cash flow will grow at roughly 6.4% a year in the long run. The base-case analysis assumes 15%. That 8.6 percentage-point difference in expected growth is the entire substance of the disagreement.

Three explanations are consistent with the evidence. First, the bear scenario (assigned 20% probability) implies a share price around $29 — the market's pricing may be a probability-weighted blend that pulls the weighted price below the base-case intrinsic. Second, the AWS OpenSearch fork, now developed under the Linux Foundation with over 1,400 contributors, may be gradually commoditising the installed base of self-managed Elasticsearch customers. The market may be discounting the possibility that this erosion eventually bleeds into the cloud product. Third, a broad compression of software-company valuations driven by interest-rate expectations may be suppressing the share price independently of any company-specific development.

The most recent annual report does not obviously support a 6.4% long-run growth rate. Cloud revenue grew 22%, the total business grew 17%, and the forward contract backlog (the contracted revenue not yet recognised, expected within the next twelve months) grew 20%. These figures are consistent with the base case, not with a stalled-growth scenario.

What would need to happen for the price to catch up to our view: the net expansion rate would need to hold above 110% for several more quarters, and the free cash flow margin would need to keep expanding toward the guided 21.5% level. If those two things happen and the discount rate on software cash flows compresses modestly, the gap between $62 and $101 would close without requiring any revision to the growth assumption.

What's Wobbling

The outstanding wobble is the thin cushion on stock-based compensation. At 17.16% of revenue, the figure is below the 18% ceiling, but the buffer is only 84 basis points — and in absolute terms, compensation expense grew $40.7 million in the year ending April 2026. That growth was absorbed because revenue grew faster, but a quarter or two of softer revenue could push the ratio back toward the threshold. It has now been below 18% for two consecutive fiscal years, so the exit condition (which requires two consecutive years above 18%) is not triggered — but the watch item is active.

The competitive position driver is classified as silent, meaning the filing itself provides no data to evaluate it. The 10-K names Datadog as a competitor in observability but gives no comparative growth numbers. External competition analysis places the current Datadog growth gap at approximately 11 percentage points, below the 15-point trigger, and confirms Elastic retained its Gartner Observability Leader status. That evidence is not from the SEC filing, so it cannot be treated as confirmed — it is a signal that requires monitoring at each Datadog earnings release.

A broader cloud-market expansion risk is worth naming: if enterprise technology spending slows sharply, Elastic Cloud growth could decelerate more quickly than the base case assumes. That is not a condition in the written thesis, but it is the macro context within which the individual drivers would deteriorate first.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • The bear scenario — permanent loss of roughly half the invested capital at 20% probability. The bear case is not a tail event; it sits at one-in-five odds. AWS OpenSearch closes the competitive gap in self-managed installations; the Elastic Cloud product loses growth momentum; the net expansion rate drops below 105% for two consecutive quarters; the free cash flow margin falls to the 14% floor. Each step is plausible individually; the bear case requires them in combination.
  • Stock-based compensation re-accelerates past 18%. The current 17.16% rate is close to the ceiling. If revenue growth softens while headcount costs remain elevated, the ratio could breach the threshold before management can adjust. A second consecutive year above 18% would trigger the written exit condition.
  • The GAAP profitability target slips past April 2028. The operating margin is improving at roughly 180 basis points per year. If research-and-development spend is pulled forward to defend against AI-native competitors, the improvement could stall. Exit condition 6 is time-bound and would fire if the target year passes without result.
  • Datadog widens the growth gap past 15 percentage points for two consecutive fiscal years. At 11 points currently, the gap would need to widen materially and sustain over two full fiscal years — plus a simultaneous Gartner Magic Quadrant downgrade — to trigger the exit condition. The double requirement makes it hard to trigger accidentally, but the trajectory of the gap warrants watching.
  • A large acquisition at an inflated price. The $500 million threshold and the 8-times-revenue multiple cap are objective and observable. The remaining $160 million of the buyback authorisation is well below the threshold, but any incremental capital allocation decision at elevated prices would be scrutinised against exit condition 5.

The Rules

The following rules are pre-committed and apply as written. Plain-English action verbs are used throughout; the rules do not grade or rank positions.

  • If the thesis is confirmed intact (no exit condition triggered, all drivers holding or silent): hold the existing position. Do not add unless the yield crosses 5.5% (approximately $56 per share on the audited free cash flow basis). Monitor the net expansion rate and cloud revenue quarterly; check the stock-based compensation ratio at each annual filing.
  • If the thesis is weakened (a driver is deteriorating but no exit condition has triggered): do not add. If the net expansion rate drops toward 107% or cloud revenue growth decelerates below 15% for two consecutive quarters, re-run the validation and assess whether to reduce. One quarter of deterioration is insufficient to confirm a structural break — two consecutive quarters are required before acting.
  • If the thesis is broken (a written exit condition has triggered): exit the full position within 30 days. The seven exit conditions are enumerated in The Case for the Bet. No averaging down on a thesis break.
  • If the share price falls below the buy edge (yield above 5.5%, approximately $56): confirm the thesis is intact. If it is, add to the position in tranches — no more than 50% of the target size in a single addition. The bear scenario (roughly −49% at 20% probability) warrants staged entry with re-confirmation between tranches.
  • If the share price rises above the trim edge (yield below 2.2%, approximately $140): trim 25% of the position to harvest the premium above fair value while keeping the core holding.
  • If the share price rises above the exit edge (yield below 1.5%, approximately $200): exit the entire remaining position. At that price, the market is paying for the bull-case scenario in full, and the asymmetry of further holding has inverted. No re-entry within 90 days to reduce the effect of anchoring on the exit price.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
