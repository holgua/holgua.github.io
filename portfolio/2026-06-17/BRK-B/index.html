Berkshire: The Bet Still Works

The Bottom Line

The bet on Berkshire is that Greg Abel and the culture Warren Buffett built will keep compounding retained earnings and insurance float into high-returning businesses at a pace the average investor cannot replicate independently. As of the latest quarterly filing, that bet is working. The share price sits right at fair value — not cheap enough to add fresh capital confidently, but not expensive enough to prompt any action. The one thing to watch is whether the modest deterioration in GEICO's underwriting results over the first three months of 2026 is a temporary blip caused by an unusually clean prior-year comparison, or an early sign of competitive pressure from rivals using more sophisticated pricing technology.

About Berkshire

Berkshire Hathaway is a holding company — a parent organisation that owns dozens of separate businesses outright and holds a large portfolio of publicly traded shares on top of that. The revenues flow in from three reinforcing directions.

The first and most distinctive is insurance. Berkshire owns some of the largest insurance operations in the world, including GEICO (the car insurer sold directly to consumers without agents), a reinsurance business called BHRG (which insures other insurance companies against catastrophic losses), and BH Primary (which writes commercial and specialty lines). Insurance businesses collect premiums from policyholders before they pay out claims. The gap between money collected and money paid out is called float — and Berkshire's float currently stands at roughly $177 billion. The remarkable thing is that Berkshire has consistently been paid to hold this float: underwriting profits mean the cost of holding that money is negative, turning insurance into a source of free investment capital.

The second direction is infrastructure. Berkshire owns BNSF, one of the two major freight railway networks serving the western half of the United States. It also owns Berkshire Hathaway Energy, a collection of regulated electric and gas utilities primarily in the western and midwestern states. Both businesses earn predictable returns on their assets because regulators set the rates they can charge — they are not glamorous but they are durable.

The third is a set of wholly owned industrial businesses — Precision Castparts (aerospace components), Lubrizol (chemicals), Marmon (industrial equipment), Pilot (truck-stop fuel and services), and many smaller operations — alongside a publicly traded equity portfolio worth roughly $298 billion, concentrated heavily in a handful of names including a major US bank, a consumer-goods company, a Japanese trading conglomerate, and an oil company.

What binds these together is a single central idea: capital allocation. Every dollar earned by these businesses is reinvested by whoever is managing the parent — historically Warren Buffett, and as of 2026, Greg Abel.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that the people running Berkshire will continue to reinvest retained earnings and the insurance float into businesses and securities that earn better long-run returns than the average investor could achieve by doing their own investing.

For that bet to keep working, four conditions must hold:

  1. Book value per share must keep growing at a rate that matches or beats the MSCI World stock index over any rolling five-year window. Book value per share (the accounting value of the company's net assets divided by the number of shares) is the best available proxy for per-share compounding. If it consistently trails the index, the capital allocation edge has evaporated and the case for owning Berkshire over a cheap index fund collapses.

  2. The insurance float must remain large and near-zero in cost. If Berkshire's underwriting deteriorates to the point where float costs money rather than paying the company to hold it, the economic engine that makes the whole structure work is gone.

  3. No reckless large acquisition must emerge. Specifically, a transaction exceeding $20 billion at more than 1.5 times the book value of what is being bought, or one that subsequently requires a goodwill write-down of more than $5 billion, would signal that Abel lacks the capital discipline that made Berkshire's compounding record what it is.

  4. The decentralised owner-operator model must stay intact. A regular dividend, a break-up of the company into separate listed entities, or a capitulation to activist pressure — any of these would signal that the culture of patient, decentralised compounding has been abandoned.

There is also a valuation ceiling: if the share price exceeds twice the book value of the company, the market is pricing in aggressive compounding optimism that historically does not leave room for further outperformance. At that level, the opportunity cost of holding Berkshire versus a cheaper alternative becomes clearly negative, and the rules call for selling.

The Rules

The rules pre-commit a response for each combination of thesis state and share price:

  • If the thesis is confirmed and the share price is within the normal range: hold without acting. Let the compounding work.
  • If individual drivers are showing signs of weakening but no exit condition is triggered: do not add to the position. Reassess after the next quarterly filing.
  • If an exit condition is triggered: sell the full position within thirty days. No averaging down.
  • If the share price falls into the cheap-to-buy range (below $417): check first that the thesis is still intact, then add to the position.
  • If the share price rises into the expensive range (above $584): trim a quarter to a third of the position to harvest the premium, keeping the core holding.
  • If the share price rises to the extreme ceiling (above $651): sell the full position. At that level, the market is pricing in roughly ten years of base-case compounding instantly; the implied forward return falls below what a passive index offers, and redeploying the proceeds makes more sense than staying.

The Conditions Today

Book value growth. Shareholders' equity grew 10.5% in the full year 2025 — from $649 billion to $717 billion — and then rose a further $9.8 billion in the first three months of 2026 to reach $727 billion. That translates to roughly $337 of book value per Class B share. The growth is comfortably above what the MSCI World index returned over the same period, so this condition is reading cleanly.

Insurance float and underwriting. The float reached approximately $176.9 billion by the end of the first quarter of 2026, up $500 million from year-end. More importantly, the cost of holding it remained negative — meaning the insurance operations collectively generated underwriting profit on top of the investment income from deploying the float. This condition is reading cleanly.

BNSF railroad. Revenue in the first three months of 2026 rose 4.8% to just under $6 billion, and pre-tax earnings rose 13.4% to $1.82 billion, driven by higher average revenue per freight car and lower fuel costs. No regulatory news emerged about any merger between Berkshire's two main railroad competitors that would alter the western-rail competitive landscape. This condition is reading cleanly.

Berkshire Hathaway Energy. The Oregon Court of Appeals reversed a major court verdict against PacifiCorp (Berkshire's Pacific-Northwest utility) related to wildfire liability, materially reducing the worst-case legal exposure that had hung over this segment for several years. PacifiCorp also agreed to sell its Washington-state operations to Portland General Electric for $1.9 billion in cash, further simplifying the regulated-utility portfolio. Energy segment earnings in the first quarter came in at $744 million, modestly above the prior year. This condition is reading positively.

Operating earnings. Pre-tax operating earnings (stripping out the volatile mark-to-market swings in Berkshire's public equity portfolio) came in at $12.4 billion for the first three months of 2026, up 6.5% from the same period a year earlier. This condition is reading cleanly.

Share repurchases. Berkshire repurchased approximately $235 million of its own shares in the first quarter of 2026. Management described this as a "relatively minor amount," which is accurate — at a price close to what the company considers fair value, meaningful buybacks would only make sense if the price were clearly below intrinsic value. The restraint reflects discipline, not a change in policy.

Management succession. No succession-related disclosures appeared in the first-quarter filing. Greg Abel continues as chief executive without any reported leadership changes.

The Share Price

The share price at the time of the most recent analysis was $484 per share. Using a price-to-book valuation method — which compares the share price to the accounting value of the company's net assets per share — the price implies a ratio of 1.45 times book value. The rules set fair value at 1.45 times book. In plain terms: the share price is sitting almost exactly at fair value.

The buy-band edge sits at 1.25 times book, which translates to a share price of roughly $417. The trim-band edge sits at 1.75 times book, which translates to roughly $584. The exit ceiling sits at 2.0 times book, which translates to roughly $651.

At $484, the share price is within the fair-value band — not cheap enough to trigger a buy signal and not expensive enough to trigger a trim or exit signal.

It is worth noting that Berkshire's own management uses book value as one internal gauge of intrinsic value. During years when the share price fell below 1.2 to 1.3 times book — a range close to the buy-band edge set in the rules — Buffett historically accelerated share repurchases. The fact that repurchases were modest in the first quarter of 2026 is itself a signal consistent with the rules' reading: management does not consider the share price to be offering a substantial discount at this level.

What's Wobbling

The one driver reading as mixed rather than cleanly positive is GEICO's combined ratio — the standard measure of whether an insurance business is underwriting profitably (a combined ratio below 100% means the operation is profitable from insurance alone, before investment income).

GEICO's combined ratio rose to 87.3% in the first three months of 2026, up from 79.8% in the same period a year earlier. That 7.5 percentage-point rise sounds significant, but context matters: the first quarter of 2025 was unusually clean by insurance standards, with near-zero catastrophe losses. Comparing to an outlier quarter makes the 2026 reading look worse than it probably is on a normalised basis. GEICO remains solidly profitable — a combined ratio of 87.3% means that for every dollar of premium collected, the business spends 87 cents on claims and expenses, keeping 13 cents. The aggregate underwriting result across all of Berkshire's insurance operations actually improved year-on-year, with total pre-tax underwriting earnings rising to $2.27 billion from $1.72 billion, because the other insurance businesses (the reinsurance operation and BH Primary) more than offset GEICO's step back.

Where the GEICO question has teeth is not in a single quarter's combined ratio but in the competitive context. Progressive, GEICO's main direct-to-consumer rival, has invested heavily in telematics (technology that monitors individual driving behaviour to price insurance more precisely). If Progressive is consistently attracting safer drivers while GEICO's pool skews toward riskier ones, the competitive position could deteriorate structurally over time — not in one quarter but over several years. The first-quarter filing does not disclose how many policies GEICO has in force (a key market-share indicator), so that question cannot be answered from current data.

Share repurchases read as mixed for a different reason: the amount ($235 million in the first quarter) is small relative to the scale of the company. This is not a concern — it reflects appropriate restraint when the share price is at fair value. It becomes a concern only if repurchases remain minimal even when the share price drops materially below fair value, which would suggest management no longer views buybacks as an efficient use of capital.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Abel deploys the cash badly. Berkshire held approximately $369 billion in cash and short-term government securities at year-end 2025. That is roughly 35% of the company's market value sitting in low-return assets, waiting to be deployed. If Abel acquires businesses at prices that destroy capital — paying too much for assets that do not earn their cost of capital — the compounding flywheel that has driven Berkshire's returns over decades could slow materially. This is the single largest forward-looking risk.

  • A large insurance catastrophe year. A sequence of hurricanes, earthquakes, or wildfires beyond historical norms could produce underwriting losses that erode the float advantage. The insurance businesses are built to survive bad years, but a truly catastrophic event cluster could dent operating earnings materially for one to two years.

  • GEICO loses market share to telematics-driven rivals. If Progressive's pricing precision gives it a sustained advantage in attracting low-risk drivers, GEICO's combined ratio could deteriorate structurally over several years — not as a one-quarter blip but as a trend. This would not trigger an exit condition immediately, but it would qualify as a thesis-weakening signal.

  • The equity portfolio contracts sharply. Roughly $298 billion of Berkshire's balance sheet consists of publicly traded shares. A severe broad market decline would reduce this figure substantially, lowering book value and the implied intrinsic value floor that cushions the downside case.

  • The renewable energy pipeline at Berkshire Hathaway Energy faces regulatory headwinds. Federal legislation enacted in mid-2025 accelerated the phase-out of tax credits that supported BHE's renewable buildout. This affects the growth pipeline at BHE, not the existing rate-based earnings, but it reduces one source of incremental returns that had been factored into the base case.

Gaps in the Picture

The share-count used in the valuation relies on an estimate derived from dividing the market capitalisation by the share price, rather than being taken directly from accounting filings. Berkshire's multi-class share structure — Class A and Class B shares with a 1,500-to-1 conversion ratio — is not cleanly captured by the automated data-extraction tools used in this pipeline. The figure used (approximately 2,150 million Class B equivalent shares) is cross-checked against the disclosed book value and appears consistent, but a direct read from the filing's share-count disclosure would be more precise.

Policies-in-force data for GEICO — the most direct measure of whether GEICO is gaining or losing market share against Progressive — is not disclosed in the quarterly report. It appears in the annual shareholder letter. The market-share question therefore carries a data gap that cannot be closed until annual data is available.

The valuation uses year-end 2025 book value ($717 billion) as the denominator. The most recent filing (for the first quarter of 2026) reports equity of $727 billion as of 31 March 2026. Using the updated figure would imply a slightly lower price-to-book ratio of approximately 1.44 — marginally below fair value rather than exactly at it. The difference is not material to the overall call.
