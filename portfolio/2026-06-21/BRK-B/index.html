The bet is working. The central premise — that the management culture and capital-allocation discipline built over decades will continue to compound retained earnings and insurance float at rates that reward patient ownership — is intact across the nine conditions checked against the latest quarterly filing. About Berkshire

Berkshire Hathaway is a holding company — a corporation that owns other businesses rather than making or selling things directly. It is structured as a permanent capital vehicle: Berkshire buys companies and securities with the intention of holding indefinitely, reinvesting the earnings from one business to fund the next.

The engine room is insurance. Berkshire owns GEICO (one of the largest car insurers in the United States), General Re (a global reinsurer), and a collection of speciality insurance businesses. Insurance companies collect premiums before claims arrive, which means they hold a large pool of other people's money — called float — that they can invest in the meantime. Berkshire's float stood at approximately $177 billion in the three months ending March 2026, and the cost of holding it was negative: Berkshire was, in effect, paid to hold $177 billion. This is the foundation of the entire investment case.

On top of insurance, Berkshire owns BNSF (Burlington Northern Santa Fe), one of the two largest freight railways in the western United States; Berkshire Hathaway Energy, a regulated electricity and gas utility operating across multiple US states; Precision Castparts, a manufacturer of aerospace components; and dozens of smaller industrial, consumer, and retail businesses. It also holds a $298 billion portfolio of publicly traded shares in companies including Apple, American Express, Coca-Cola, and Chevron.

The company was shaped over six decades by Warren Buffett, who handed the chief executive role to Gregory Abel on 1 January 2026. Buffett remains chairman. Abel built his career running Berkshire Hathaway Energy and is by background an operator rather than a portfolio manager. His capital-allocation track record at the parent level is still short, and how he deploys Berkshire's $369 billion cash and treasury-bill fortress — roughly a third of the company's market value — is the central question for the next decade.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that the management culture and decentralised operating model built at Berkshire will continue to compound retained earnings and insurance float at rates that exceed what a passive investment in a broad stock-market index could achieve. For the bet to keep working, nine conditions must continue to hold:

  1. Book value per share grows at least as fast as the MSCI World total-return index over any rolling five-year window. Book value (total assets minus total liabilities, per share) is used as a proxy for the underlying compounding rate. The exit condition fires if this benchmark is trailed for five or more consecutive years.

  2. Insurance float cost remains near zero or negative. Berkshire borrows the float implicitly by collecting insurance premiums before paying claims. If the cost of float rises above what Berkshire earns on it for an extended period, the central economic advantage disappears.

  3. Aggregate underwriting profit is maintained. The combined ratio — the percentage of premium income paid out as claims and expenses — must stay below 100% on average to confirm that the float is free rather than expensive.

  4. Operating earnings (excluding mark-to-market swings in the investment portfolio) continue to grow. Berkshire's reported net income is dominated by the accounting value of its stock portfolio, which rises and falls with the market regardless of whether Berkshire buys or sells anything. Operating earnings strip that out and measure the actual earning power of the businesses.

  5. Share repurchases are conducted only when the price is below intrinsic value. Berkshire does not pay a dividend. Instead, capital not deployed into acquisitions or operating-business reinvestment should be returned through buybacks — but only when the price is genuinely below what the business is worth. Buying back shares above intrinsic value destroys compounding.

  6. Management succession proceeds without disruption to the decentralised model. The exit condition fires if a regular dividend is initiated, if the company is broken up, or if an activist investor forces a structural change that abandons the decentralised culture.

  7. BNSF maintains its competitive position in western US freight rail. The watch item is a potential merger between Union Pacific and Norfolk Southern, which the Surface Transportation Board would need to approve. Such a merger would end BNSF's western-rail duopoly and alter its competitive standing.

  8. Berkshire Hathaway Energy's regulatory and legal environment remains manageable. The watch items are PacifiCorp's wildfire liability exposure in Oregon and Washington, and the phase-out of renewable energy tax credits under legislation enacted in July 2025.

  9. GEICO maintains or grows its market share against Progressive and other competitors. Progressive has invested heavily in telematics-based pricing — systems that set premiums based on actual driving behaviour measured by a phone app or in-car device. If GEICO's policies-in-force trend declines persistently, it signals a competitive disadvantage that the combined ratio alone cannot capture.

The Rules

The pre-committed rules for what to do when conditions shift are:

  • If all conditions are working and the price is within the normal band: hold. No action. Let it compound.
  • If any condition is deteriorating but no exit condition has fired: do not add to the position. Re-run the validation against the next quarterly filing before deciding.
  • If any exit condition fires: exit the full position within 30 days. No averaging down.
  • If the share price falls to or below the cheap edge ($417): confirm the thesis is intact first. If confirmed, add to the position.
  • If the share price rises to or above the trim edge ($584): trim 25–33% of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.
  • If the share price rises to or above the exit edge ($651): exit the full position. At that price the market is pricing in roughly ten years of base-case compounding instantly; the implied forward return falls below the long-term equity premium, and reallocation becomes the better call. No re-entry within 90 days.

The Conditions Today

Book value growth. Berkshire's total shareholders' equity grew 10.5% in the twelve months ending December 2025, rising from $649 billion to $717 billion. In the three months ending March 2026, equity grew a further $9.8 billion to $727 billion — implying a book value per share of approximately $337. No MSCI World comparison data is available in the current filing, but the 10.5% annual growth rate is above the long-run equity-market average. The condition is working.

Insurance float. Float reached approximately $177 billion at the end of March 2026, up $500 million from December 2025. The cost of float was negative, meaning Berkshire earned more on the investments funded by float than it paid out in insurance claims above premiums. The condition is working.

Aggregate underwriting. The picture here is mixed. GEICO's combined ratio rose to 87.3% in the three months ending March 2026, from 79.8% a year earlier — driven by higher claims frequency and severity. However, GEICO's ratio is still well below 100%, meaning it remains solidly profitable. More importantly, Berkshire's other insurance businesses (its general reinsurance and speciality businesses) more than offset the GEICO deterioration: the aggregate pre-tax underwriting result across all Berkshire insurance businesses improved to $2.27 billion from $1.72 billion a year earlier. The condition is intact on the aggregate measure, though GEICO bears watching.

Operating earnings. Pre-tax operating earnings (excluding investment gains) were $12.4 billion in the three months ending March 2026, up 6.5% from $11.6 billion a year earlier. For the full year 2025, operating income reached $20.1 billion, up 16.8% from $17.2 billion in 2024. The condition is working.

Share repurchases. Berkshire repurchased approximately $235 million of its own shares in the three months ending March 2026 — described in the filing as "relatively minor amounts." At a price near fair value (book-value multiple of approximately 1.45), this is consistent with the stated policy of buying only when shares are priced below intrinsic value by a meaningful margin. The modest pace reflects discipline, not a change in policy. The condition is working, in the sense that no capital is being returned at prices the rules would classify as too expensive.

Management succession. The quarterly filing contains no succession-related disclosure. Greg Abel continues as chief executive with no reported leadership changes. The condition is quiet — neither confirming nor challenging anything.

BNSF competitive position. The Burlington Northern Santa Fe railway generated revenues of $5.99 billion in the three months ending March 2026, up 4.8% from a year earlier, and pre-tax earnings of $1.82 billion, up 13.4%. No merger news involving Union Pacific and Norfolk Southern was disclosed during the quarter. The condition is working.

BHE regulatory environment. Two significant developments in the three months ending March 2026. First, the Oregon Court of Appeals reversed a lower-court wildfire liability verdict against PacifiCorp (Berkshire Hathaway Energy's main utility subsidiary in the Pacific Northwest), materially reducing the worst-case legal exposure that had clouded BHE's outlook since 2023. Second, PacifiCorp agreed to sell its Washington state operations to Portland General Electric for $1.9 billion in cash, further derisking BHE's regulated-utility portfolio. Pre-tax earnings from BHE were $744 million, up 1.4% from a year earlier. The condition is working.

GEICO competitive position. The quarterly filing does not disclose policies-in-force — the data point needed to track whether GEICO is gaining or losing market share against Progressive and others. What is available is the combined ratio (87.3%, discussed above) and the earnings figure. The market-share picture requires the annual letter data. The condition is a known gap rather than a confirmed deterioration.

The Share Price

At $484, Berkshire's share price implies a book-value multiple of approximately 1.45 — essentially identical to the fair-value anchor built into the thesis. The cheap-edge multiple of 1.25 translates to a share price of $417; the trim-edge multiple of 1.75 translates to $584; and the exit multiple of 2.0 translates to $651.

The book-value multiple is the lens through which the thesis is designed to be read. At 1.45 times book, the market is paying for the value of Berkshire's net assets (the insurance businesses, the railway, the energy utility, the operating subsidiaries, the cash hoard, and the equity portfolio, net of liabilities) plus a modest premium for the management's demonstrated ability to allocate capital well over time. It is not a cheap price, but it is not an expensive one either. The share price is at fair value.

One nuance: the valuation uses book value at the end of December 2025 ($717 billion) rather than the more recent March 2026 figure ($727 billion). Using the March figure would put the implied multiple at approximately 1.435 — fractionally below the fair-value anchor. That reading is slightly more favourable but does not alter the conclusion.

- A second alert at $417, the cheap edge. A crossing arms the buy order below; before adding, the latest re-validation should confirm the conditions are still working.

Standing orders (actionable).

- A standing trim order at $584. The rules call for trimming 25–33% of the position when the share price closes at or above this line, to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.
- A standing exit order at $651. The rules call for closing the full position when the share price closes at or above this line. At that reading, the market is pricing in roughly ten years of base-case compounding immediately; the implied forward return falls below the long-run equity premium even before accounting for growth, and redeploying the proceeds at the next cheap-edge crossing or into a more attractively priced alternative becomes the better call. The three order levels do not contradict the cost-basis verdicts above: those verdicts describe the current reading; the standing orders describe what the rules will execute if the share price moves to each line.

What's Wobbling

GEICO's combined ratio. The ratio rose 7.5 percentage points to 87.3% in the three months ending March 2026, driven by higher claims frequency and severity. This is the largest single wobble visible in the current filing. Two contextual points matter. First, the year-earlier quarter (March 2025) had near-zero catastrophe losses, making it an unusually clean benchmark that inflates the apparent deterioration. Second, GEICO's combined ratio of 87.3% still represents a profitable underwriting operation — premiums exceed claims and expenses by 12.7 cents on every premium dollar. The exit condition for this driver is framed as a market-share and competitive-position concern, not a single-quarter ratio print. The policies-in-force trend — the data needed to judge market share — is not separately disclosed in quarterly filings. That gap is the more important watch item than the ratio itself.

Share repurchases were modest. The $235 million repurchased in the three months ending March 2026 is small relative to Berkshire's scale. This is not a cause for concern — at a book-value multiple near fair value, restraint is the disciplined response. The risk would be the opposite: returning capital at prices above intrinsic value. The modest buyback pace reflects that no clear bargain presented itself, which is precisely what the rules call for.

The Abel track record is still short. Greg Abel completed his first full quarter as chief executive in the three months ending March 2026. The thesis depends on his ability to allocate the $369 billion cash and treasury-bill position intelligently over time. His first significant parent-level deployment — a roughly $9.5 billion transaction for OxyChem in January 2026 — is too recent for its returns to be visible. The succession driver carries no signal yet.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Abel misallocates the cash hoard. At roughly a third of the company's market value, the $369 billion in cash and treasury bills is large enough that its deployment economics will define the next decade's compound rate more than any single operating business. One or two large acquisitions priced at more than 2.5 times book value with no demonstrable competitive advantage would materially impair the compounding case.
  • A catastrophic insurance loss event. An insurance catastrophe exceeding $15 billion in a single year would not break the company — the fortress balance sheet absorbs it — but would crimp the float and sharply reduce the capital available for reinvestment for several years. The risk is permanent if combined with a credit-rating downgrade that raises the cost of the float.
  • The equity portfolio concentration. Roughly 65% of Berkshire's $298 billion public-equity portfolio is concentrated in five positions. A sustained bear market in those positions would compress book value through mark-to-market accounting, potentially triggering exit condition (4) — the price-to-book exit at 2.0 times — not because the business deteriorated but because book value fell while the share price held.
  • BHE's regulatory environment worsens. The Oregon appeals-court reversal in April 2026 materially reduced the wildfire liability, but PacifiCorp's legal exposure is not fully extinguished. Further adverse court rulings, or new wildfire seasons with significant PacifiCorp involvement, could revive liabilities that the current filing treats as substantially reduced.
  • GEICO loses sustained market share to Progressive. Progressive's telematics pricing has allowed it to attract lower-risk drivers more accurately than traditional underwriting models. If GEICO's policies-in-force decline persistently, the combined ratio improvement achieved through underwriting discipline will eventually be offset by a shrinking premium base, reducing the float that powers the investment engine.
