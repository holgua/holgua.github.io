Recent Development

The most significant development in the latest filing — for the three months ending 2026-06-30 — is the capital-return engine running at double its prior-year pace. Booking Holdings repurchased $7.8 billion of its own shares in the first half of 2026 alone, compared with $3.7 billion in the same period of 2025. With $14.5 billion of authorisation remaining and the board raising the quarterly dividend further, the per-share compounding story is intact and accelerating.

The network itself is growing at a measured pace. Room nights grew 5.3% in the second quarter — solid in absolute terms, but below the previous quarter's 6.0% and still carrying the drag of the Middle East conflict, which continued to reduce international long-haul demand even as direct impacts in the region had largely normalised by June. The $10.1 billion deferred booking pipeline at the end of June, up from $8.2 billion three months earlier, is the strongest forward indicator in the filing: travellers have already paid for future stays, which is a concrete signal of H2 demand.

Revenue grew 8.1% in the quarter and 11.4% over the first half of the year. The take rate — revenue as a share of total gross bookings — compressed slightly, by about a tenth of a percentage point, driven by a mix shift toward the merchant model rather than competitive pressure. That is the main thing to keep an eye on as the the Share Price assessment is drawn.

About Booking

Booking Holdings runs the world's largest online travel marketplace. Its main platform, Booking.com, connects roughly 4.7 million properties — hotels, apartments, guesthouses, villas, and boats — with travellers in 220-plus countries. A property lists its rooms; a traveller finds, prices, and pays for a stay; Booking collects a fee of roughly 14 cents on every dollar of the booking value. It does the same across its other brands: Priceline and Agoda serve primarily the American and Asian markets respectively, KAYAK is a flight and hotel comparison engine, and OpenTable is a restaurant-reservation platform. The company earns no revenue until a booking completes — it keeps the fee whether the traveller pays upfront (the merchant model, now 73% of the mix) or the property bills the traveller directly on checkout (the agency model).

The business is unusually capital-light. It owns no hotels, operates no flights, and employs no concierges. Its physical footprint is principally data centres and office space. Free cash flow — the cash left over after paying all expenses and investing in the technology platform — was $9.1 billion in 2025 on revenues of $26.9 billion, a margin of about 34 cents on every dollar. That cash is returned almost entirely to shareholders: in 2025, the company spent $6.4 billion buying back its own shares and paid $1.2 billion in dividends.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that Booking.com's two-sided network is durable enough to survive the structural pressures facing online travel — from AI-powered search disruption to hotel-chain direct-booking campaigns — while the capital-return engine compresses the share count and compounds per-share value. Five conditions must hold for this to remain true:

  1. Room-night growth stays above the low-single-digit threshold across multiple regions. The network effect that makes Booking.com attractive to properties depends on consistent demand-side growth. If growth falls into low single digits or turns negative across multiple geographies for two or more consecutive quarters — absent a clearly isolated external shock — it signals the demand-side flywheel is losing momentum.

Latest reading: 325 million room nights in the three months ending 2026-06-30, +5.3% year-on-year. The Middle East conflict has provided a recognised carve-out for two consecutive quarters, but Q3 2026 will be the decisive test: if growth does not recover toward 6% or above as conflict-related flight disruption further attenuates, the carve-out loses credibility.

  1. Marketing as a share of gross bookings does not rise meaningfully for two or more consecutive years. Booking.com's moat is partly a cost moat: direct bookings (mid-fifties per cent of room nights) arrive without performance-marketing spend. If the platform must spend progressively more on paid channels to defend its room-night share, it is buying growth that the network used to generate for free — a structural deterioration.

Latest reading: Marketing costs were approximately 4.70% of gross bookings in the second quarter of 2026, compared with 4.61% in the same period of 2025 — a drift of roughly 9 basis points (hundredths of a percentage point). Combined with the first quarter's 4-basis-point drift, the first half of 2026 is running modestly worse than the first half of 2025. The company attributes this to the conflict forcing cancellations and declining organic search traffic pushing spend toward paid channels. Exit condition (2) requires "two or more consecutive years" of meaningful rise — the current drift does not clear that bar, but the clock is running.

  1. The revenue take rate (revenue as a share of gross bookings) does not compress materially or persistently. The take rate — about 14 cents on every dollar — represents the economic value Booking captures from each transaction. Persistent compression would signal that competitors, regulators, or AI agents are reducing that pricing power.

Latest reading: 14.43% in the second quarter of 2026, down 10 basis points from 14.53% in the same period of 2025. The compression is mechanical — the ongoing shift toward the merchant model brings higher processing costs — and management confirmed that the net payment spread (revenues from facilitating payments minus the associated variable costs) remains positive over the trailing twelve months. Exit condition (3) requires "material or persistent" compression; 10 basis points over one quarter does not approach that threshold.

  1. Capital-return discipline remains intact — buybacks continue while the cash-flow yield is attractive, with no value-destructive large acquisition. The supporting bet is that management continues to shrink the share count and compound per-share value rather than destroy it with an empire-building deal.

Latest reading: $7.8 billion in share repurchases in the first half of 2026 (versus $3.7 billion in the first half of 2025), $664 million in dividends, and $14.5 billion of repurchase authorisation remaining. The Transformation Programme annual cost savings raised to $650 million run-rate. No material acquisitions. Exit condition (4) is not approaching.

  1. By the third quarter of 2027, management has articulated and visibly executed a coherent AI-first strategy. The concern is that AI-powered trip planning — from Google, OpenAI, Perplexity, and others — shifts consumer trip discovery away from Booking.com as a starting point. The exit condition requires concrete shipped product with measurable engagement, direct-traffic, or margin evidence, and explicit acknowledgement of the disintermediation risk in earnings communications.

Latest reading: The latest filing repeats the same "AI-powered traveller experience" and "Connected Trip" language as the prior quarter, with no new AI products described as having shipped. Mobile app bookings as a share of all room nights reached the high-fifties percentage — up from the mid-fifties a year earlier — but the language was identical to the first quarter's filing, suggesting the improvement has plateaued. Four quarterly cycles remain before the Q3 2027 deadline. The pressure is real; the deadline is not yet imminent.

The Share Price

At $180 per share, Booking Holdings is priced modestly below the internal fair-value estimate of $196. That estimate is derived from an owner-earnings yield (a measure of the cash the business generates relative to the price paid for it) of 5.5% — the mid-point of what has historically been a fair price for this business's cash-generation quality. At $180, the actual yield is about 6.0%, which places the share price within the hold band — neither cheap enough to call for adding more, nor expensive enough to suggest trimming.

The gap to fair value is approximately 8%, and the market appears to be pricing in long-run growth of roughly 4% per year — a meaningfully more pessimistic assumption than the 13% base-case growth rate used in the internal model. That divergence is large enough to be interesting but not large enough to be classified as a severe disagreement. The most straightforward reading is that the market is paying a discount for the AI-disruption risk and the regulatory overhang, both of which are real concerns but neither of which has yet produced a measurable decline in the core economics.

What's Wobbling

Room-night growth has slowed for two consecutive quarters, with the conflict justification wearing thinner. Growth of 5.3% in the second quarter of 2026 follows 6.0% in the first — both below the 6–9% trajectory that the base case assumes. The Middle East conflict has served as a recognised carve-out because direct impacts were isolated to a specific geography. But the latest filing notes that indirect effects — elevated international flight prices, reduced long-haul capacity — persisted throughout the second quarter. If Q3 results, when those indirect effects should be further attenuated, still show below-6% growth, the argument that this is a temporary isolated shock becomes harder to sustain. This is not yet at the line from The Case for the Bet — that requires low-single-digit or negative growth across multiple regions — but it is the closest of the four wobbles to mattering.

Marketing costs ticked higher again. The ratio of marketing spend to total gross bookings rose for the second consecutive quarter, driven partly by conflict-related cancellations (paid marketing spend was incurred but the booking was later cancelled) and partly by declining organic search traffic that forces displacement toward paid channels. The company's own language — "We expect SEO traffic to decline in the short to medium term" — is the clearest public acknowledgement that AI-disrupted search is already affecting the cost structure. The exit condition requires two or more consecutive years of meaningful rise. One half-year of slight drift does not clear that bar, but the pattern is worth watching closely.

The AI strategy is treading water. The filing's language on artificial intelligence is identical to the prior quarter's — no new products are described, no new engagement metrics are cited. The deadline for exit condition (6) is Q3 2027, and four quarterly cycles remain. The absence of new evidence in Q2 is not a trigger, but it is not reassuring either.

Regulatory noise has increased. The US Federal Trade Commission's staff intend to recommend a complaint against Priceline for fee-and-disclosure practices. A Spanish competition authority fine of $472 million has been recorded on the balance sheet but suspended pending appeal. Neither is at the threshold that would trigger exit condition (7) — that requires a formal decision imposing structural product remedies or a fine exceeding 3% of annual revenue (approximately $808 million in a single year). The cumulative regulatory exposure is, however, real and ongoing.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • AI disintermediation accelerates faster than the Q3 2027 deadline. If Google's AI Mode, Perplexity, or a specialist trip-planning agent achieves meaningful consumer adoption as a trip-starting interface before Booking.com has a credible response, the demand-side flywheel could begin to slow structurally rather than cyclically. The KAYAK impairment recorded in 2025 is evidence this risk is not theoretical.
  • European regulatory enforcement crosses from compliance cost to structural remedy. Six simultaneous national competition-authority proceedings and obligations under the Digital Markets Act and Digital Services Act create a complex regulatory environment. A formal product remedy — such as mandatory removal of pricing parity clauses across the EU — could structurally reduce Booking.com's economic terms with properties, compressing the take rate durably.
  • Take-rate compression proves to be competitive rather than mechanical. If hotel chains succeed in shifting meaningful booking volume back to their own direct channels, or if AI-powered booking agents begin negotiating lower commissions on behalf of travellers, the 14-cent take rate per dollar of bookings could compress in a way that no operational efficiency can offset.
  • A large value-destructive acquisition. With $14.5 billion of buyback authorisation and strong free cash flow, the company has both the means and the incentive to stay the course on capital return. But the temptation to make a transformative acquisition — perhaps to buy an AI capability rather than build it — grows as AI disruption anxiety intensifies. A poorly priced deal at scale would be structurally negative.

Gaps in the Picture

Owner earnings are computed as free cash flow (operating cash flow minus total capital expenditure), net of stock-based compensation. Because Booking Holdings does not separately disclose maintenance versus growth capital expenditure, total capital expenditure is used throughout. Given that capital expenditure was $322 million on $26.9 billion of revenue in 2025 — about 1.2% of revenue — the distinction between maintenance and growth spending is small relative to the cash-flow figures involved and does not materially affect the yield calculation.

The first-half 2026 free cash flow run-rate of approximately $13.5 billion (annualised from $6.75 billion in H1) is materially above the $9.1 billion figure for the full year 2025 used in the yield calculation. Booking's business is highly seasonal — the first half generates the bulk of the advance payments — so H2 will be lower. The owner-earnings yield of 6.0% computed on trailing 2025 free cash flow is therefore a conservative figure; if the full-year 2026 free cash flow is materially higher, the yield computed on that basis would compress toward the buy edge.

The Spain CNMC fine of $472 million (denominated in USD on the balance sheet) has been recorded as a liability but suspended pending appeal. If upheld in full, it would represent approximately 1.8% of 2025 annual revenue — below the 3% exit-condition threshold but a meaningful cash outflow. A data breach disclosed in April 2026 has no quantified financial impact in the filing.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
