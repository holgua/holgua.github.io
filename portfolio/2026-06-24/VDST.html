About the Fund

This is a fund that holds a constantly rolling basket of US government bonds maturing within the next twelve months. Run by Vanguard Group (Ireland) Limited and listed on the London Stock Exchange in US dollars, it tracks an index of Treasury bills — the short-dated IOUs issued by the US government when it borrows money for weeks or months at a time. Because the bills mature so quickly, the fund's price is remarkably stable; gains come almost entirely from the interest the bills pay while they are held. The fund is structured as an accumulating class, which means it does not distribute that interest as a dividend — instead, the income is quietly reinvested into the basket, nudging the net asset value upward day by day.

At 0.07% in annual charges, the cost of running this fund is negligible — less than a tenth of a percentage point per year, leaving almost the full yield intact. With $2.4 billion in assets, the fund is large enough to maintain liquid markets: average daily trading volume runs at around $5.5 million, adequate for routine rebalancing at typical portfolio sizes.

No tracking-difference or tracking-error figures are available from the data feed for this fund; those metrics are not surfaced for exchange-traded funds listed outside the US on standard data providers. What is known is the latest basket yield — 3.73% as of 2026-05-31, drawn from Vanguard's internal pricing system — which serves as the decision-useful signal in place of tracking quality for a vehicle this simple.

The Case for the Bet

The idea behind holding this fund is straightforward: when no equity at a fair price offers a superior risk-adjusted return, cash earns its keep. US Treasury bills are the closest thing to riskless that the financial system offers — they carry the full faith and credit of the US government, mature in under a year, and can be liquidated on short notice. The cost of sitting in this vehicle while waiting for better opportunities to appear elsewhere is just 0.07% a year in charges. That is the bet: cheap optionality in the world's safest short-term instrument.

The relevant question for this fund is not whether the business is growing or the valuation is attractive — it is whether the vehicle itself remains trustworthy. Three conditions must keep holding for the bet to work:

  1. Annual charges remain low. The fund's annual charges must not rise above 0.10% for two consecutive quarterly reviews. Comparable ultra-short-duration US Treasury funds exist from other providers at similar or lower cost; if the charge advantage erodes, the rationale for this specific fund weakens.

  2. Fund size remains substantial. Assets under management must stay above $1 billion. A fund significantly smaller than that carries liquidity and operational risk inconsistent with the cash-equivalent role. The figure is verified manually each quarter against the Vanguard product page, as automated data feeds do not reliably surface this number for funds listed outside the US.

  3. The mandate and the underlying credit quality stay intact. The fund must continue to track a basket of US Treasuries with maturities of zero to one year. If the index provider redefines the mandate, Vanguard restructures the fund, or US sovereign credit is downgraded below AA- by any two of the three major rating agencies (Fitch, Moody's, or Standard & Poor's), the cash-equivalent premise would require reassessment.

The Rules

The pre-committed rules translate the three conditions above into five branch responses, applied in order of severity:

  • If all three conditions confirm (thesis intact): hold. The vehicle is doing its job; no action required.
  • If any condition shows signs of weakening but has not breached a threshold (thesis weakened): do not add. Re-assess at the next quarterly review. Monitor charges and fund size trends before increasing the allocation.
  • If one or more numbered exit conditions has fired (thesis broken): exit immediately. Reinvest the proceeds into EUR cash or short nominal German government bonds.
  • If the basket yield rises to 5.5% or above (below the buy edge in price terms, at $40 or lower): add at the margin, if cash is available. At that yield, US Treasury bills are unusually attractive relative to EUR alternatives. Vehicle-integrity conditions still gate any large addition.
  • If the basket yield falls to 3.5% or below (above the trim edge in price terms, at $63 or higher): trim at the margin. At that yield, the carry advantage over EUR cash has compressed materially.

That gap is not an alarm. What matters more is the direction: the 3.73% yield has drifted 107 basis points below the 4.8% base case, sitting considerably closer to the 3.5% bear-case floor than to the 5.5% bull-case ceiling. The Federal Reserve has been cutting rates from the 2023–2024 highs, and the short end of the yield curve has followed. What's Wobbling

Only one thing warrants attention, and it has not crossed a threshold. The basket yield at 3.73% has drifted materially below the 4.8% base-case anchor over the past year, reflecting Federal Reserve rate cuts that have pulled short-term US rates lower. With 23 basis points of cushion before the trim floor, another round of cuts of a quarter percentage point or more would push the yield into trim territory. That would not break the thesis — the vehicle-integrity conditions (charges, fund size, mandate) would remain intact — but it would call for a modest reduction in the allocation.

The methodology-stability condition generates no signal in the automated pipeline; that is expected and is not a wobble. It is simply not detectable without a human checking regulatory filings. Nothing in the current snapshot suggests the mandate, the fund structure, or US sovereign credit quality has changed.

What Could Break the Fund

  • Aggressive Federal Reserve cutting. If the Fed cuts rates to 2% or below, the basket yield would follow, likely falling below the 3.5% trim floor. A sustained period at those levels would also compress the yield well below the EUR cash alternative, weakening the argument for holding the position at all. This is the most plausible near-term risk given current macroeconomic dynamics.
  • Fee creep. If Vanguard raises the annual charges above 0.10% for two consecutive quarters, the fund's cost advantage over alternatives disappears. Comparable funds from iShares and State Street exist; a fee rise would prompt a switch rather than an exit from the T-bill mandate.
  • Fund shrinkage below the liquidity floor. If assets under management fall below $1 billion — through sustained outflows or a merger of the fund class — liquidity in the secondary market could deteriorate. This is a low-probability scenario for a $2.4 billion fund with a major institutional sponsor, but it is a written exit condition.
  • US sovereign credit deterioration. A downgrade of US Treasury debt below AA- by two of the three major rating agencies would require a fundamental reassessment of the cash-equivalent premise. US Treasuries currently carry the highest short-term credit ratings from all three agencies; getting to a double-downgrade would require a significant and visible deterioration in US fiscal policy.
  • Currency mismatch. The fund is denominated in US dollars, while the wider portfolio is EUR-based. A sustained strengthening of the euro against the dollar would erode the EUR value of the position even if the underlying yield held steady. This is an implicit cost of using a USD T-bill vehicle as a cash placeholder in a EUR portfolio.
