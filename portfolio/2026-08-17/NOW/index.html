About ServiceNow

ServiceNow sells software that automates the routine tasks that keep large organisations running: a service desk query, a new-employee onboarding form, a software vulnerability that needs routing to the right team, a finance approval that has to traverse three departments before it is settled. The platform is sold on an annual subscription, billed upfront, and designed so that the workflows a company builds on it become deeply embedded in day-to-day operations — the longer a customer has been on the platform, the harder it becomes to replace.

The company generates almost all of its revenue from subscriptions (roughly 97% of the total in the most recent full year), and the cash it collects from customers upfront consistently runs ahead of the profit it reports under accounting rules — producing a free cash flow margin of around 34% in the most recent full year against total revenues of $13.3 billion. The key complication in reading those numbers is stock-based compensation: ServiceNow pays its employees partly in shares, which costs shareholders roughly $1.95 billion a year in dilution even though no cash leaves the business. Stripping that out, the "owner earnings" available to long-term shareholders work out to approximately $2.6 billion per year — the figure the valuation framework uses as its anchor.

The Case for the Bet

The bet on ServiceNow rests on the proposition that its workflow-automation platform is structurally embedded in enterprise IT, and that seven consecutive years of 98% renewal rates are evidence of a moat that will persist through AI disruption. The conditions that must keep holding for the bet to work are:

  1. Subscription revenue growth stays above 15% year-on-year for two consecutive quarters. Below 15% for two quarters in a row signals that AI commoditisation is winning and the business is losing its expansion trajectory.

Latest reading: 24.6% year-on-year growth in the three months ending 2026-06-30; 23.4% in the first half of 2026. Both well above the threshold.

  1. The net renewal rate stays at or above 97% in every reported period. The renewal rate is the single most direct reading of whether enterprise customers are staying on the platform. Seven consecutive years at 98% is the empirical backbone of the moat argument; a dip below 97% would be the first credible evidence that the moat is eroding.

Latest reading: 98% for the three months ending 2026-06-30, and 98% for the six months ending 2026-06-30. The seven-year streak is intact.

  1. Current contracted backlog growth stays above 18% year-on-year for two consecutive quarters. The contracted backlog (the portion of the total obligation pipeline due within twelve months) is the forward pipeline for near-term revenue recognition. Sustained deceleration below 18% signals that new bookings are no longer replenishing the growth.

Latest reading: The current-year portion of the total contracted backlog grew 21% year-on-year as of 2026-06-30. Above the threshold.

  1. The Now Assist AI product reaches $2.5 billion in annual contract value by the fourth quarter of 2026. Now Assist is the AI monetisation layer built on top of the existing platform — if it stalls, the primary incremental growth driver above the base platform is gone.

Latest reading: The quarterly filing for the three months ending 2026-06-30 provides no quantitative Now Assist annual contract value disclosure. The last known figure, from earnings call commentary referenced in the prior re-validation, was over $1 billion. This condition cannot be assessed from the filing alone.

  1. The GAAP gross profit margin stays above 70% for two consecutive quarters. Below 70% for two quarters in a row signals that the operational cost of absorbing recent acquisitions is outpacing efficiency gains, impairing the operating leverage that underpins the long-run margin thesis.

Latest reading: 70.7% for the three months ending 2026-06-30 — only 70 basis points above the threshold. Down from 77.5% in the same quarter a year earlier, driven by roughly $194 million per quarter in amortisation of acquired intangible assets from the Armis and Veza transactions. This is the most critical condition to monitor before the next quarterly filing.

  1. The Department of Justice investigation does not result in a debarment notice or formal exclusion proceeding targeting federal contracts. A debarment order would directly impair approximately 11% of revenues and the federal moat anchored by the September 2025 government-wide AI platform agreement.

Latest reading: The investigation is ongoing as of 2026-07-23. The filing confirms continued production of documents to the Department of Justice, the Department of Defence Inspector General, and the Army Suspension and Debarment Office. No debarment action or consent order has been initiated. The revenue concentration through the single federal reseller, Carahsoft, rose to 13% of total revenues in the period (from 11% in the comparable prior period), increasing the materiality of this condition.

  1. The non-GAAP operating margin stays above 28% for two consecutive quarters. Non-GAAP operating margin excludes stock-based compensation and acquisition-related charges; below 28% for two quarters signals that AI investment is consuming economics faster than it generates incremental revenue.

Latest reading: 29.4% for the three months ending 2026-06-30; 30.6% for the six months ending 2026-06-30. Both above the threshold, though compressing year-on-year due to elevated stock-based compensation and acquisition-related costs.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • AI platform commoditisation. Microsoft, operating from within existing enterprise software licences, and Salesforce, through its agentic AI product, are both attempting to extend into workflow automation. If either succeeds in reducing the frequency of ServiceNow licence renewals or in blocking new workload expansions, subscription revenue growth could fall toward the 12% range assumed in the bear case — triggering condition 1 within two quarters.
  • Acquisition integration failure. Three large acquisitions — Armis (security, roughly $6.4 billion), Moveworks (AI chatbot, roughly $2.85 billion), and Veza (identity governance, roughly $1.2 billion) — are being integrated simultaneously. Goodwill on the balance sheet reached $9.8 billion as of 2026-06-30. If integration proves slower than expected or forces write-downs, earnings power and operating margins could both disappoint.
  • Post-acquisition leverage. The Armis transaction introduced roughly $7.5 billion in gross debt. Interest coverage is comfortable at current earnings levels, but a revenue deceleration combined with acquisition-related cost inflation would reduce that cushion quickly.
  • Department of Justice debarment. An adverse finding in the Army Chief Information Officer investigation that leads to a debarment order would directly remove roughly 11% of revenues and could impair the broader federal market expansion thesis anchored to the government-wide AI platform agreement.
  • Stock-based compensation dilution. Annual stock-based compensation of roughly $1.95 billion — equivalent to 14.7% of total revenue in the most recent full year — means the cash earnings available to long-term shareholders are materially lower than the reported cash flow figures suggest. If the business does not grow fast enough to dilute the dilution, per-share owner earnings stagnate even as the business grows.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
