Recent Development

The three months ending in June 2026 brought two distinct storylines. The first is straightforwardly good: Berkshire's railroad subsidiary, BNSF, posted earnings 13.7% higher than the same period a year ago, with its operating ratio (the share of revenue consumed by costs — lower is better) improving to 65.5% from 66.4%; its energy business, Berkshire Hathaway Energy, grew earnings 11.3% year-on-year; and Greg Abel's team repurchased $4.5 billion of stock in the quarter alone — the largest single-quarter buyback in recent memory, signalling that management views the current share price as below the business's intrinsic value. Shareholders' equity reached $747.9 billion at 2026-06-30, up roughly 12% per share year-on-year.

The second storyline is the one that moves the verdict. Berkshire's car-insurance business, GEICO, recorded a combined ratio — a measure of claims and expenses against premiums, where below 100% means profit — of 89.3% in the first six months of 2026, compared with 81.7% in the same period of 2025. That 7.6 percentage-point deterioration spans two consecutive reporting periods and traces to rising claims frequencies and severities: bodily injury frequencies up five to seven per cent, property-damage frequencies up three to five per cent, bodily injury severities up ten to twelve per cent. Pre-tax underwriting earnings at GEICO fell 40% to $2.4 billion. Advertising expenditure surged 28%, suggesting GEICO is spending aggressively to defend market share against Progressive's telematics-driven competitive advantage — and doing so without yet producing evidence that the spending is converting to new policies.

One significant positive offset: a major legal development at the energy subsidiary. In April 2026, the Oregon Court of Appeals reversed a trial-court wildfire verdict against PacifiCorp — Berkshire's Oregon-based utility — cutting the remaining wildfire liability from $1.2 billion to $572 million. The Oregon Supreme Court has granted review with oral argument in November 2026, so some uncertainty remains; but the direction of travel for this multi-year liability overhang has turned materially better. About Berkshire

Berkshire Hathaway is best understood not as a company but as a permanently capitalised holding company — a vehicle for compounding capital across a diversified collection of wholly owned operating businesses and a large portfolio of publicly traded shares. The wholly owned businesses are grouped into three clusters: insurance (led by Berkshire's car-insurance business, GEICO; its global reinsurance arm, Berkshire Hathaway Reinsurance Group; and various primary commercial lines); regulated capital-intensive infrastructure (Berkshire's railroad, BNSF, covering the western United States; and Berkshire Hathaway Energy, a regulated utility with operations across electricity generation and transmission); and a broad manufacturing, services, and retail arm spanning industrial components, chemicals, consumer goods, and retail chains. On top of those operating businesses, Berkshire holds a public equity portfolio worth more than $300 billion, anchored by a large stake in Apple.

What holds these pieces together is the insurance float. When customers pay premiums but claims have not yet been paid, the cash in between — the float — belongs to Berkshire to invest. Berkshire's combined insurance operations held roughly $177 billion of float at 2026-06-30, and the cost of that float was negative: the insurance businesses collectively generated underwriting profits, meaning Berkshire was effectively paid to hold the money. That float, deployed over decades into BNSF, Berkshire Hathaway Energy, the equity portfolio, and a rotating series of acquisitions, is the structural engine behind the compounding record. Greg Abel became chief executive at the start of 2026, succeeding Warren Buffett who remains non-executive chairman. Abel spent 33 years at Berkshire Hathaway Energy before taking on overall responsibility; his compensation is structured as an owner-operator, with no stock awards.

The Case for the Bet

The central bet is that the management culture built over six decades — decentralised, capital-disciplined, and float-fuelled — will continue to compound book value at rates ahead of what passive alternatives can offer. Four conditions must keep holding for the bet to work:

  1. Book value per share must not persistently trail a broad market index. The exit condition fires if book value per share growth trails the broad global equity index total return for five or more consecutive years — a signal that the compounding edge has gone and the opportunity cost of holding has become negative.

Latest reading: Shareholders' equity grew to $747.9 billion at 2026-06-30, with book value per Class-A-equivalent share compounding approximately 12.1% year-on-year. That comfortably exceeds any plausible benchmark reading and is nowhere close to the five-consecutive-year deterioration test. The condition is working.

  1. Any large acquisition must demonstrate that Greg Abel has inherited Buffett's capital discipline. The specific trip-wire is a transaction exceeding $20 billion at a reported price above 1.5 times the book value of the target, or one that subsequently requires a goodwill write-down exceeding $5 billion — either would signal a cultural shift rather than a tactical misstep.

Latest reading: The most recent major transaction — the acquisition of OxyChem, closed in January 2026 — is still in its early integration phase; no impairment has been recorded. Abel's $4.5 billion repurchase in the three months ending June 2026 at prices in the $490s is a positive capital-allocation signal, consistent with acting only when price is below intrinsic value. The condition is holding, though the acquisition is too recent for a full verdict.

  1. The decentralised owner-operator model must remain structurally intact. The exit condition fires if a regular dividend is introduced, if the holding company is broken up, or if an activist capitulation signals that the model is being abandoned. All three would indicate a shift away from the permanent-capital philosophy that makes the float machine work.

Latest reading: No commentary in the three-months-to-June-2026 filing suggests any move toward dividends, break-up, or activist pressure. The buyback programme's explicit criterion — repurchases only when the chief executive believes the price is below intrinsic value, conservatively determined — reaffirms the decentralised owner-operator model. The condition is intact.

  1. The price-to-book ratio must stay below 2.0 times. Above that level the valuation fully prices in optimistic compounding and the opportunity cost versus a passive index becomes clearly negative. The current price-to-book ratio, using year-end 2025 book equity, is approximately 1.5 times — within the hold band, above the fair-value anchor of 1.45 times, and well below the 2.0-times exit threshold.

Latest reading: Price-to-book at 2026-08-10: 1.495. Fair value 1.45. Buy edge 1.25. Trim edge 1.75. No valuation exit condition is approached. The condition is working.

The Share Price

At $497, the share price sits in the upper half of the hold band and fractionally above the fair-value anchor. The valuation methodology for Berkshire is straightforward in concept: compare the share price to the book value of equity per share (the accounting measure of what the business owns minus what it owes, expressed per share), then read that ratio against a set of predetermined thresholds. A price-to-book ratio of 1.25 or below has historically been the level at which Berkshire itself has repurchased shares aggressively, treating it as a hard signal that the price is below intrinsic value. A ratio of 1.45 represents fair value. A ratio of 1.75 or above signals a premium that warrants trimming.

The current ratio of approximately 1.50 — using year-end 2025 equity — places the share price just inside the upper half of the hold band and marginally above the fair-value anchor. There is no urgency in either direction: the price is neither cheap enough to add nor stretched enough to reduce. One informational note: using the more recent 2026-06-30 equity figure (which is higher, reflecting the strong first half), the implied ratio drops to approximately 1.43 — fractionally below fair value. Both readings arrive at the same practical verdict: the share is priced at or very close to fair value.

What's Wobbling

The single wobble in the current reading is Berkshire's car-insurance business, GEICO. Its combined ratio — claims plus expenses as a share of premiums — rose to 89.3% in the first half of 2026, from 81.7% in the same period of 2025. That 7.6 percentage-point deterioration covers two consecutive reporting periods, which is the condition in The Case for the Bet that would eventually trigger concern: not a single bad quarter, but a pattern. At 89.3%, underwriting is still profitable (below 100% means the insurance operation generates more in premiums than it pays in claims and expenses), so no written exit condition has fired. But the direction is wrong and the mechanism is structural: Progressive's telematics-based pricing — technology that tracks actual driving behaviour and prices accordingly — has created an advantage that GEICO has not matched. GEICO's advertising surge (up roughly 28% year-on-year) suggests the business is spending heavily to defend market share, without yet demonstrating that new policies are following.

This does not cross any exit condition. Exit would require one of the four conditions in The Case for the Bet to fire, and the GEICO deterioration, while real, sits inside a broader insurance operation that improved year-on-year (total pre-tax underwriting earnings rose to $4.4 billion from $4.3 billion, because Berkshire Hathaway Reinsurance Group and the primary commercial lines both recovered materially). The wobble is real; it is one business unit inside a franchise worth roughly $750 billion of shareholders' equity.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • GEICO loses the competitive race to Progressive. If Progressive's telematics platform continues to attract better-risk customers, GEICO's loss ratio climbs further, eventually pushing the combined ratio above 100% — meaning the insurance operation loses money on underwriting and the float no longer comes free.
  • Greg Abel makes a capital-destroying acquisition. Fifty years of Buffett's discipline cannot be assumed to transfer automatically. A large deal — say, above $20 billion — struck at a price well above the target's book value and subsequently impaired would be the clearest signal that the cultural inheritance has failed.
  • The energy subsidiary faces renewed wildfire liability. The Oregon Court of Appeals reversed the first-phase wildfire verdict, but the Oregon Supreme Court has granted review, with oral argument scheduled for November 2026. A reversal back toward the original verdict could restore and potentially increase the liability. A new catastrophic fire event at PacifiCorp would layer additional exposure on top.
  • The equity portfolio draws down sharply. Roughly a third of the investment portfolio is concentrated in a single technology company. A sustained decline in that position would not impair the operating businesses, but it would reduce book value, which would push the price-to-book ratio up if the share price did not fall proportionately — and would generate large reported net losses under the mark-to-market accounting rules.
  • A combination of headwinds arrives simultaneously. The bear case in the Share Price section carries a 20% probability and requires BNSF volume pressure, energy subsidiary losses, portfolio drawdown, and an acquisition stumble all at once. No single headwind breaks the thesis; the combination could.

Gaps in the Picture

The share count used in the price-to-book calculation is derived from market capitalisation divided by share price — a necessary approximation because the standard financial data extracts do not map Berkshire's two-class share structure (Class A and Class B, with one Class A convertible into 1,500 Class B) to a single diluted-share figure in the way most companies report. The per-share book value in the latest quarterly filing is approximately $348 per Class B equivalent (using 2026-06-30 equity of $747.9 billion and the Class-A-equivalent share count of 1,431,693 disclosed in the filing), implying a spot price-to-book of about 1.43 — marginally below fair value. The validator uses year-end 2025 equity as the standard basis; readers should be aware the two readings differ by roughly 0.06 price-to-book ratios.

No dividend yield is recorded for Berkshire. Berkshire does not pay dividends; this is an expected data gap, not a retrieval failure.

The fair-value-bands calculation has no peer-comparable data available — no other company has a comparable structure combining insurance float, a major railroad, a regulated utility, and a large public-equity portfolio. The bands therefore rely only on Berkshire's own historical price-to-book multiples, which are sparse and internally consistent but not cross-validated against peers.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
