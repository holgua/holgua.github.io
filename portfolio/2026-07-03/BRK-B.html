Berkshire: The Bet Still Works

The Bottom Line

The bet is working. The central premise — that Greg Abel and the culture Warren Buffett spent sixty years building will keep compounding retained earnings and invested float at returns a diversified index fund cannot match — continues to hold against the latest figures. The share price sits in the middle of the hold band, at fair value: neither a bargain nor an overpay, simply a fair price for a high-quality franchise. The one condition worth watching most closely is the performance of Berkshire's car-insurance arm, GEICO, where the profitability ratio deteriorated in the three months ending March 2026 — not yet a signal that fires any exit rule, but the number to track in the next quarterly filing.

Recent Development

The headline development from Berkshire's latest quarterly filing (the three months ending March 2026) is a court victory that materially reduces the most dangerous legal overhang the company has carried for several years. Oregon's appeals court reversed a trial verdict against PacifiCorp — Berkshire's Pacific north-west power utility — that had assigned liability for wildfire damages. That reversal, combined with PacifiCorp's separately announced agreement to sell its Washington state operations to Portland General Electric for $1.9 billion in cash, means Berkshire Hathaway Energy's worst-case legal exposure is now substantially smaller than it appeared at the start of the year.

The underlying business continues to grow. Book value — the most important single figure for tracking whether the compounding thesis is working — grew 10.5% in 2025 against the prior year, and the first quarter of 2026 added a further $9.8 billion to reach $727.2 billion. Operating earnings (the segment-level profits that strip out the accounting noise from Berkshire's large publicly listed stock portfolio) rose 6.5% in the first quarter against the same period a year earlier. The freight-rail business, BNSF, was particularly strong: revenues up 4.8% and pre-tax profits up 13.4% in the quarter, aided by higher prices per car and lower fuel costs.

The single number that moved in the wrong direction — detailed in The Conditions Today — is GEICO's combined ratio (the measure of how much of every dollar of premium the insurance business pays out in claims and expenses). It rose from an unusually clean 79.8% in the first quarter of 2025 to 87.3% in the first quarter of 2026. That shift is the main item on the watchlist, though it remains well within profitable territory and the overall insurance underwriting result actually improved. For the implications for the share price, read on.

About Berkshire

Berkshire Hathaway is a holding company: it owns a collection of businesses outright and a large portfolio of publicly listed shares, and it earns money through three reinforcing mechanisms. First, it writes insurance — through GEICO (personal car insurance), General Re (reinsurance), and several other carriers — and the premiums it collects before claims are paid, called the float, sit in Berkshire's hands as an essentially free pool of capital available for investment. That float stands at around $177 billion. Second, wholly-owned operating businesses — most prominently the BNSF freight railway (one of two railroads covering the western United States), Berkshire Hathaway Energy (a regulated utility operator across six states), and Precision Castparts (aerospace components) — generate reliable earnings and steady capital growth. Third, Berkshire holds around $298 billion in publicly listed shares, with Apple, American Express, and Bank of America as the largest positions.

The combination is unusual: an insurance company whose underwriting profits fund an investment portfolio at near-zero cost, wrapped around operating businesses that generate $20 billion or more in annual profits. The accounting consequence is that Berkshire's reported net income swings wildly from one year to the next — from a loss of $22.8 billion in 2022 to a profit of $96.2 billion in 2023 — because the rules require marking the listed share portfolio to market each quarter. Those swings are largely noise. The figure that tracks actual business performance is operating income, which grew from $11.2 billion in 2022 to $20.1 billion in 2025 with no down years.

Warren Buffett, age 95, stepped down as chief executive on 1 January 2026. Gregory Abel, who spent thirty years building Berkshire Hathaway Energy from a small Iowa utility into a major regulated power operator, now runs the company. Buffett remains chairman and holds roughly 30% of the voting interest.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that Berkshire's combination of insurance float, capital allocation discipline, and decentralised operating culture will compound shareholders' equity at rates ahead of a passive global equity index over a long enough holding period.

For the bet to keep working, four conditions must hold:

  1. Book value per share keeps growing ahead of the MSCI World index. If Berkshire's equity per share trails the index for five or more consecutive years, the compounding edge the bet depends on has gone. The benchmark for comparison is the total return of the global equity market.

  2. No capital-allocation breakdown under Abel. Specifically: no single acquisition exceeding $20 billion at a reported price above 1.5 times the book value of the acquired business, and no subsequent write-down of goodwill from any deal exceeding $5 billion. A deal of that size and at that price would signal that Abel lacks the discipline Buffett demonstrated across sixty years.

  3. No structural model change. The bet rests on the decentralised, owner-operator culture: subsidiaries run themselves, capital flows to Berkshire's centre for redeployment, and no regular dividend dilutes the compounding. A regular dividend, a break-up, or an activist-forced capitulation would each signal that the model is changing.

  4. The share price stays below 2.0 times book value per share. Above that level, the valuation fully prices in optimistic compounding and the opportunity cost of holding Berkshire rather than a cheaper alternative becomes clearly negative.

The Conditions Today

Condition 1 — Book value compounding. Shareholders' equity grew 10.5% in 2025 against 2024, from $649 billion to $717 billion. The first quarter of 2026 added a further $9.8 billion. The per-share figure (expressed in Berkshire's Class B share equivalents) is approximately $337. The MSCI World comparison requires the full year's index total return, which is not in the quarterly filing; the annual check is the binding one. The trend is positive.

Condition 2 — Capital-allocation discipline. In the first quarter of 2026, Berkshire repurchased roughly $235 million of its own shares — described by management as "relatively minor amounts." That language is consistent with discipline rather than indifference: the price sat near intrinsic value, and the share repurchase rules only call for buying when shares are meaningfully below intrinsic value. Abel's first notable acquisition, OxyChem (a chemicals business at approximately $9.5 billion), is well below the $20 billion threshold and was priced rationally. No exit-condition signal is visible on this driver.

Condition 3 — Model continuity. No dividend has been introduced. No break-up has been proposed. No activist disclosure has emerged. Greg Abel continues as chief executive with the structure unchanged. Silent on the quarterly filing; no news is good news here.

Condition 4 — Valuation ceiling. The price-to-book ratio (share price divided by book value per share) stood at approximately 1.45 at the end of 2025, right at the fair-value anchor. The exit condition fires at 2.0 times book, which at current book value implies a price of roughly $667 per share. The current price of $484 is well below that level.

The wobble — GEICO's combined ratio. The combined ratio in insurance is the fraction of every premium dollar spent on claims plus operating expenses. A combined ratio below 100% means the insurance operation is profitable; above 100%, it is losing money on underwriting. GEICO's combined ratio for the three months ending March 2026 was 87.3%, up from 79.8% in the same period of 2025. That deterioration reflects higher claim frequency and severity, partly because the first quarter of 2025 had unusually few catastrophe losses, making the prior-year comparison look worse than the underlying trend warrants. GEICO remains solidly profitable. The aggregate underwriting result across all Berkshire insurance businesses actually improved — from $1.7 billion to $2.3 billion pre-tax — because other units more than offset GEICO's softer quarter. This is the condition to watch most closely going forward, though no exit rule fires on a single quarter's reading.

The Share Price

The current share price is $484. The fair-value anchor — the book-value-based price at which the valuation is neither cheap nor expensive — sits at $484 as well, making this as close to a precisely fair-value reading as the method produces.

To understand the context: Berkshire's valuation is tracked by the ratio of its share price to its book value per share (book value being the difference between assets and liabilities as recorded on the balance sheet). Over the past four-and-a-half years of monthly prices, that ratio has sat between roughly 1.32 times and 1.73 times, with a median of around 1.55 times. Today's reading of approximately 1.45 times puts the share price in the lower portion of that historical range — modestly below the long-run median, but not at a level that historically has triggered large recoveries.

The buy-band edge is 1.25 times book, which translates to a share price of $417 at today's book value. The trim-band edge is 1.75 times book, or approximately $584. At $484, the price sits comfortably between the two — in the middle of the range the rules define as normal holding territory.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Greg Abel's first large acquisition disappoints. If the next deal exceeds $20 billion at a price far above the book value of the acquired business, or if a recent deal subsequently requires a large write-down, the capital-discipline exit condition fires. The $369 billion cash reserve makes a large deal possible at any time.
  • GEICO loses the competitive race to telematics-first rivals. Progressive surpassed State Farm to become the largest US personal auto insurer in early 2026, while GEICO holds third place. If Progressive's data advantage in pricing individual drivers accurately continues to compound, GEICO's underwriting profitability could deteriorate over multiple years — a gradual erosion rather than a sudden break.
  • The Union Pacific/Norfolk Southern rail merger is approved. If the regulator approves a combination of the two eastern railroads, the result would be a combined competitor with roughly 40% of the US freight rail market, ending the comfortable western duopoly that has supported BNSF's margins for decades. The regulatory decision is not expected before 2027.
  • Berkshire Hathaway Energy's wildfire liability re-escalates. The Oregon appeals court reversal reduces the immediate exposure, but wildfire litigation is long and multi-jurisdictional. An adverse ruling in a subsequent proceeding, or a new wildfire event, could reopen the liability discussion.
  • The $369 billion cash reserve earns only Treasury-bill returns indefinitely. If Abel proves unwilling or unable to deploy the cash at returns above the roughly 5% Treasury rate, Berkshire becomes an expensive proxy for a money-market fund, and the case for paying a premium to book value weakens materially.

Gaps in the Picture

The share count used in the valuation calculation is derived from dividing the reported market capitalisation by the share price, not directly from the official share-registry data. Berkshire's two-class share structure — Class A shares worth 1,500 Class B shares each — means standard accounting databases cannot extract the diluted share count automatically. The derived figure of approximately 2,150 million Class B equivalents is consistent with management's own reported book-value-per-share disclosures and cross-checks within normal bounds, but it is an approximation rather than a directly reported number. Management discloses the per-share book value directly in annual filings, which provides the most reliable anchor.

Policies in force at GEICO — the metric that would most directly reveal whether the business is winning or losing customers against Progressive — are not disclosed in quarterly filings. Only the annual filing provides this data, meaning the competitive picture at GEICO lags by up to a year.

Gregory Abel's compensation structure under the new chief-executive arrangements is not available in the source filings reviewed here. The structure under Buffett was famously conservative; whether the same applies to the Abel-era contract is unknown.

The Rules

The following rules govern what action follows from each combination of circumstances. They are pre-committed standing instructions.

  • If the thesis is confirmed intact (all drivers holding, no exit condition triggered): hold — no action. Let it compound.
  • If the thesis is weakened (a driver deteriorates but no exit condition fires): do not add to the position. Re-run the validation against the specific deteriorating driver. Decide after the next quarterly filing.
  • If the thesis is broken (an exit condition fires): exit the full position within thirty days. No averaging down.
  • If the price falls below the buy band (share price at or below $417): confirm the thesis is intact first. If confirmed, add to the position.
  • If the price rises above the trim band (share price at or above $584): trim 25–33% of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.
  • If the price rises above the exit band (share price at or above $651): exit the full position. At that price, the market is pricing in roughly ten years of base-case compounding instantly; redeploy proceeds at the next cheap-edge crossing or into a cheaper alternative. No re-entry within ninety days.
