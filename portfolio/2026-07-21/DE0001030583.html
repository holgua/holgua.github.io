About Bund Linker 2033

The Federal Republic of Germany is one of the few sovereign borrowers in the world to have never defaulted on a euro-denominated obligation. This bond — issued in 2021 and maturing on 2033-04-15 with a nominal coupon of 0.10% — is an inflation-linked instrument: both the principal repayment and each coupon payment are scaled by the German Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices excluding tobacco (HICP ex-tobacco), which is compiled and published by Eurostat. As consumer prices rise over time, the bond's effective principal grows in step, preserving the real purchasing power of the investment. The coupon rate of 0.10% is applied to this inflation-adjusted principal, not to the original face value.

Settlement mechanics are worth understanding before comparing the quoted price to what actually changes hands. The bond trades at a quoted clean price — €95.07 as of 2026-07-20 — which is expressed in real terms, at the price levels of the issuance date. To arrive at the actual euros that transfer at settlement, that clean price is multiplied by the indexation coefficient (currently 1.26385, representing accumulated HICP inflation since issuance), and then accrued interest is added. A buyer pays the dirty price of €120.19 per €100 original face value at settlement, not €95.07. The €95.07 is the quoted clean price in real terms (same purchasing-power units as the issuance date). To get the actual euros that change hands, multiply by the index ratio (1.26385), then add accrued interest.

The Case for the Bet

The bond is held as a structural inflation hedge within a EUR portfolio. Three conditions must keep holding for that purpose to remain valid:

  1. The real yield at the current market price must remain positive — that is, the bond must not be priced to guarantee a real loss. The hold band runs from −0.50% (trim threshold, where a guaranteed real loss begins to look unattractive versus alternatives) to 1.50% (buy threshold, where the real return is highly attractive for a zero-credit-risk AAA instrument). An exit fires when the real yield has been below −1.0% for two consecutive quarters and EUR cash simultaneously offers a superior after-tax real return.

  1. Germany's sovereign credit must remain above AA− at a minimum of two of the three major rating agencies (Fitch, Moody's, S&P). A downgrade to AA− or below at any two agencies would signal a structural shift in issuer creditworthiness and trigger exit condition (2). The Federal Republic of Germany has never defaulted on a EUR obligation; the bet relies on that remaining true.

  1. The HICP ex-tobacco index must remain methodologically intact — the reference index must continue to track realised consumer-price inflation. What's Wobbling

Nothing is wobbling in the sense of approaching a threshold. The real yield at 0.86% sits in the middle of the hold band. The closest condition to watch is the ECB rate path: the ECB deposit rate of 2.25% is what determines EUR cash's real yield, and therefore the size of the linker's lead over the alternative. If the ECB were to cut rates sharply — say, to 1.0% — breakeven inflation staying at 2.10% would push EUR cash into negative real territory, which counterintuitively makes the linker look better relative to cash, but removes the cash-comparison leg of exit condition (1) as a useful signal. No ECB cut of that magnitude is in the current trajectory, but it is the variable that most directly affects whether the exit framework functions as designed.

The breakeven inflation rate of 2.10% also warrants periodic monitoring. At 2.10%, the market is pricing in roughly ECB-target-aligned inflation over the next seven years. A sustained move toward 3.50% or above would suggest the market expects the ECB to fall behind its target — a scenario in which the linker's HICP-linked accrual accelerates and the holding becomes more valuable, but in which the ECB would likely respond with higher rates that could also lift the linker's own real yield. Both outcomes would be favourable to the current position, but the monitoring condition remains relevant.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Sustained negative real yields. If the ECB cuts rates aggressively and EUR nominal yields compress, the linker's real yield could fall below −1.0% for two consecutive quarters, firing exit condition (1). This is the primary tail risk.
  • EUR cash becomes a superior real-return alternative. If ECB rate cuts end and breakeven inflation falls simultaneously, EUR cash's real yield could exceed the linker's, completing the compound trigger even at mildly negative linker real yields.
  • German sovereign downgrade. While effectively implausible under current conditions, a sustained fiscal deterioration — for example, from prolonged defence spending well above the debt-brake limits without offsetting revenue — could put German creditworthiness under review. Two agencies would need to downgrade below AA− for exit condition (2) to fire.
  • HICP methodology change. Eurostat has not proposed a material restructuring of the ex-tobacco HICP index. However, EU statistical harmonisation processes can change methodology with relatively short notice. A change that substitutes a different price index or substantially alters the basket weights could sever the link to realised consumer prices, triggering exit condition (3).
  • Liquidity thinning near maturity. As the bond approaches 2033, the outstanding float may thin, widening bid-ask spreads. This does not affect the hold thesis but can make exit execution more expensive.

The Rules

  • If all thesis conditions are confirmed and the real yield is within the hold band, hold the position. The inflation-hedge allocation is working as intended.
  • If the thesis is weakening — real yield has moved below 0% but exit condition (1) has not fired — do not add. Re-assess the real after-tax return against EUR cash and nominal Bunds before any action.
  • If the thesis is broken — one or more numbered exit conditions have fired — exit the full position and move proceeds to nominal short-duration EUR sovereign bonds or EUR cash.
  • If the real yield rises above 1.50% (clean price falls below €91), add to the inflation-hedge allocation in tranches proportional to the target allocation.
  • If the real yield falls below −0.50% (clean price rises above €104), trim 25% on the first crossing. If the yield remains below −0.50% at the next quarterly review (at least 90 days later), trim a further 25%. The ladder is bounded — further crossings do not trigger additional trims until exit condition (1) fires.
  • If the compound trigger fires — real yield below −1.0% for at least 90 days and EUR cash offering a superior real return — exit the full position and switch proceeds to EUR cash or short-duration nominal sovereign bonds; the inflation-hedge function is no longer additive versus the alternative.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
