Berkshire: The Bet Is Wobbling

The Bottom Line

Berkshire Hathaway is the sprawling holding company built by Warren Buffett over six decades and now run by Greg Abel. The latest quarterly filing shows the headline machine still turning over — book value grew again, the railway widened its margin, the insurance funding pool expanded — but the famous car insurer, GEICO, had a notably weaker quarter. The shares are trading right at the price level the written plan calls "fair", which means there is nothing cheap on offer and one moving part that now needs watching. The decision today is to hold; do not add to the position.

The Verdict

The overall call is to hold. The shares sit at the centre of their fair-value zone and one of the businesses inside Berkshire weakened in a way that matters. None of the conditions written down ahead of time that would force an exit have been crossed. So the action is the same for almost everyone: sit still and stop topping up.

No position yet — looking at fresh money at $484. Today's price, around $484, is exactly the line the plan calls fair value. That is not a level at which fresh money goes in. Two things are worth waiting for. Either the price drifts down to roughly $417, which is the level at which the shares would be cheap enough on their own to be worth buying. Or one more quarter passes and shows whether the GEICO weakness was a one-off claims spike or the beginning of something more lasting. A price alarm at $484 and another at $417 is the sensible setup; the next filing will do the talking.

Shares bought below $400 — sitting on a gain of more than 21 per cent. This is the comfortable end of the table, and the answer is to do nothing. The compounding engine that earned that gain — a growing book value, a rising railway profit, a still-expanding insurance funding pool — is intact. The wobble in GEICO does not cross any of the four exit lines written into the plan. Trimming to take profit is not called for, because the plan does not signal a partial sale until the share price climbs another twenty per cent or so above today's level. Hold and let it run.

Shares bought between $400 and $500 — roughly even today. Today's price sits inside that range. This is the largest group of shareholders and the rule for them is the bluntest: do nothing, and also do not add. The price is at fair value, the underlying businesses are still compounding, but the GEICO setback turns "do nothing" into "do nothing and do not buy more shares at this level either".

Shares bought between $500 and $600 — sitting on a mild loss of three to nineteen per cent. The answer is still to hold. A mild loss is uncomfortable but it is not a reason to sell into a wobble that has not yet broken the plan. The four written conditions for an exit remain uncrossed. The GEICO trouble is a reason to monitor the next quarter closely, not to capitulate at a loss.

Shares bought above $600 — nineteen per cent or more underwater. The answer is the hardest one to follow and also the most important: do not sell here. Selling a long-duration core holding into a quarter that wobbled but did not break is the worst possible move. Book value just grew by almost ten billion dollars in three months on top of a strong full-year gain. The railway profit rose. The insurance funding pool expanded. The underlying machine is working. Hold, watch the next quarter, and do not average down either.

About Berkshire

Berkshire is not really one company. It is roughly six things stacked on top of each other. The largest engine is insurance. GEICO sells motor cover directly to American households; the reinsurance arm writes cover that other insurers cannot or will not write themselves; and a primary commercial arm covers everything from workers' compensation to specialty lines. The collective pile of money those insurance businesses hold between collecting a premium and paying a claim is called the float, and today it stands at roughly $176.9 billion. The remarkable part is that, on average, customers are effectively paying Berkshire to hold their money — the underwriting business itself runs at a profit, so the float is free, or even slightly better than free.

Sitting alongside the insurance core are the railway BNSF (Burlington Northern Santa Fe), which runs goods trains across the western half of the United States in a duopoly with one competitor; an energy and utilities group called Berkshire Hathaway Energy, which owns regulated power and gas networks plus pipelines; a large basket of manufacturing, service and retail businesses including Precision Castparts in aerospace and Marmon in industrial goods; and a roughly $298 billion portfolio of shares in other public companies — Apple, American Express, Coca-Cola, Bank of America and many more. Finally there is about $373.5 billion sitting in cash and short-dated United States government bills, ready to be deployed. The job of Greg Abel, who took over from Buffett on the first day of 2026, is to allocate that capital — to decide what gets bought, what gets retained, what gets returned to shareholders — and that capital allocation is, in the end, the only reason this collection of businesses is worth more than the sum of its parts.

The Case for the Bet

The position is held on four conditions, all written down when the air was calm.

  1. Greg Abel and the culture Buffett built will keep reinvesting retained profit and the insurance float into businesses and shares that earn higher returns than a passive index fund.
  2. The shareholding is sized as a long-term core position, not a trade — meant to be held for many years.
  3. The exit lines are specific. The shareholding gets sold if book value per share trails a global stock index for five years in a row; if a single acquisition larger than $20 billion is paid at more than 1.5 times the target's book value, or later requires a writedown of more than $5 billion; if Berkshire's character changes structurally (a regular dividend, a break-up, a surrender to activist investors); or if the price-to-book ratio climbs above 2.0 times.
  4. As long as none of those exit lines is crossed, the position holds and the compounding does the work.

The Rules

The plan was written in advance to make the decision today mechanical rather than emotional.

If the central bet is confirmed, hold and let the position compound.

If the central bet is wobbling but not broken, do not add to the holding. Run a focused check on the specific business that is weakening, and decide again after the next quarterly filing.

If the central bet is broken, exit the full position within thirty days, with no averaging down on the way out.

If the share price falls below the cheap edge of about $417, confirm first that the central bet is still intact, and only then add to the holding.

If the share price rises above the expensive edge of about $584, trim a quarter to a third of the holding to take some premium off the table while keeping the core position.

If the price climbs all the way above the exit edge of about $651, exit the full position. At that level the market is pricing in roughly ten years of base-case compounding in advance, redeploy the proceeds at the next cheap-edge crossing, and stay out for at least ninety days to avoid anchoring back in.

The Conditions Today

Today, the wobble rule is the one that has fired. The central bet is not broken — none of the four exit lines is crossed — but one of the watched businesses has weakened materially enough that the right call is to stop adding while the next filing is awaited.

The book value, which is the simplest measure of what shareholders collectively own inside Berkshire, rose another $9.8 billion in the first three months of 2026 to $727.2 billion, on top of a 10.5 per cent gain for the previous full year. The insurance funding pool grew by about $500 million in the quarter and continues to cost less than nothing. The railway BNSF cut its operating costs as a share of revenue by 2.3 percentage points, and pre-tax profit rose 13.5 per cent. Total operating earnings across the five reporting buckets rose about 5 per cent year on year. The first material acquisition of the Abel era — OxyChem, a chemicals business, bought for about $9.5 billion in January 2026 — sits comfortably below the $20 billion size at which the plan would worry.

The problem is GEICO. Pre-tax underwriting profit at the motor insurer fell 34.8 per cent year on year to $1.416 billion. The combined ratio — a single number that measures how much of every premium dollar gets paid back out in claims and costs — worsened by 7.5 percentage points to 87.3 per cent. The cause is two things at once: drivers are filing more claims, and each claim costs more. Bodily-injury claim severity is up 12 to 14 per cent year on year. At the same time the cost of acquiring new policies rose, while premium income grew only 1.5 per cent. This is the second quarterly reading in a row pointing the same way. It is not yet a broken thesis, but it is the kind of thing the written plan was built to catch early.

One other thing is worth noting. Berkshire bought back only $235 million of its own shares in the quarter — described by management as "relatively minor" — even with $373.5 billion sitting in cash and short-term government bills. That is not a problem in itself, but it is a quiet signal: management is not seeing the current share price as materially below their own internal estimate of what the business is worth.

What's Wobbling

The wobble is GEICO. To repeat the headline number, because it is the one that matters: pre-tax underwriting profit fell 34.8 per cent year on year on a 4.9 percentage-point jump in the loss ratio and a 2.6 percentage-point jump in the expense ratio. Two readings in a row now point in the same direction. The reason this is the wobble of record and not a footnote is that GEICO is one of the four named structural advantages inside Berkshire — the direct-to-consumer motor insurer whose cost structure has historically undercut every agency-distributed competitor. If the trouble is a passing claims spike, it will fade in the next two quarters. If it is something else — a sustained loss of pricing power to a competitor running more sophisticated telematics, or a structural shift in claim frequency — it will keep showing up. The next quarterly filing will start to resolve which it is.

Two smaller wobbles are worth filing but not acting on. The United States utilities sub-segment of Berkshire Hathaway Energy saw earnings fall 16.1 per cent year on year, partly because of the step-down in renewable-energy tax credits and partly because of higher spending on wildfire prevention. And the truck-stop business Pilot swung from a small profit to a $218 million pre-tax loss on fuel-hedge timing and a one-off gain in the comparison period. Neither one is large enough to threaten the central bet.

What Could Still Go Wrong

Five things, none of them imminent, but all worth holding in mind:

  • The GEICO weakness keeps spreading for another two or three quarters and starts to look structural rather than cyclical. That would not by itself break the central bet, but it would dent the case for paying fair value rather than a meaningful discount.
  • Greg Abel makes a very large acquisition — more than $20 billion — at a price more than 1.5 times the target's book value, or one that later requires a major writedown. That is exit line number two from the written plan.
  • Wildfire liabilities at the Pacific Power and Light subsidiary in the western United States escalate beyond the roughly $2.9 billion already accrued. A favourable court ruling in April 2026 helped, but the underlying litigation has years left to run.
  • The character of Berkshire changes in some structural way — a regular dividend gets started, the conglomerate gets broken up, an activist investor extracts concessions. That is exit line number three.
  • The shares climb past 2.0 times book value while none of the underlying business momentum has improved — meaning the market would be paying for compounding that has not yet appeared. That is exit line number four.

Gaps in the Picture

Two things are worth flagging as not fully visible from the quarterly filing alone. First, the new chief executive Greg Abel has only one major capital-allocation move on the record so far — the OxyChem chemicals purchase in January 2026 — and the return on that deal will not be measurable for at least another year. Second, Berkshire's share count is awkward to read from the standard regulatory filings because of the two-class structure (Class A shares and Class B shares with a 1500-to-1 conversion ratio), so the per-share book value used to compute the price-to-book ratio relies on a derived share figure that has been cross-checked against the filing itself and matches to within roughly a third of a per cent. Neither gap changes today's call but both are worth keeping in mind when reading future quarters.
