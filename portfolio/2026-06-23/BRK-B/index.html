About Berkshire

Berkshire Hathaway is a holding company that owns, outright or through subsidiaries, four main clusters of business. The first is insurance — Berkshire's car-insurance business GEICO, the reinsurance operations of General Re and Berkshire Hathaway Reinsurance Group, and a broad array of smaller specialty insurers. These businesses collectively hold what Berkshire calls its "float": the pool of premiums collected from customers that has not yet been paid out as claims. That float — roughly $177 billion as of early 2026 — sits in Berkshire's investment account, earning returns at Berkshire's disposal for as long as the insurance policies stay in force. When underwriting is profitable, the float costs nothing; in many years it has a negative cost, meaning Berkshire is paid to hold it.

The second cluster is the railroad BNSF (the Burlington Northern Santa Fe), which moves freight across the western half of the United States on 32,500 miles of track. The third is Berkshire Hathaway Energy, a regulated utility that supplies electricity and gas across several US states and operates wind farms, pipelines, and transmission lines. The fourth is a collection of wholly-owned industrial and retail businesses — aerospace-component maker Precision Castparts, the Pilot Flying J truck-stop network, and dozens of others — alongside a roughly $298 billion portfolio of publicly traded shares in companies including Apple, Bank of America, and American Express.

The compounding logic that holds it all together: insurance generates float at near-zero or negative cost; that float, plus retained earnings from the operating businesses, is redeployed into more businesses and securities by a management team that has historically earned superior returns on capital. Warren Buffett built this model over six decades. In January 2026, Gregory Abel became chief executive; Buffett remains executive chairman.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that Berkshire's management culture — and the structural advantages of the insurance float and the railroad franchise — will continue compounding shareholders' equity at rates above what a passive global equity index would deliver. Four specific conditions must keep holding for the bet to work:

  1. Book value per share must keep growing. The specific exit line: if book-value-per-share growth trails the total return of the global equity index (the MSCI World) for five or more consecutive years, the compounding edge is gone and the position exits.

  2. Gregory Abel must preserve Buffett's capital discipline. The specific exit line: a single acquisition exceeding $20 billion at a price above 1.5 times the book value of the target, or one that subsequently requires a goodwill write-down exceeding $5 billion, signals that the discipline has broken.

  3. The decentralised owner-operator model must remain intact. Any structural change — a regular dividend, a break-up, a capitulation to activist pressure — signals that the model Buffett built has been abandoned, and the position exits.

  4. The price-to-book ratio must stay below 2.0 times book value. Above that level the market is fully pricing in optimistic compounding; the opportunity cost of staying versus a passive index becomes clearly negative, and the position exits.

The Rules

If the thesis is intact and the conditions keep working: hold without action and let the compounding run.

If any one condition shows signs of deteriorating but has not crossed an exit line: do not add to the position, keep watching the specific deteriorating driver, and revisit after the next quarterly filing.

If any condition crosses an exit line: close the full position within 30 days. No averaging down.

If the share price falls below $417 (the book-value buy threshold): confirm the thesis is intact first, then add to the position.

If the share price rises above $584 (the trim threshold): trim 25–33% of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.

If the share price rises above $651 (the exit threshold): close the full position. The market is pricing in roughly 10 years of base-case compounding instantly; the implied forward return falls below the long-run equity premium, and reallocation into cheaper alternatives makes more sense. No re-entry within 90 days to defuse the tendency to anchor on the exit price.

The Conditions Today

Book value growth is working clearly. Berkshire's shareholders' equity — the book-value base — grew 10.5% in the year ending December 2025, from $649 billion to $717 billion. In the three months ending March 2026 it rose a further $9.8 billion to $727 billion. At roughly $337 per Class B share, book value is growing at a rate comfortably above global equity returns in the same period. The five-consecutive-year exit clock has not started.

Capital allocation has so far confirmed the discipline. Gregory Abel's first notable acquisition — a stake in OxyChem at approximately $9.5 billion — is well below the $20 billion threshold and was priced rationally. Share repurchases in the three months ending March 2026 were modest ($235 million total), appropriate at a price near book-value fair value: repurchasing aggressively when the stock trades at intrinsic value would destroy value, and management said as much. The $369 billion cash and Treasury-bill position remains the central unresolved question — not a red flag yet, but the deployment track will define Abel's tenure.

The owner-operator model remains structurally intact. No dividend has been declared, no break-up is in prospect, and no activist campaign is reported.

Price-to-book ratio stands at approximately 1.45 times book value using year-end 2025 equity — essentially at the fair-value anchor of 1.45. Using the updated March 2026 equity of $727 billion the ratio drops to roughly 1.43, modestly below fair value. The exit line at 2.0 times book implies a share price around $668 at current book values — well above today's price.

The insurance float reached $177 billion in the first quarter of 2026 at a negative cost — meaning Berkshire's policyholders are effectively paying it to hold the money. This is the core structural advantage, and it is growing.

Operating earnings (the measure that strips out the very large swings in market value of Berkshire's equity portfolio, which distort the headline profit figure) rose 6.5% in the three months ending March 2026 compared with a year earlier. For the full year 2025 they rose 16.8%. The underlying earning power of the operating businesses is expanding.

BNSF rail posted revenues up 4.8% and pre-tax earnings up 13.4% in the three months ending March 2026, driven by higher pricing per freight car and lower fuel costs. No Surface Transportation Board decision on a Union Pacific/Norfolk Southern combination was announced during the quarter.

Berkshire Hathaway Energy saw its most acute legal overhang — a wildfire-liability verdict against its PacifiCorp subsidiary in Oregon — reversed by the Oregon Court of Appeals in April 2026, materially reducing the worst-case liability exposure. PacifiCorp also agreed to sell its Washington state operations to Portland General Electric for $1.9 billion cash, further derisking the regulated-utility portfolio.

The Share Price

The share price of $484 sits precisely at the fair-value anchor (1.45 times book value on year-end 2025 equity of $717 billion, implying a fair-value price of about $484). That is not cheap; it is not expensive either. The buy-band edge, where the rules would call for adding capital, stands at $417 — approximately 14% below today's price. The trim-band edge stands at $584 — about 21% above. The exit-band edge, where the market would be pricing in a decade of compounding instantly, stands at $651.

Historically, Berkshire has traded in the range of 1.3 to 1.7 times book value across the last five years. At the current price it sits very close to the long-run median of that range. There is no margin of safety in the traditional sense — no meaningful gap between today's price and a conservative estimate of fair value — but there is also no obvious excess.

What's Wobbling

GEICO's combined ratio rose to 87.3% in the three months ending March 2026, from 79.8% in the same period a year earlier — a 7.5 percentage-point deterioration. The combined ratio is a measure of insurance profitability: below 100% means the insurer is profitable on underwriting alone; the lower the better. At 87.3% GEICO is still earning a solid underwriting profit, but the deterioration matters for two reasons.

First, the comparison period (the first quarter of 2025) had unusually low catastrophe losses, making the year-on-year comparison look worse than the underlying trend. The overall underwriting result across all of Berkshire's insurance businesses actually improved — the reinsurance and specialty units more than offset GEICO's setback. Second, the exit condition for GEICO in the bet is a market-share and competitive-position concern, not a single-quarter combined-ratio print. A combined ratio of 87.3% does not cross any of the four exit lines. It is worth watching closely.

Share repurchases were described by management as "relatively minor" in the three months ending March 2026. At a price near book-value fair value, restrained repurchases are correct capital discipline — management has been explicit that it will buy back shares only when they are clearly below intrinsic value. This is the correct behaviour; the optics of a small buyback number are not a signal of anything wrong.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Gregory Abel makes a large, value-destroying acquisition. The single biggest risk to the compounding engine. A deal above $20 billion at an inflated price — no moat, thin projected returns — would confirm that the capital-allocation discipline defining Berkshire's identity has been lost. At $369 billion in uninvested cash, the capacity for a very large mistake exists.

  • GEICO loses structural ground to Progressive. Progressive overtook State Farm in early 2026 to become the largest US personal auto insurer, with GEICO holding third place — a rank it lost from second since 2022. Ajit Jain, Berkshire's vice-chairman of insurance, has confirmed GEICO reached telematics parity with Progressive by mid-2025. Parity in technology is not the same as recovering lost market share. If GEICO's combined ratio stays elevated for several more quarters while Progressive's improves further, the gap could widen into something that affects the float thesis.

  • The proposed Union Pacific/Norfolk Southern railroad combination clears regulatory approval. A combined entity would control roughly 41% of US freight rail versus BNSF's 35%. The western duopoly that has supported BNSF's strong pre-tax margins would face a more capable competitor for the first time in decades. The Surface Transportation Board decision is expected around 2027.

  • PacifiCorp wildfire liabilities re-escalate. The April 2026 court reversal materially reduced the worst-case exposure, and the annual wildfire accrual dropped to $100 million in 2025 from $1.7 billion two years earlier. But additional wildfire seasons and pending claims could reverse that trend. An annual accrual above $1 billion would be the signal to watch.

  • The $369 billion sits uninvested indefinitely. Cash earning Treasury-bill yields at scale is not a thesis-breaker, but it suppresses the return on equity and keeps the stock's earnings yield well below the risk-free rate on an operating-income basis. The opportunity cost compounds with time.

Gaps in the Picture

The share count used to compute book value per share is implied from the market capitalisation divided by the current price, rather than read directly from Berkshire's filings. This is because Berkshire's multi-class share structure (Class A shares, each economically equivalent to 1,500 Class B shares) means the standard figures reported in regulatory filings are not directly usable without the conversion arithmetic. The implied share count of approximately 2,150 million Class B equivalents is consistent with prior periods and cross-checks against Berkshire's publicly disclosed per-share book value, but it is an estimate.

The fair-value anchor of $484 and the three-year expected return of roughly 10.8% per year (base case) rest on different assumptions. The $484 anchor uses today's book value as the base and applies a long-run average price-to-book multiple; it assumes the market keeps valuing Berkshire the way it does today. The 10.8% figure assumes the share price moves from $484 today to a fair-value central estimate — also $484 in this case — which is why the reconciliation note above is not very illuminating here; both anchors point to roughly the same price.

The OBBBA (the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed 2025-07-04) accelerates the phase-out of clean-energy production tax credits for Berkshire Hathaway Energy projects beginning construction after 2025-12-31. The financial impact on BHE's $32 billion capital plan for 2026–2028 has not been quantified in the filings reviewed here; this is a real but unmodelled headwind.
