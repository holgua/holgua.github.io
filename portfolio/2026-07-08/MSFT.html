About Microsoft

Microsoft makes its money primarily in three areas: enterprise cloud infrastructure, enterprise productivity software, and personal computing. The cloud infrastructure business — Azure — provides computing, storage, networking, and AI processing capacity to businesses on a consumption basis — customers pay for what they use — and has been growing at 30–40% a year as enterprises migrate workloads and layer new AI services on top of existing contracts. The productivity software business — Microsoft 365 — bundles email, calendar, document editing, video calls, and now AI writing and coding assistants into a per-seat monthly subscription sold to hundreds of millions of business users; because switching away requires replacing every part of an organisation's communications stack at once, this business renews reliably year after year. The personal computing segment — Windows, the Xbox gaming platform, and the Bing search engine — is smaller and slower-growing, but contributes steady cash flow.

The identity and security system at the core of the enterprise offering — Azure Active Directory, now branded as Entra ID — is the deepest moat in the portfolio. Every application, device, and access policy in a large organisation points at this directory; migrating away would require replacing all of them simultaneously. This switching-cost dynamic, reinforced by 50,000-plus patents and government-grade security certifications that took years to earn, is why the business has sustained returns on invested capital of 22–27% for five consecutive years — well above the roughly 9% cost of capital, confirming that the moat generates genuine and durable excess returns.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that Microsoft's enterprise identity lock-in — its all-or-nothing switching dynamic — will continue generating excess returns as the company layers AI services on top of an already-entrenched base, and that the current surge in infrastructure spending will peak and begin to lever against a growing revenue base rather than permanently compressing cash generation.

The specific conditions that must keep holding:

  1. Azure quarterly revenue growth stays above 25% while capital spending remains above 20% of revenue. The combination matters — elevated spending is acceptable if Azure growth is high enough to imply the investment is earning a return. The exit condition fires only when both legs are simultaneously in breach for two consecutive quarters.

Latest reading: Azure grew 40% in the three months ending in March 2026 and 40% across the nine months to March. Capital spending was 33–37% of revenue per quarter in the current fiscal year — elevated, but Azure growth at 40% prevents the compound trigger from firing.

  1. The ratio of free cash flow to net earnings stays above 0.65 across two consecutive fiscal years, or Azure AI revenue improves. This condition guards against capital spending permanently impairing cash generation; the safeguard is the "or Azure AI improves" clause, which means sustained AI-revenue growth permits the ratio to stay low while the investment cycle is live.

Latest reading: The nine-month ratio is 0.48 — below the 0.65 threshold. However, the exit condition requires two consecutive full fiscal years (not nine months) at this level, and Azure AI revenue continues to grow. Neither leg of the compound trigger is met.

  1. Consolidated operating profit margin stays above 42% across two consecutive quarters. This guards against the AI investment cycle causing structural erosion in the business's core economics rather than a temporary dip.

Latest reading: Operating margin was 46.3% in the three months ending in March 2026 and 47.4% across the nine months to March. Both readings are more than four percentage points above the 42% floor; there is no risk on this condition at the current reading.

  1. The IRS transfer-pricing dispute does not escalate to Tax Court, and no single-quarter tax charge exceeds $5 billion. Microsoft is in a long-running dispute with the US tax authority over how profits were allocated between jurisdictions from 2004 to 2013. The outstanding claim is approximately $28.9 billion plus interest and penalties; the dispute is currently at the administrative appeal stage.

Latest reading: The dispute remains at administrative appeal with no new adverse development in the March 2026 filing. Accruals are unchanged. No Tax Court filing, no large quarterly charge.

  1. No formal antitrust complaint from the US Federal Trade Commission seeking structural remedies. The FTC has been investigating Microsoft's cloud bundling practices and its relationship with artificial-intelligence firm OpenAI. A formal lawsuit seeking to break up or restructure a product line would be a material threat to the cross-sell leverage that underpins the enterprise moat.

Latest reading: Competition law scrutiny is noted in the risk disclosures, but the March 2026 filing contains no formal complaint or structural-remedy proceeding.

  1. Gross margin stays above 65% across two consecutive quarters. Gross margin (the proportion of each sales dollar left after paying for the direct cost of delivering the service) measures whether AI infrastructure costs are being absorbed by the business model or are eroding the underlying economics.

Latest reading: Gross margin was 67.6% in the three months ending in March 2026 and 68.2% across the nine months to March — both above the 65% floor.

  1. Commercial contracted backlog grows by at least 30% year-on-year and does not decline quarter-on-quarter in absolute terms. Contracted backlog — the total value of cloud and software contracts already signed but not yet recognised as revenue — is the most direct measure of enterprise commitment to the platform. Weakness here would precede weakness in reported revenue by several quarters.

Latest reading: Commercial contracted backlog reached $627 billion as of March 2026, up 99% from the same point a year earlier. Exit condition 7 is not remotely approached.

The Share Price

The share price sits at the trim edge — approaching but not yet at the level where the rules call for reducing the position. Put plainly: the share price is $389, and the trim level is $401. The gap is roughly $12.

The metric driving this is the earnings yield on a per-share basis — specifically, net income divided by total market value — which measures how much of the business's annual earnings output is available per dollar of share price. At $389, that yield is approximately 2.1%. The fair-value anchor — the yield level calibrated at thesis-write time to represent a neutral valuation — is 3.5%, which corresponds to a price of roughly $229. The buy level of 5.0%, where the rules would call for adding to the position, corresponds to about $160.

In one sentence: the stock trades well above what the thesis defines as fair value on this metric, and the only reason no trim order has fired is that the price has not yet crossed the $401 trim line.

What's Wobbling

Two readings from the March 2026 filing require continued monitoring, though neither has triggered an exit rule.

The free-cash-flow-to-earnings ratio is the more immediate concern. At 0.48 for the nine months ending in March 2026, it is well below the 0.65 level that signals a problem — driven by capital spending of roughly $80 billion across those nine months as Microsoft builds out AI data-centre capacity. The exit rule for this condition requires two consecutive full fiscal years at below 0.65 with no improvement in Azure AI revenue contribution. Microsoft's fiscal year runs to June, so the next full-year figure will be available in late 2026. If the pattern persists into the full fiscal year ending June 2026 without AI revenue improvement, one leg of the compound trigger will be met, and the following year will be watched closely.

Capital spending as a proportion of revenue is the second reading. At 33–37% of quarterly revenue, it is running well above the 22.9% of the full fiscal year ended June 2025 — and well above the 20% level that forms the second leg of exit condition 1. However, exit condition 1 requires Azure growth to fall below 25% at the same time, which at 40% is not happening. In other words, the spending is elevated but the growth justifying it is also elevated; the compound trigger requires both to deteriorate simultaneously.

One further observation: the earnings yield at 2.1% sits approximately six hundredths of a percentage point above the 2.0% trim threshold. The share price is about $12 below the $401 trim level. The distance is small enough that a modest move in the stock could arm the trim order at the next quarterly check.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • AI capital spending fails to earn a return. Microsoft is committing tens of billions of dollars a year to building AI infrastructure on the assumption that customers will pay to use it at scale. If enterprise adoption of AI workloads is slower or cheaper than expected, the spending will have permanently compressed free cash flow without building the expected moat extension. The bear case assigns this a 20% probability and projects flat free cash flow for two years.
  • Azure growth decelerates sharply. Open-source AI models — large language models that can be run without paying Microsoft — reduce the switching cost in the AI inference layer. If they succeed in commoditising that layer, Azure's advantage would narrow to raw computing power, where Amazon Web Services has been the scale leader for longer. A deceleration to mid-teens growth would put exit condition 1 at risk within a few quarters.
  • IRS dispute settles at the upper bound. The full claim of roughly $28.9 billion plus interest and penalties would consume more than a year of free cash flow and compress the balance sheet. This would not impair the operating business but would limit the capital available for buybacks and the AI investment programme.
  • FTC obtains structural remedies. A forced separation of Teams from the Microsoft 365 bundle, or a requirement to allow customers to run Azure workloads without the identity-management layer, would reduce the cross-sell leverage that makes the enterprise moat so durable. This is the one scenario where the moat itself, rather than merely the cash flow, would be impaired.
  • OpenAI equity losses scale materially. Microsoft's equity stake in OpenAI produced a roughly $4.7 billion loss in the fiscal year ended June 2025. If those losses continue to grow, they will weigh on reported net income and potentially on the political optics of the AI partnership.

Gaps in the Picture

Two data-quality notes carry over from the March 2026 filing review.

The fair-value anchor — currently set at 3.5% on the earnings-yield scale — diverges significantly from the current reading of 2.1%. The gap of 41% (relative to the anchor) is above the 30% threshold that normally prompts a recalibration review. The anchor was set when the stock traded closer to the anchor price of $229; at the current price of $389, the anchor is no longer a live constraint on the valuation. A recalibration is warranted at the next annual cycle.

There is a secondary divergence between the anchor used in the earnings-yield framework ($229 per share) and the midpoint implied by the Fair-Value-Bands model (approximately $2,085 per share, implying an earnings yield of 0.38%). The two models use materially different methodologies and are not calibrated to the same metric; they should not be compared directly, but the gap is large enough to note. The Fair-Value-Bands figure is not used in the standing orders or the score above — the earnings-yield framework governs.

The risk-free rate used in the hurdle-rate calculation (4.86%) is read from the portfolio's broad-market holding rather than from a Microsoft-specific market data file, which does not carry this field. This is the intended fallback and does not affect the score.

The Rules

If all the conditions described in The Case for the Bet are intact — as they currently are — hold the position without adding. Azure growth is sustained, AI contracted backlog is compounding, and the moat is intact. No additional buying at the current price is warranted; the margin of safety is insufficient for a new addition. This is a compounding position, not a distressed recovery.

If any one of the monitored conditions begins to show sustained deterioration — Azure growth declining toward 25%, free-cash-flow conversion failing to improve, operating margin eroding toward 42%, or contracted backlog growth slowing toward 30% — do not add to the position. Monitor for one additional quarter before reassessing size. Determine whether the weakening is cyclical (likely CapEx timing) or structural (likely competitive displacement), and escalate to the exit review if a second consecutive quarter confirms the signal.

If any of the seven exit conditions fires — specifically: Azure growth below 25% for two consecutive quarters while capital spending remains above 20% of revenue; free-cash-flow-to-earnings below 0.65 for two consecutive fiscal years with no Azure AI improvement; operating margin below 42% for two consecutive quarters; the IRS dispute escalated to Tax Court or a single-quarter tax charge above $5 billion; a formal FTC complaint seeking structural remedies; gross margin below 65% for two consecutive quarters; or contracted backlog growth below 30% or an absolute quarterly decline — exit the full position within 30 days, with no averaging down.

If the share price falls to or below $160 (the formal buy-threshold level, corresponding to a 5% earnings yield) and no exit condition has fired, add in tranches: first below $352, second below $322, each tranche representing 25–33% of the intended addition. The formal buy-threshold price of $160 is a severe-bear scenario level; the $352 and $322 prices are the actionable near-term guideposts.

If the share price rises to or above $401 (the trim threshold), trim 20–25% of the position to harvest the premium. Do not trim below 50% of the current holding unless a thesis exit condition has also fired.

If the share price rises to or above $1,605 (the exit threshold, at which the business would be priced above roughly $6.8 trillion), exit the full position and do not re-enter within 90 days.
