Berkshire: The Bet Still Works

The Bottom Line

The bet at the heart of this holding is a capital allocation compounder: a business built to reinvest enormous quantities of cash at above-average rates, year after year, across insurance, rail, utilities, and a vast portfolio of owned companies and public shares. The latest quarterly filing, covering the three months to the end of March 2026, shows the machine running normally — the insurance operation generated more cash than it cost to run, the freight-rail business improved its operating efficiency, and book value per share continued its upward march. The share price, at around $484, sits precisely at fair value as measured by the rules governing this position — neither cheap nor expensive. The one condition to watch is Berkshire's car-insurance business, GEICO, where claims costs rose sharply in the first quarter.

The Verdict

The thesis is intact as of the first-quarter 2026 filing. The three standing conditions — growing book value, low-cost insurance float, and disciplined capital allocation — are all holding. The share price is at the midpoint of the valuation band, which means the rules call for patience rather than action on either side.

As a fresh investment at today's price (around $484 per share). The rules say wait. The price is at fair value, which means there is no margin of safety for a new entry — the upside from here depends entirely on the base case playing out as expected. The better entry point is at or below $417, where the rules arm the buy order. Set a price alert at that level and monitor whether the conditions are still working if the price gets there.

As an existing investment bought below $400 per share — currently sitting on a gain of roughly 21% or more. Hold. The thesis is intact, and the trim trigger has not been reached — the rules call for trimming only above $584. There is no reason to act. Let it compound.

As an existing investment bought roughly between $400 and $500 per share — currently around break-even, give or take. Hold. The position is squarely inside the fair-value band, the thesis is intact, and no action is warranted. The rules call for holding unless a condition breaks or the share price crosses a band edge.

As an existing investment bought between $500 and $600 per share — currently showing a loss of up to about 19%. Hold. An underwater cost basis does not change the thesis assessment. The rules specifically provide for this situation: if the thesis is intact, the correct response to being below cost is to hold, not to sell into an unbroken compounding machine. The value being sought is in the long-term compounding, not in short-term price recovery.

As an existing investment bought above $600 per share — currently showing a loss of roughly 19% or more. Hold. The same logic applies. The exit conditions written into the rules — book value growth persistently lagging world equity markets, a reckless large acquisition, a structural culture change, or a price-to-book ratio above 2.0 — have not been triggered. A large paper loss is uncomfortable, but the rules say it is not a signal to exit when the underlying thesis is intact.

About Berkshire

Berkshire Hathaway is not a single business. It is closer to a privately run investment fund that owns everything outright — dozens of operating companies across insurance, freight rail, regulated utilities, aerospace manufacturing, consumer goods, and financial services — plus a public share portfolio worth roughly $298 billion at the time of writing, holding large stakes in companies including Apple, Bank of America, and Coca-Cola.

The financial engine is insurance. Berkshire's car-insurance operation (GEICO), its reinsurance businesses, and several specialty insurance units together hold what the industry calls "float" — the pool of premiums collected but not yet paid out in claims. This float stood at approximately $176.9 billion at the end of March 2026. The key is that Berkshire earns this float at no cost: the insurance operations collectively take in more in premiums than they pay out in claims, meaning Berkshire is effectively borrowing $176 billion for free and investing it in whatever it chooses. That invested float, compounding at above-average rates for decades, is how a relatively ordinary insurance business became the vehicle for one of the most remarkable capital-compounding records in modern corporate history.

Warren Buffett, who built the business over six decades and stepped down as chief executive on 1 January 2026, remains chairman. Gregory Abel, who previously ran the energy subsidiary Berkshire Hathaway Energy, is the new chief executive. The question every fresh assessment of this holding must confront is whether Abel can sustain the capital allocation discipline that made the compounding possible.

The Case for the Bet

The central proposition is straightforward: Berkshire will continue to reinvest its substantial cash flows and float at rates that exceed what could be achieved by holding a passive index fund. The specific conditions that must keep holding for this to remain true are:

  1. Book value per share must keep growing faster than world equity markets. Book value per share (the net worth of the business divided by the number of shares) is the primary scoreboard. If it trails the returns of a broad world equity index — the MSCI World total return — for five or more consecutive years, the compounding edge is gone. Underlying this is a requirement that the operating businesses collectively keep growing their earnings.

  2. Insurance float must remain large and cheap. The float is the fuel. If the insurance operations become structurally unprofitable — meaning they start paying out more in claims than they take in premiums, consistently — the cost of the fuel turns positive and the engine becomes less powerful. GEICO is the largest single contributor to float and the one most exposed to claims inflation and competitive pressure.

  3. Capital allocation discipline must hold through the Abel era. Any single acquisition exceeding $20 billion at a price above 1.5 times the target's book value, or one that subsequently requires a write-down of goodwill (the premium paid above book value) exceeding $5 billion, would signal that the new chief executive lacks the required discipline. A structural change — a regular dividend, a break-up, or a capitulation to activist shareholders demanding the business be reorganised — would signal the abandonment of the decentralised owner-operator model.

  4. The share price must not get so far ahead of intrinsic value that the opportunity cost becomes clearly negative. If the price-to-book ratio (the share price divided by the net worth per share) exceeds 2.0 times, the valuation fully prices in optimistic compounding and a passive index becomes the better alternative. This condition is assessed in The Share Price.

The Rules

The action plan is set in advance, not improvised in the moment. Five situations are specified:

  • If the thesis is confirmed: Hold without action and let the compounding continue.
  • If the thesis is weakening: Do not add to the position. Re-examine the specific deteriorating driver after the next quarterly filing before deciding anything.
  • If the thesis is broken: Exit the full position within thirty days. No averaging down.
  • If the price falls below the buy-band edge: Confirm the thesis is still intact first. If it is, add to the position.
  • If the price rises above the trim-band edge: Trim 25 to 33 percent of the position to harvest the valuation premium while keeping the core holding.

The Conditions Today

Condition 1 — Book value growth vs. world equity markets. Book value (shareholders' equity) grew from $649 billion at the end of 2024 to $717 billion at the end of 2025 — a rise of 10.5 percent. In the first quarter of 2026 alone, it grew a further $9.8 billion to $727 billion, driven by $10.1 billion of net earnings. Underlying this: pre-tax earnings from the operating businesses reached $12.4 billion in the first quarter of 2026, up 6.5 percent from a year earlier, with the freight-rail business, BNSF, contributing 13.4 percent after-tax growth. The condition is working.

Condition 2 — Insurance float, large and cheap. Float rose to $176.9 billion at the end of March 2026, up about $500 million from the end of 2025. The cost of float was negative in the first quarter of 2026, meaning the insurance operations were profitable in aggregate. GEICO is the wobble: its combined ratio — the proportion of premiums paid out in claims and expenses, where below 100 means profitable — rose to 87.3 percent in the first quarter of 2026 from 79.8 percent a year earlier, as claims frequency and severity both increased. The float is still growing and its aggregate cost remains negative, so the condition is working — but the GEICO portion warrants monitoring.

Condition 3 — Capital allocation discipline under Abel. The most significant transaction in the period was the closing in January 2026 of Berkshire's acquisition of OxyChem, the chemicals division of Occidental Petroleum, for approximately $9.5 billion. This is well below the $20 billion threshold that would trigger concern, and the price paid was at a reasonable multiple of book value. Buyback activity in the first quarter was minimal — $235 million covering a small number of shares — consistent with management's view that the current share price is near intrinsic value and not a level that justifies aggressive repurchase. No structural changes to the ownership or governance model were disclosed. The condition is working.

Condition 4 — Share price vs. the 2.0-times-book exit ceiling. At a current price of around $484, the price-to-book ratio sits at 1.45 — comfortably below the 2.0 times ceiling. A reading of 2.0 times would require the share price to reach approximately $667, which is substantially above even the exit-order level of $651 that the rules define for this position. The condition is not in play. See The Share Price for the full valuation picture.

The Share Price

Berkshire is valued here using the ratio of share price to book value per share (net worth per share). The current reading is 1.45 times book value. The fair-value anchor is 1.45 — so the share price is, to an unusual degree of precision, exactly at the midpoint of what the rules define as fair.

The buy edge sits at 1.25 times book value, which translates to approximately $417 per share at current book. The trim edge sits at 1.75 times book value, translating to approximately $584 per share. Neither has been approached.

This means the current price of around $484 offers no margin of safety for a new entry — the valuation is neither a bargain nor a stretch. It is the baseline: the price at which the share is worth holding if the conditions keep working, but not particularly worth adding to unless it falls to the buy edge.

The Standing Orders

The rules translate the share-price bands into three standing orders and two price alerts. Each is the operationalisation of a pre-committed rule.

Price alerts (informational).
- A first alert at $484, the fair-value anchor. The share price is currently at this level. Any meaningful move away from it — particularly a drop toward the buy edge — warrants a fresh read of the conditions.
- A second alert at $417, the cheap-edge. Reaching this level arms the buy order below; before adding, the latest re-validation should confirm the conditions are still working.

Standing orders (actionable).
- A standing buy order at $417. The rules call for adding in tranches once the share price closes at or below this line and the conditions are still intact. The order may be staged across one or two quarters rather than filled at once.
- A standing trim order at $584. The rules call for trimming 25 to 33 percent of the position when the share price closes at or above this line, to harvest the valuation premium while keeping the core holding.
- A standing exit order at $651. The rules call for closing the full position when the share price closes at or above this level. At that reading, the market is pricing in roughly 10 years of base-case compounding instantly; the implied forward return falls below the long-run equity premium even before accounting for growth, and redeployment into a cheaper alternative beats continuing to hold. No re-entry within 90 days.

The three order levels do not contradict the cost-basis verdicts above: those verdicts describe the current reading; the standing orders describe what the rules will execute if the share price moves to each line.

What's Wobbling

GEICO is the headline watch item. The car-insurance operation's loss ratio — the proportion of premiums paid out in claims — rose 4.9 percentage points in the first quarter of 2026 compared with the same quarter a year earlier. Claims frequency (how often accidents are filed) rose five to seven percent for bodily-injury coverage and two to four percent for property-damage coverage. Claims severity (how much each claim costs) rose twelve to fourteen percent for bodily injury and two to four percent for property damage. Underwriting expenses rose 29 percent, partly reflecting a push to write more new policies.

Two things provide context. First, the first quarter of 2025 was an exceptionally favourable comparison period for GEICO — loss ratios were unusually low following a period of aggressive rate increases that outran claims inflation. The 2026 deterioration is at least partly a normalisation from that unusually good starting point. Second, GEICO's combined ratio of 87.3 percent, while worse than a year ago, is still a healthy underwriting margin by industry standards. The car-insurance business remains profitable.

A more modest wobble concerns Berkshire Hathaway Energy and its subsidiary PacifiCorp, which faces wildfire-related legal liabilities from fires in Oregon. Cumulative probable losses accrued stand at $2.9 billion as of the end of March 2026. A significant positive development arrived in April 2026 when an Oregon appeals court reversed the first-phase verdict in one of the key cases, potentially discharging roughly $719 million in posted bonds. Some retrial risk remains. The BHE energy subsidiary also faces the phase-out of renewable energy tax credits under legislation enacted in July 2025 — this affects the growth pipeline for new renewable projects but does not erode the earnings of already-operating rate-based assets.

Neither wobble crosses an exit condition. Both warrant monitoring.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • The capital allocation transition fails. Greg Abel inherits a $369 billion cash and Treasury-bill fortress and an equally large public equity portfolio. If that capital is deployed at poor returns — large acquisitions at excessive prices, acquisitions that subsequently require large write-downs — the compounding engine slows or reverses. This is the central risk and the one that no quarterly filing can fully dispel. The OxyChem acquisition is Abel's first material test; the outcome will take several years to be visible in the returns.
  • A large insurance catastrophe erodes float economics. A single catastrophic year of claims — major hurricanes, widespread floods, a pandemic — could turn the cost of float sharply positive. Berkshire's geographic and risk diversification provides meaningful protection, but the insurance business cannot be made immune to tail events.
  • GEICO loses ground to technology-driven competitors. Progressive and other insurers have invested heavily in real-time driving-behaviour analysis (telematics) to price risk more precisely. If GEICO's pricing becomes systematically less accurate, policies will migrate to better-priced competitors. The 29 percent rise in policy-acquisition expenses in the first quarter of 2026 may be an early sign of competitive pressure to grow the policy count; it warrants monitoring across multiple quarters.
  • The equity portfolio concentrates and then falls. The public equity portfolio is heavily concentrated — the five largest positions represent roughly 65 percent of the total. A sharp, sustained decline in those holdings would reduce book value materially and test the patience of shareholders who focus on the quarterly net income figure rather than operating earnings.
  • BHE wildfire liability escalates beyond current accruals. The $2.9 billion accrued is the current estimate of probable losses. If the legal exposure expands materially — through adverse appellate outcomes, class certification on remand, or new fire events — the drain on BHE's capital could impair its ability to fund the regulated utility investments that underpin its earnings.

Gaps in the Picture

The diluted share count used in the valuation calculation is not directly reported in Berkshire's regulatory filings — it is implied by dividing the disclosed market capitalisation by the share price. This is an approximation, and the multiple share classes (Class A, with full voting rights, and Class B, with 1/1500th the economic interest) complicate the conversion. The book value per share used in the price-to-book calculation should be verified against the per-share figure that management discloses directly in the filing's management discussion and analysis section. The discrepancy is likely modest, but the approximation carries a small margin of error that propagates into the band-edge prices cited above.
