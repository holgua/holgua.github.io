Recent Development

The annual filing for the year ending 2026-04-30 confirmed the thesis across all measurable dimensions. The headline numbers were a 17% revenue increase to $1.74 billion and a 23% rise in free cash flow (operating cash less capital expenditure) to $322 million, equivalent to an 18.5% margin on revenue. The cloud-delivered subscription business, where Elastic hosts the software for customers rather than letting them run it themselves, grew 22% and now accounts for 48% of total revenue — up from 43% two years earlier. That mix shift matters because cloud customers tend to expand their spending more predictably than those running the software on their own servers.

The customer count also moved in the right direction. The number of customers spending more than $100,000 a year grew from 1,510 to 1,720, and those spending more than $1 million reached 240. The net expansion rate — which measures how much more existing customers spent compared with a year ago — held at 112%, seven percentage points above the exit threshold of 105%.

One item to note: the company's compensation costs in stock (shares granted to employees as part of their pay), which have been one of the main watch items, declined from 18.2% of revenue in the prior year to 17.7% in the most recent year. The filing for 2026 marks the first year below 18% since the watch began. The margin is narrow, but the direction is the right one.

About Elastic

Elastic N.V. makes software for searching, monitoring, and securing data. Its core product, the Elasticsearch engine, is used by companies to build internal search tools, monitor the health and performance of their software systems, and detect security threats — three applications that all share the same underlying need to sift through large volumes of data quickly.

The business model is subscriptions. Customers pay either to run the software themselves (on their own servers or a cloud provider they choose) or to have Elastic run it for them through a cloud service called Elastic Cloud. Cloud customers account for nearly half of all revenue and have been growing faster than the self-managed segment.

Elastic earns 76 cents in gross profit on every dollar of subscription revenue — a high-margin profile typical of software businesses that incur most of their costs in research and engineering rather than in delivering each incremental unit of service. The company's cash generation (the money left over after paying for operations and capital expenditure) reached $322 million in the year to 2026-04-30, an 18.5% margin that has risen steeply from near-zero four years earlier.

The Case for the Bet

The central bet is that Elastic's three-use-case platform — search and artificial intelligence, system observability, and security — sits on a single shared data layer, and that once a company has embedded its production pipelines on that layer, switching to a different vendor is expensive enough to keep them in place while they gradually expand their usage. The expansion is the compounding engine: customers who started with one use case tend to add another, which is why existing customers have been spending about 12% more each year than the year before.

The supporting bet is that the shift from self-managed to cloud-delivered subscriptions, which is already under way, improves the unit economics of each customer relationship over time, while a series of margin improvements work their way through the income statement as the business scales.

For this bet to keep working, eight conditions must hold:

  1. Customer expansion rate above 105%. This measures how much more existing customers spent compared with a year earlier. A reading above 100% means the business is growing from within its existing customer base alone, even before adding new customers. The exit condition fires if this falls below 105% for two consecutive quarters.

Latest reading: 112% as of 2026-04-30, confirmed in the FY2026 annual filing. Seven percentage points above the exit threshold, with the count of high-value customers (those spending more than $100,000) growing from 1,510 to 1,720.

  1. Elastic Cloud revenue growing above 10% annually. The cloud-delivered business is the faster-growing, higher-margin segment. A deceleration below 10% for a full year would confirm that the open-source fork maintained by Amazon Web Services has reached a scale where it draws away meaningful cloud demand.

Latest reading: 22% growth in the year to 2026-04-30, with the annual contract segment growing 28%. Cloud revenue now accounts for 48% of total revenue, up from 43% two years ago. Well above the exit floor and the 15% level where caution would increase.

  1. Cash generation above 14% of revenue. The 14% floor is the level at which the business would need to be reassessed as a capital-light compounder. Below this, the economic case rests on a narrower base.

Latest reading: 18.5% in the year to 2026-04-30 ($322 million on $1.74 billion of revenue), 4.5 percentage points above the floor. Management has guided for approximately 21.5% in the coming year.

  1. Stock-based compensation below 18% of revenue for a second consecutive year. Employee compensation paid in shares rather than cash is the main gap between the company's headline reported profits and its underlying cash generation. One year above 18% has already been logged (the prior year at 18.2%). A second would trigger the exit.

Latest reading: 17.7% in the year to 2026-04-30, clearing the threshold but narrowly. The multi-year direction is favourable: 20% three years ago, 18.2% two years ago, 17.7% now. The risk of a second consecutive breach has passed for this cycle; the watch continues at the next annual filing.

  1. No acquisition above $500 million at an excessive revenue multiple. Large acquisitions at prices far above what the business earns in revenue, without a credible path to cash-generation payback within three years, would signal a change in how capital is being allocated.

Latest reading: The only acquisition in the most recent year was a small artificial-intelligence specialist (Jina AI, at approximately $37 million) focused on embedding models for the search product. Well below the $500 million threshold.

  1. Positive operating profit by 2028. On standard accounting rules (which include stock-based compensation as an expense), Elastic has been running at an operating loss. The trajectory has been narrowing steeply: the operating loss shrank from 10% of revenue two years ago to 3.7% the year before to 1.9% in the most recent year. The exit condition fires if this does not reach zero by 2028.

Latest reading: Operating loss of $33.5 million (−1.9% of revenue) in the year to 2026-04-30, narrowing by 39% from the year before. Gross margin expanded to 76%, providing the operating leverage. The current trajectory of approximately 1.8 percentage points of improvement per year would reach zero by 2027, ahead of the 2028 deadline.

  1. Competitive position not deteriorating structurally. This condition requires two things to occur simultaneously: Datadog's annual revenue growth exceeding Elastic's by more than 15 percentage points for two consecutive years, and Elastic's position in a major industry analyst ranking of observability vendors falling below the top tier. Exit requires both.

Latest reading: The annual filing names Datadog as a primary competitor but does not disclose comparative revenue growth or analyst rankings — these require Datadog's own filings and Elastic's investor-relations releases. No exit condition is triggered; the data is simply not in this filing.

  1. Acquisition discipline — size and payback. (Subsection of condition 5 above, covering the financial terms of any future acquisition.) An acquisition above $500 million at a revenue multiple above 8 times without a three-year cash-generation payback commitment would signal poor capital discipline.

Latest reading: Only one acquisition this year, at $37 million. Condition intact.

The Share Price

The fair value estimate — the price at which the business's cash generation, if it continues at the current rate, would represent a neutral return for a long-term holder — is $101. At today's price of $59, the share price sits deeply below that anchor.

The way the valuation works: the cash the business generates in a year ($322 million) is divided by the total market value (shares times price), producing what is called the earnings yield (cash earned divided by price paid, expressed as a percentage). At $59, that yield is about 5.2%. The rules call for adding to the position when the yield rises above 5.5% (equivalent to a price at or below $56), for trimming when it falls below 2.2% (a price around $140), and for a full exit if it falls below 1.5% (approximately $200). At today's reading, the yield is close to but has not yet reached the add-signal level.

The share price is therefore in the hold band — not cheap enough to trigger the add rule, not expensive enough to trigger the trim rule, and firmly below what the base-case model says the business is worth.

Why the Market Sees It Differently

At $59, the market is pricing the business as though its cash generation will grow at around 5.8% a year over the long term — roughly in line with the assumed inflation rate, which amounts to saying the business will barely grow in real terms. Our model's base case assumes 15% annual growth.

The gap between those two numbers — 9.2 percentage points — is large enough to qualify as an extreme divergence. The market is not slightly more pessimistic; it is assigning roughly half our base growth rate to the business.

Three hypotheses explain a gap of this size. First, the market may assign significantly higher probability to the bear case: if the open-source Amazon Web Services fork does real damage to the self-managed renewal base and cloud growth stalls, the 9% annual growth scenario (which produces a much lower intrinsic value) would be the right anchor. Second, the market may be pricing in that current levels of employee equity compensation will persist indefinitely, reducing the cash that actually flows to shareholders below what the headline free cash flow figure suggests. Third, the stock has spent most of the past three years trading well below what discounted-cash-flow models suggest, and it is possible that the market simply does not believe the profitability trajectory will materialise on the guided timetable.

The most recent filing provides some evidence that cuts against all three hypotheses. Customer expansion is holding, cloud growth is above 20%, cash generation is rising, and the compensation burden is declining. None of this disproves the bear case — the filing covers only one year — but it is evidence that the business is not tracking the bearish scenario yet.

What would need to happen for the market to revise its view upward? The likeliest catalyst is two or three consecutive quarters of cloud growth remaining above 20%, combined with the operating loss finally turning positive. The share price will figure this out eventually, or the filing data will not hold and the market will have been right.

What's Wobbling

Two things are worth watching without triggering any exit condition.

Stock-based compensation. At 17.7% of revenue, compensation paid in shares cleared the 18% threshold for the first time in three years — but only just. The exit condition requires two consecutive years above 18%, and the current year broke the streak. The direction is right; the margin is thin. If the next annual filing shows a reversal — compensation creeping back above 18% — the streak restarts and the exit condition comes back into view.

Price approaching the add signal. At $59, the earnings yield of 5.2% is 33 basis points (hundredths of a percentage point) below the 5.5% level that would arm the buy order. A modest further decline in the share price would cross that line. The rules are clear: add only once the yield crosses 5.5%, and only after confirming the conditions are still working. The conditions are currently intact; the price simply has not moved yet.

Competitive position unassessed. The condition that monitors Datadog's growth relative to Elastic's cannot be read from the annual filing — it requires Datadog's own results and industry analyst rankings. This is a structural gap in the annual-filing validation cycle, not an emerging concern, but it means one of the seven exit conditions is essentially unmonitored between dedicated competitive-research runs.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • The Amazon Web Services fork takes hold. Amazon Web Services maintains a free, open-source alternative to Elasticsearch under the Linux Foundation. If enterprise customers start choosing this over the paid Elastic product — particularly in the self-managed segment — renewal rates would fall and the net expansion rate would follow, eventually triggering exit condition one.
  • Cloud growth decelerates sharply. At 22% today, cloud revenue growth is comfortably above the 10% floor. But the floor is a minimum, not a comfort zone. If artificial-intelligence workloads shift to specialised vector databases (which focus exclusively on one type of search that Elastic also handles) rather than to Elastic's broader platform, cloud growth could slow faster than expected.
  • The compensation burden reverses. Three consecutive years of declining stock-based compensation, ending at 17.7%, is encouraging. But the absolute amount is still $308 million a year — nearly equal to the total annual cash generation. If headcount growth accelerates in a competitive engineering talent market, the ratio could reverse.
  • The bear case is not remote. At 20% probability as assigned, the scenario in which the intrinsic value is approximately $29 per share — 51% below today's price — is not a tail risk. It is one in five. That probability is built into the analysis, but it bears repeating: this is not a position where the downside has been ruled out.
  • Profitability trajectory stalls. The GAAP operating loss is narrowing on a clear trend, and the 2028 target looks achievable at the current pace. But it is a target, not a certainty. A single year of higher costs — a large acquisition, a spike in cloud-hosting commitments, or aggressive hiring — could delay it and reset expectations.

Gaps in the Picture

  • The competitive-position exit condition (Datadog growth gap and analyst rankings) cannot be assessed from the annual filing alone. It requires Datadog's own quarterly results and Elastic's investor-relations communications. The condition is effectively unmonitored between dedicated competitive-research cycles.
  • Two model-anchor divergence warnings are present. The fair-value estimate used in the valuation band ($101, derived from a discounted-cash-flow model at 15% annual growth and a 10% discount rate) diverges significantly from two other reference points: the current yield-based reading of what the stock "should" yield at fair value, and a revenue-and-earnings-multiple model that produces a substantially higher fair-value estimate ($244). The $101 anchor is the more conservative choice and is the one used for the buy, trim, and exit rules. The divergence does not indicate a problem with the rules; it reflects the fact that different models answer different questions.
  • The risk-free rate used in the score calculation was read from a companion holding's market-data file rather than from Elastic's own, because Elastic's file does not carry this field. The SPY market-data file (dated 2026-07-07) provided the substitute rate of 4.43%. This is a mechanical note about data sourcing, not a concern about the score itself.
  • Stock-based compensation is the primary gap between reported free cash flow and the cash that would accrue to shareholders if the share count were fixed. At $308 million a year against $322 million of free cash flow, the ratio is unusually high. The valuation uses raw free cash flow rather than a post-compensation figure, which is the basis on which the buy, trim, and exit thresholds were calibrated. The choice is deliberate and documented, but anyone reading the cash-flow figure should be aware of it.

The Rules

The following rules govern what follows from each combination of conditions and share price. They apply regardless of when or at what price shares were purchased.

  • If all conditions are working: hold the position and monitor. Do not add unless the share price falls to or below $56. Confirm the conditions are still working before adding; do not add on price alone.
  • If conditions start to weaken (customer expansion rate dropping toward 107%, or cloud growth decelerating toward 15% for two consecutive quarters): do not add. Require two consecutive quarters of deterioration before any reduction — one quarter is insufficient to confirm a structural break.
  • If any written exit condition fires: close the full position within 30 days. No averaging down on exits. The exit conditions are listed in The Case for the Bet above; any one of the seven, when confirmed as triggered, is sufficient.
  • If the share price falls to or below $56: add to the position in staged tranches, provided the conditions are still working. No more than half the target size in a single tranche.
  • If the share price rises to or above $140: trim approximately a quarter of the position. Keep the core holding; harvest the excess.
  • If the share price rises to or above $200: exit the entire remaining position. At that level the market is pricing in the bull case immediately, and the long-run expected return falls below the minimum required. No re-entry for 90 days.
