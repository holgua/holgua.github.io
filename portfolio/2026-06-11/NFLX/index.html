Netflix: The Bet Still Works

The Bottom Line

The case for Netflix as an investment is built on one idea: that a global streaming service with 325 million paying households can keep expanding its profit margin year after year, because the cost of delivering the same content to the next million subscribers is close to zero. That idea is holding. The three months ending in March 2026 produced the company's highest-ever operating margin — 32.3 cents of profit on every dollar of sales — already above management's target for the full year. At $82 per share, the price is fair rather than cheap: roughly 23% above the conservative estimate of what the business is worth today. The one thread to watch is advertising revenue, whose trajectory cannot be confirmed or refuted until the year-end figures land.

The Verdict

The latest quarterly filing clears every exit condition in the written investment case. The operating-leverage flywheel is turning. No condition is broken; none is close. The overall standing instruction is to hold, with specific guidance depending on when shares were acquired.

As a fresh investment at today's price (around $82 per share). The rules say wait. The share price sits 23% above the conservative estimate of intrinsic worth ($67 per share), and there is no margin of safety for a new buyer. Set a price alert at $67 (where the share price returns to fair value) and a second at $45 (where the earnings yield crosses the buy threshold). Neither order fires today.

As an existing investment bought below $80 per share — currently sitting on a gain of roughly 3% or more. Hold without action. The thesis is intact, the flywheel is producing above-target margins, and the trim threshold sits at $112. Nothing in the latest filing changes the standing instruction to continue compounding.

As an existing investment bought between roughly $80 and $90 per share — currently around break-even, give or take. Hold without action. Today's price ($82) is in this band. The filing confirms every pillar of the bet; the trim trigger is 37% away. Continue compounding.

As an existing investment bought between $90 and $100 per share — currently showing a loss of roughly 9% to 18%. Hold without action. A mild loss at a moment when the underlying business is performing above expectations is not a signal to act. The thesis is intact; none of the eight exit conditions has fired. The growth-lens case for adding applies only if the price falls to the buy edge.

As an existing investment bought above $100 per share — currently showing a loss of roughly 18% or more. Hold without action. The wobbles that exist do not cross any of the written thresholds.

About Netflix

Netflix is a subscription streaming service. Roughly 325 million households in 190 countries pay a monthly fee — ranging from roughly $9 to $23 in the United States — to watch an on-demand library of films, television series, documentaries, and live events. The company produces a large share of that library itself, which is where most of its spending goes: content is written, filmed, and delivered once, then streamed to each additional subscriber at near-zero marginal cost. That structure is the foundation of the whole investment case.

The company earns its money almost entirely from those monthly fees. An advertising-supported tier, introduced in 2022, is growing but as of the most recent quarterly filing still does not represent a separately disclosed or material fraction of total sales. Netflix does not pay dividends; it returns surplus cash to shareholders by buying back its own shares. In the twelve months ending in December 2025 it spent $9.1 billion on buybacks — roughly the same as its annual free cash flow (cash generated after meeting all obligations and capital spending).

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that Netflix's cost base is largely fixed while its revenue base continues to grow — and that this gap keeps widening. More subscribers and higher prices feed higher revenue; content is already made and costs little more to deliver to the next million households; operating profit therefore grows faster than revenue. The company calls this the operating-leverage flywheel.

For that flywheel to keep turning, five conditions must keep holding:

  1. Operating margin stays above 28%. If the quarterly operating margin (profit as a share of sales) falls below 28% for two consecutive quarters without a one-off explanation, the operating-leverage thesis is broken.

  2. Advertising revenue tracks toward $2.5 billion annualised by the end of 2026. The ad tier is the next layer of the monetisation stack; if it stalls, the incremental revenue that funds the flywheel beyond subscription price increases is gone.

  3. Return on invested capital stays above 20%. This ratio (net operating profit divided by the capital employed to earn it) measures whether the moat is compounding or eroding. Two consecutive years below 20% would signal the moat is leaking.

  4. Cash profit margin stays above 18% on a full-year view. Free cash flow (cash left after capital spending) as a share of revenue must not fall below 18% on an annual basis. A sustained breach would signal that cash conversion is deteriorating.

  5. Content spending stays disciplined. Content cash paid out divided by content amortisation (the annual accounting charge) must not exceed 1.3 times on a sustained basis. A breach would signal the capital-light model is breaking down — the company is spending more to make content than it is charging through its books each year.

Three further conditions are monitored but not yet due:

  1. Capital-return discipline must hold. Buybacks should not slow or stop while the earnings yield is attractive, and management should not redirect capital into a large value-destroying acquisition.

  2. By the third quarter of 2027, a coherent artificial-intelligence strategy must be articulated — one that shows measurable engagement-retention or attention-share defence against AI-native entertainment platforms. This deadline reflects the view that generative AI is a named risk to consumer attention that deserves a visible management response within a reasonable horizon.

  3. Brazilian tax and foreign-film tariff exposure must not expand materially beyond the known envelope. The known envelope is a $619 million booked liability plus $700 million in cash deposits already made. A tail outcome 30–50% worse, or a formal foreign-film tariff absorbed without pass-through to subscribers, would structurally re-rate margins.

The Rules

The investment case has five pre-set branches — one for each combination of thesis health and share price:

  • If all conditions are working and the share price is in the middle band: hold without action. Continue compounding.
  • If any condition is wobbling but not broken: do not add to the position. Recheck after the next two quarterly filings before deciding whether the wobble resolves or deepens.
  • If any condition is broken (an exit condition fires): close the full position within 30 days. No averaging down. Record which condition fired and why.
  • If the share price falls below the buy edge ($45): first confirm the thesis is still intact, then add in stages over one to two quarters.
  • If the share price rises above the trim edge ($112): trim 25–33% of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.

There is also an upper exit ceiling, addressed in The Standing Orders below.

The Conditions Today

Operating margin is the strongest pillar. In the three months ending in March 2026, Netflix earned 32.3 cents of operating profit on every dollar of sales — the highest quarterly margin in the company's history, and already above management's target of 31.5% for the full year 2026. Revenue grew 16% compared with the same quarter a year earlier; the cost of delivering that content grew only 12%. That gap is the operating-leverage signal in its simplest form. Annual operating income for 2025 was 28% higher than for 2024. The exit condition (28% margin for two consecutive quarters) is not close to being triggered.

Advertising revenue is the one condition that cannot yet be scored. Netflix does not separately disclose advertising revenue in its quarterly report; the company describes it only as one of three contributors to revenue growth, and explicitly as "not a material component" of the total. The 2026 full-year test — whether ad revenue tracks toward $2.5 billion annualised — can only be answered at year-end. The company is investing in the advertising business (sales and marketing headcount costs rose by $48 million in the quarter, partly attributable to the ad operation), but the trajectory is deferred.

Return on invested capital cannot be computed quarterly, as Netflix does not disclose the inputs needed on a three-month basis. The last annual figure, for 2025, was 25.8% — well above the 20% exit threshold and up from 15% in 2023. The quarterly margin expansion in early 2026 is consistent with continued improvement.

Cash profit margin on a full-year view was 20.9% in 2025, comfortably above the 18% floor. The first quarter of 2026 included an unusual $729 million cash payment related to Brazilian tax assessments — a one-off that inflated cash outflows. Stripping that out, the quarterly cash profit margin was approximately 18.7%. The exit condition is measured on an annual basis, and the 2025 full-year figure is the operative reading.

Content discipline is intact. In the three months ending in March 2026, content cash paid divided by content amortisation was 1.15 times — well below the 1.3 times ceiling. Total content obligations of $24.1 billion were essentially flat versus the prior year-end, and amortisation is growing at roughly 10% per year while revenue is growing at 16%. The capital-light model is working.

Capital-return discipline is the most clearly confirmed pillar this quarter. Netflix spent $1.3 billion buying back shares in March 2026 (the January and February pause is explained by the attempted acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery, which was terminated on 2026-02-27; upon termination Netflix collected a $2.8 billion fee from the would-be counterparty). The remaining buyback authorisation stands at $6.8 billion. The walk-away from the Warner Bros. Discovery deal — absorbing a fee rather than overpaying — is the governing capital-discipline signal for this condition, and it is strongly positive.

Artificial-intelligence strategy is not yet due: the deadline is the third quarter of 2027. Netflix's most recent annual report Risk Factors are unchanged from the prior year, which means no new disclosure on this front. Five more quarterly filing cycles remain before the condition must be scored.

Brazilian tax and tariff exposure sits inside the known envelope. The $729 million paid in the first quarter of 2026 is within the $619 million booked liability plus $700 million deposit range that the thesis already prices in. No escalation beyond that envelope was disclosed; the foreign-film tariff was not mentioned in the filing and risk factors are unchanged.

The Share Price

At $82, Netflix trades roughly 23% above the conservative estimate of what the business is worth today.

That conservative estimate — $67 per share — is built by discounting ten years of projected owner earnings (net profit plus non-cash charges, minus the capital spending required to maintain the business) at a 10% annual rate and assuming earnings grow at 6% per year, then slow to 3% in the terminal period. The underlying owner earnings figure used is $9.5 billion for 2025. At $82, the earnings yield on that figure is 2.74% — below the 3.37% that would represent fair value, and well below the 5% that would represent a bargain entry.

In plain terms: a buyer at $82 is paying a premium over the conservative floor, and there is no buffer for error. The market's implicit assumption is roughly 5.8% perpetual earnings growth — plausible but not conservative. The Base-case midpoint (management guidance achieved, advertising reaches $6 billion by 2028) implies fair value around $86, barely above today's price. The Bear case ($9 midpoint) represents an 89% decline from current levels if growth assumptions fail.

The share price is fair-to-slightly-above-fair on a conservative valuation basis. It is not cheap.

The Standing Orders

The rules translate the share-price bands into three standing orders and two price alerts.

Price alerts (informational).
- A first alert at $67, the fair-value anchor. If the share price falls to this level, it means the market is pricing in exactly what the conservative model says the business is worth — no margin of safety, but no premium either. A fresh read of the conditions is warranted before acting.
- A second alert at $45, the buy-edge. The share price crossing this level arms the buy order below; before adding, the latest re-validation should confirm the conditions are still working.

Standing orders (actionable).
- A standing buy order at $45. The rules call for adding in stages once the share price closes at or below this level and all exit conditions remain unbroken. Additions may be staged across one or two quarters rather than filled in a single transaction.
- A standing trim order at $112. The rules call for trimming 25–33% of the position when the share price closes at or above this level, to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.
- A standing exit order at $607. The rules call for closing the full remaining position when the share price closes at or above this level. At that reading the market would be pricing in roughly 29 years of base-case earnings growth instantaneously — the implied forward return falls below the long-run rate of return available from equities broadly, and the case for holding rather than reallocating collapses. In practice, the action plan specifies a lower exit trigger: if the earnings yield falls below 0.5% (a price of approximately $450 per share, above the bull-case range top of $395), the full position should be exited within one calendar quarter, on the grounds that the asymmetry has inverted and the trim tier has already harvested the gain band.

The three order levels do not contradict the cost-basis verdicts above: those verdicts describe the current reading; the standing orders describe what the rules will execute if the share price moves to each level.

What's Wobbling

Three of the eight conditions came back with a mixed reading from the latest filing — not confirmation, but not a challenge to the thesis either.

Advertising revenue cannot be scored this quarter. Netflix does not disclose the figure separately, and the exit condition ($2.5 billion annualised by year-end 2026) requires the full-year disclosure to settle. The company is building out the advertising operation — the headcount investment is visible — but the flywheel's speed is unknown until the numbers surface. This is a deferred signal, not a deteriorating one.

Return on invested capital is mixed for a mechanical reason: it is computed annually, not quarterly, and the most recent figure is the 2025 full year. The directional evidence — operating margin expanding, revenue growing faster than costs — is consistent with continued improvement, but the number cannot be confirmed until the next annual filing.

Brazilian tax exposure sits inside the envelope, but the final resolution amount is still unknown. The $729 million paid in the first quarter of 2026 is within what was already priced in, and no escalation was disclosed. But the total remains open; the foreign-film tariff, if enacted without pass-through, would be a separate and potentially more damaging development. Neither has crossed a trigger — both remain background risks.

None of these three wobbles crosses an exit condition. None approaches a threshold. They are the sensible things to watch at the next quarterly disclosure.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • The advertising tier fails to compound. If ad revenue tops out below $4 billion by 2028 while content amortisation keeps growing at roughly 10% per year, the operating-leverage flywheel slows. The multiple the market is willing to pay compresses. This scenario does not necessarily destroy the subscription business, but it re-rates it materially.

  • The foreign-film tariff is enacted and absorbed. A formal tariff on foreign-produced films, absorbed without passing through to subscribers via price increases, would structurally compress margins. One analyst estimated the impact at up to 20% of annual earnings if fully absorbed. Netflix produces roughly half its content abroad.

  • Generative AI migrates consumer attention at scale. The one scenario with genuinely moat-destructive potential. If AI-native entertainment platforms draw meaningful view-time away from streaming over the next five to seven years, the subscriber count and pricing power that underpin the entire investment case erode. This risk is speculative at present; the exit condition gives management until the third quarter of 2027 to articulate a coherent response.

  • Brazilian tax exposure expands into the tail. A final assessment 30–50% worse than the known envelope would create a cash drain beyond what the thesis prices in. Management has stated it does not expect material future impact; the risk is not eliminated.

  • The co-CEO transition fails to maintain strategic coherence. Reed Hastings is departing in June 2026. Ted Sarandos and Greg Peters are experienced executives, but neither has managed the company through a major downturn independently. A guidance miss of 5% or more for two consecutive quarters after the transition would raise questions that the current track record suppresses.

Gaps in the Picture

Three data points are missing or deferred from the latest disclosure.

The most consequential gap is advertising revenue. Netflix chose not to disclose it separately in the most recent quarterly filing — it is treated as immaterial to total revenue by accounting standards. The bet's second pillar (the advertising flywheel) cannot be confirmed or refuted until the company reports its full-year 2026 results. Anyone reading this today should treat the ad-revenue condition as open.

The second gap is the maintenance versus growth split of capital spending. The analysis above uses total capital expenditure as a proxy for maintenance spending when computing the earnings yield. Netflix does not separately disclose how much of its capital spending maintains existing infrastructure and how much expands it; the split is estimated. If the maintenance share is lower than assumed, owner earnings are higher than stated — and the share price is less expensive than it appears against the conservative floor.

The third gap is return on invested capital on a quarterly basis. Netflix does not compute or disclose this figure for individual quarters. The 2025 full-year figure (25.8%) is the operative number, and it is fifteen months old relative to today.
