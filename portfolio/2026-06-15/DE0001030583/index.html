About the Bond

Germany's federal government — the Federal Republic of Germany, rated AAA by all three major credit-rating agencies — issues bonds to finance itself, just as any national government does. Most of those bonds pay a fixed nominal coupon: the annual payment is stated at issuance and does not change. This bond is different. It is an inflation-linked bond (sometimes called a "linker"): both the coupon payment and the final repayment of principal at maturity are adjusted upward in line with actual inflation. The reference measure is the Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices excluding tobacco, a Eurostat series that tracks how much the average basket of goods and services in the euro area costs over time. When consumer prices rise, the bond's payments rise proportionally.

The bond was issued in 2021 with a real coupon of 0.10% per year — a nominal rate so low as to be almost trivial. What matters is the inflation adjustment. By 2026-06-15 (the factsheet date used here), consumer prices have risen enough since issuance that the indexation coefficient stands at 1.25631: every €100 of original face value now commands €125.63 in inflation-adjusted terms. A buyer pays the dirty price of €119.93 per €100 original face value at settlement, not €95.38. The €95.38 is the quoted clean price in real terms (same purchasing-power units as the issuance date). To get the actual euros that change hands, multiply by the index ratio (1.25631), then add accrued interest.

The bond matures on 2033-04-15. At that point, the full inflation-adjusted principal is repaid in a single lump sum. A holder who reaches maturity and reinvests the annual coupon receives a real return equal — by construction — to the real yield embedded in the price paid at purchase.

The Case for the Bet

The case rests on one structural claim: inflation protection at near-zero credit risk, at a price that embeds a positive real return. Germany has never defaulted on a euro-denominated obligation. The HICP inflation index is a long-established Eurostat series. Together, those two facts mean the position carries essentially no issuer risk and no index-integrity risk under normal conditions. The real yield at market price — and how it drifts over time — is the primary signal for whether the position remains worth holding.

Three conditions must keep holding for the bet to work:

  1. The real yield must not stay persistently below −1.0%. Specifically, the real yield at current market price must not remain below −1.0% for two consecutive quarters while EUR cash or conventional Bunds offer a superior real return. At that point, the inflation-hedge benefit would be outweighed by a guaranteed real loss baked into the price.
  2. Germany's sovereign credit must remain strong. A downgrade below AA− by any two of the three major agencies (Fitch, Moody's, S&P) would signal a structural change in issuer creditworthiness and trigger an exit.
  3. The HICP inflation index must not be materially restructured. If Eurostat were to replace the reference index with a measure that no longer tracks realised consumer-price inflation, the bond's inflation-protection mechanism would be severed.

The Rules

The pre-committed rules for this holding translate each state of the bet and of the price into a specific action:

  • If the bet is working as intended: hold. Real yield is within band, German credit is intact, and HICP linkage provides inflation protection as intended.
  • If the bet is weakening but not broken: do not add. Re-assess if real yield moves below 0% but exit condition (1) has not yet fired; compare real after-tax return against EUR cash and conventional Bunds before adding.
  • If one or more exit conditions fires: exit the full position. Move proceeds to nominal short-duration EUR Bunds or EUR cash.
  • If real yield rises above 1.50%: add to the position. A real yield above 1.50% on a seven-year AAA EUR sovereign linker is highly attractive; scale in proportionally to the inflation-hedge allocation target.
  • If real yield falls below −0.50%: trim 25% on first crossing. If real yield remains below −0.50% at the next quarterly review (at least 90 calendar days later), trim an additional 25%. Park proceeds in EUR cash or short conventional Bunds. The 25-plus-25 ladder is bounded — further crossings do not trigger additional trims until exit condition (1) fires.
  • If the compound exit trigger fires — real yield below −1.0% for at least 90 days and EUR cash now offering a superior real return — exit the full position. Switch proceeds to EUR cash or short-duration nominal Bunds; the inflation-hedge function is no longer additive versus the available alternative.

Break-even inflation. The break-even inflation rate — the market's implied average inflation expectation over the remaining holding period, derived by comparing this bond's real yield with the nominal yield on conventional German bonds of similar maturity — is 1.98%. Two conventional Bunds bracket this bond's 2033-04-15 maturity: one maturing in February 2033 (nominal yield 2.77%) and one in August 2033 (nominal yield 2.80%). Interpolating between them gives a nominal midpoint of approximately 2.78%, and subtracting the real yield of 0.80% yields a break-even of 1.98%. That figure sits almost exactly at the European Central Bank's stated 2% inflation target: the market prices in target-level inflation over the remaining ~7 years, not a speculative overshoot. No bubble premium for inflation protection is baked into the price.

German sovereign credit. Germany is rated AAA by Fitch, Aaa by Moody's, and AAA by S&P. No rating watch or negative outlook is in effect at any agency. Exit condition (2) — a downgrade below AA− by two agencies — is far from triggered.

Inflation index integrity. No Eurostat announcement of any restructuring of the HICP ex-tobacco methodology is known as of 2026-06-15. The mechanism that links this bond's payments to realised consumer-price inflation remains intact. Exit condition (3) is inactive.

At a clean price of €95.38, the bond embeds a real return of 0.80%. If the price were to rise to €104, the real yield would have fallen to −0.50% — the first trim trigger. If the price were to rise to €108, the real yield would have fallen to −1.00% — the threshold for the compound exit trigger, once the 90-day persistence and cash-comparison conditions are also met.

For exit condition (1) to fire — beyond the yield threshold and persistence requirements — EUR cash would need to offer a superior real return, which today it does not.

What's Wobbling

Nothing is wobbling this cycle. All four drivers confirm the thesis: real yield is positive and above the fair-value anchor, break-even inflation at 1.98% aligns with the ECB's stated 2% target, German sovereign credit is at maximum ratings from all three agencies, and the HICP index methodology is unchanged. The break-even's 3-basis-point drift from the prior snapshot (from 2.01% to 1.98%) is fully within rounding of the nominal Bund yields and does not change the read.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Inflation falls sharply below 2%. If the euro area enters a prolonged period of disinflation or deflation, the indexation coefficient stops rising, the bond's nominal cash flows shrink, and the inflation-protection thesis loses force — though the real yield calculation is unaffected for a holder who reaches maturity.
  • ECB rates rise further. A material increase in the ECB deposit rate would erode the real advantage of this bond relative to EUR cash. The linker currently leads EUR cash's real yield by 53 basis points. A significant rate rise could narrow or reverse that gap, bringing the cash-comparison leg of exit condition (1) closer to firing.
  • Liquidity in the German linker market dries up. German inflation-linked bonds trade in smaller volumes than their nominal counterparts. In a market stress scenario, the bid-ask spread widens and a forced seller pays a liquidity penalty.
  • Duration risk. With roughly 6.8 years to maturity and a modified duration of approximately 6.76 years, the bond's clean price is sensitive to changes in real interest rates. A 100-basis-point rise in real yields would cause the clean price to fall by roughly €6.76 per €100 of face value — a meaningful short-term drawdown for someone who cannot hold to maturity.
  • Eurostat index revision. The HICP methodology has been stable for years but is not immutable. A change that substituted a different inflation measure or materially restructured the index basket would alter the economic character of the bond, even if the change were modest by Eurostat's own standards.
