The Bottom Line

Data as of 2026-08-12 · Latest filing 2026-07-31.

The bet is wobbling. Amazon's cloud and advertising engines are performing well above their exit thresholds — but capital allocation has shifted materially, with $38.7 billion deployed into minority stakes in OpenAI and Anthropic in a single half-year, funded partly by nearly doubling long-term debt. No written exit condition has fired, yet the thesis assumption of acceptable capital discipline is now under strain. The share price sits in the upper half of the hold band, well above fair value. That much is settled — what remains open is whether the debt-funded AI minority bets prove to be the next chapter of Amazon's compounding story or a diversion from it.

About Amazon

Amazon makes its money across three interlocking businesses that feed one another. The oldest is online retail: a marketplace where Amazon itself sells goods alongside millions of third-party merchants, supported by a logistics network of fulfilment centres, delivery stations, and a proprietary air cargo operation that together can deliver over 13 billion packages to customers in the same or next day. Retail is not where the profit lives — the segment operates on thin margins — but it generates the customer traffic and purchasing-intent data that powers the second business.

That second business is digital advertising. Brands pay to appear in Amazon's search results and in streaming video, because a user searching "running shoes" on Amazon is already further along the purchase decision than a user browsing social media. Advertising brought in $68.6 billion in the year ending 2025, growing at 22%, and operates at very high margins. The third business is cloud computing: Amazon Web Services, which provides the infrastructure — storage, databases, artificial intelligence tools, and several hundred other services — that companies and governments use to run their software rather than operating their own data centres. AWS generated roughly $45.6 billion in operating profit in 2025 on around $107 billion in revenue, growing at 17% and now accelerating. The three businesses are mutually reinforcing: retail funds the logistics network that builds consumer trust; consumer data feeds advertising; advertising cash flows fund cloud expansion.

The Share Price

At $231, the share price sits above fair value. The method used here derives fair value from the cash the business produces relative to its market price — specifically, the ratio of owner earnings (operating cash flow, net of both capital spending and stock-based compensation) to market capitalisation. At today's price, that ratio stands at 4.5% — below the 6.0% that the rules define as fair value. A 6.0% yield would correspond to a share price of about $173.

Put plainly: the rules say the shares are worth around $173 today, based on the cash the business is currently producing. The market is paying $231, which is 33% above that figure. The share price sits in the upper half of the hold band — above fair value but below the trim edge at $260.

There is an important caveat, documented in Gaps in the Picture: a second valuation method that incorporates projected future earnings puts fair value considerably higher. The current owner-earnings yield anchors to what the business earns right now, during a period of extraordinarily heavy capital spending on AI infrastructure. If that spending yields the returns management expects, the current earnings are understated. The two methods disagree by roughly 60%, which is why the valuation picture carries a formal conflict warning.

Why the Market Sees It Differently

At $231, the market is paying 33% more than fair value as derived from the cash-yield method. That gap is described as material.

The difference between the market's price and the fair-value estimate implies an owner-earnings growth rate of about 7.8% annually — only 0.2 percentage points below the 8% base-case assumption built into the rules. In other words, the market and the rules are nearly aligned on long-term earnings growth. The premium exists for other reasons.

Three explanations are possible. The first is structural demand acceleration: the market may be crediting the business with sustaining earnings growth well above the conservative 8% anchor — perhaps 12–15% annually — as AI workloads compound on high-margin cloud infrastructure. The filing strongly supports this: cloud revenue grew 37% in the most recent quarter and advertising grew 26%, both substantially above any conservative baseline. The second explanation is quality expansion: cloud operating margins have expanded to 39.4%, and international commerce has turned sustainably profitable, adding a second margin tailwind. Both of these are also supported by the filing. The third explanation is strategic optionality: the $28.7 billion stake in OpenAI and $10 billion in Anthropic represent economic exposure to AI outcomes that the cash-yield method cannot see. If these stakes appreciate significantly, Amazon's economic book value exceeds what current cash flows can capture. This is partially supported — the stakes are real — but the mechanism for realising value for shareholders is not specified, and the debt-funded structure introduces its own risk.

What would have to happen for the market's premium to be right? Either the business sustains earnings growth materially above 8% as AI monetisation scales — for which the current quarter's 37% cloud growth is encouraging evidence — or the minority stakes prove strategically transformative in a way that shows up in future cash flows.

What's Wobbling

Capital allocation. The most significant wobble is the $38.7 billion in AI minority stakes deployed in a single half-year — $28.7 billion into OpenAI and $10 billion into Anthropic — paired with near-doubling of long-term debt and absent share repurchases. The written thesis accepted capital discipline described as "acceptable but not excellent," citing the absence of buybacks at the 2022–23 lows as a known imperfection. The H1 2026 pattern crosses from "not excellent" into challenged: the scale of concentrated minority bets in a single period, the debt-funding mechanism, and the absence of any stated mechanism for returning value to shareholders from these stakes collectively push beyond the tolerance implied in the original thesis. No written exit condition has fired — there is no specific dollar threshold for minority investments — but this driver is now formally challenged and warrants close attention at the next filing.

Capital spending trajectory. First-half 2026 capital spending on property and equipment reached $96.3 billion, and management guided that spending will increase further in 2026. The trailing twelve-month total stands at $169 billion. The written exit condition requires capital spending above $120 billion in both 2027 and 2028 without commensurate cloud acceleration — a two-year test that cannot fire until 2027 data is available, and that carries an explicit carve-out when cloud revenue is accelerating strongly (which it is). The wobble here is that the normalisation narrative for 2027–28 remains to be demonstrated; the FY2026 annual report will be the first clean test.

Valuation. The trailing twelve-month cash generated by the business, after subtracting all capital spending using management's definition, turned negative at −$7.6 billion. This is the investment-cycle reality, not a sign of business deterioration — the cash-yield method used to set the bands uses the narrower definition of capital spending. But it underscores how much the current valuation relies on future normalisation of cash flow rather than current earnings.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Cloud growth stalls. If AI workload demand turns out to have been pulled forward rather than structurally expanded, cloud revenue growth could decelerate below 15% within two to three quarters. At that point the exit condition fires and the central investment logic changes. The $169 billion of trailing capital spending would then represent overbuilt capacity generating sub-threshold returns.
  • Capital spending fails to earn its keep. The bear case is essentially this: $128 billion or more per year of infrastructure investment generates cloud revenue growth that decelerates rather than accelerating, leaving the business with high debt, no buyback capacity, and cash generation well below what the cash-yield method implies as a steady state.
  • FTC antitrust proceedings result in a structural remedy. The ongoing litigation could in a tail scenario require Amazon to separate its marketplace business or open its logistics network to third parties on regulated terms. This exit condition is immediate — no two-quarter confirmation required.
  • The OpenAI and Anthropic stakes underperform. The $38.7 billion in AI minority bets carries no governance rights over OpenAI and a non-voting structure on Anthropic. If the broader AI landscape commoditises or if either company fails to reach the scale implied by the investment, the capital deployed is at risk with no mechanism to protect it via board influence.
  • Tariff environment worsens. China-based sellers account for a significant share of marketplace revenue and third-party goods. A reversal of the tariff reduction seen in the most recent quarter, or an escalation of trade restrictions on Chinese-manufactured goods, would compress retail margins and could reduce advertising revenue as China-based sellers pull back.

Gaps in the Picture

The owner-earnings calculation uses the narrow definition of capital spending — the accounting line for purchases of property and equipment, which was $6.7 billion in the 2025 annual period. Management's own definition includes finance leases and leased data centre assets, bringing the total to approximately $128 billion for 2025. The two definitions produce very different owner-earnings figures, and both are disclosed. The rules use the narrower accounting-standard definition; those who apply management's definition would see a much lower — and currently negative — yield figure. This difference is described in the filing and is not a data error; it reflects genuine uncertainty about what portion of capital spending is truly maintenance versus growth investment.

A second valuation method, based on the prices at which comparable businesses trade relative to their projected future earnings, puts fair value at approximately $438 — roughly 60% above the $173 implied by the cash-yield method. The gap exists because that method anchors to current depressed cash flows while the scenario model looks through to projected future earnings once the AI capex cycle normalises. Both methods are disclosed; the conflict does not invalidate either approach, but it means the valuation picture carries more uncertainty than usual.

The Rules

The rules were set before any of the current readings were known. They are:

  • If the thesis is confirmed — all drivers reading positively and the price within the hold band — hold without adding. Continue compounding the cloud and advertising flywheel.
  • If the thesis is weakened — any driver challenged, as is currently the case — do not add to the position. Run the thesis re-validation against the specific deteriorating driver. Decide after the next two quarterly filings: one quarter of data, then confirm with a second.

Particular care: cloud revenue and margin exits require confirmation across two consecutive quarters before action; a court-ordered structural remedy or unexpected auditor change is an immediate exit.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
