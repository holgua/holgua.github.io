About PayPal

PayPal operates a two-sided digital payments network: consumers hold funds, make purchases, and send payments to other people; merchants accept those payments, access buy-now-pay-later financing, and use Braintree to process card and wallet transactions at scale. Every dollar that flows through the network earns PayPal a small fraction as a fee, and the company generated $33.2 billion in revenue in FY2025 from that mechanism across 200 countries and 439 million active accounts.

The network's value comes from its size: a buyer already enrolled on PayPal is immediately useful to any merchant that accepts it, and a merchant already accepting PayPal becomes immediately more attractive to the hundreds of millions of buyers on the platform. That self-reinforcing dynamic is what has kept PayPal at the centre of digital commerce for two decades despite sustained competition. The more recent challenge is that Apple Pay and Google Pay are built directly into smartphones, which means consumers can pay without ever opening a separate app — bypassing the PayPal layer entirely at the moment of checkout. PayPal's response is to invest in its own device-layer presence, to build an artificial-intelligence-driven checkout product called PayPal World, and to pursue a bank charter that would give it deposit-funded lending capabilities that pure payment processors cannot match. The bet is not that PayPal wins this contest — it is that the underlying cash flows are large enough and cheap enough at the current share price to deliver strong returns even in the scenario where the network share trend does not meaningfully reverse.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that PayPal's two-sided payments network generates enough cash to compound per-share earnings at a double-digit rate through buybacks, even as the growth rate of the underlying business stays modest. Five conditions must hold for that outcome:

  1. Branded checkout volume share does not decline by more than 2 percentage points in any fiscal year. A loss of that magnitude would signal secular displacement rather than a cyclical fluctuation — it would mean Apple Pay and Google Pay have structurally taken PayPal's position in the consumer checkout flow rather than just adding choice alongside it.

Latest reading: Not separately disclosed for the three months ending 2026-03-31. Total payment volume grew 11% year-over-year, and PayPal-branded transaction revenue grew approximately $140 million year-over-year, suggesting branded volumes are rising in absolute terms. The branded checkout share relative to total online checkout is not calculable from the quarterly filing. Two class actions at the pleadings stage allege undisclosed impediments to the branded checkout growth strategy; no evidence of a 2-percentage-point share loss has been filed or ruled on.

  1. No consent order from the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, the Department of Justice, or the Federal Trade Commission that materially restricts core product features. A consent order of this kind would constrain the product and impose compliance costs that structurally reduce earnings power — qualitatively different from an open investigation that imposes only legal fees.

Latest reading: As of 2026-03-31, no consent order exists. Two civil investigative demands from the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (relating to error-resolution obligations) and two from the Federal Trade Commission remain open and are being cooperated with. In March 2026, the UK Financial Conduct Authority opened a new investigation under UK competition law into PayPal's contractual agreements with Visa and Mastercard. The PPH Securities class action was dismissed with prejudice on 2026-03-31. Not one of these proceedings has produced a consent order or a product restriction — exit condition 2 is not triggered.

  1. Consumer credit charge-off rate remains below 10%. PayPal operates a buy-now-pay-later lending business; if borrower defaults rise above the 10% ceiling, the credit book becomes a liability rather than an asset, and the capital that supports it can no longer be deployed into buybacks.

Latest reading: Consumer net charge-off rate 3.0% annualised as of 2026-03-31, down from 4.5% in the same period a year earlier. The share of consumer loans that are current (not past due) held steady at 96.3%. Transaction loss rate 0.06%. All three readings are well below the threshold.

  1. Management maintains the buyback programme and does not take on material debt above 2.0 times net operating earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortisation. The investment case is built on per-share compounding at depressed prices; abandoning the programme or leveraging up the balance sheet would remove the mechanism.

Latest reading: 34 million shares repurchased in the three months ending 2026-03-31 for $1.5 billion at an average of $44.60 per share. $12.4 billion of authorisation remains. Net cash position is $14.0 billion. April 2026 reorganisation is funded through cost savings, not incremental debt. Capital-return condition is intact.

  1. GAAP operating income remains above $5.5 billion in any fiscal year, and management does not guide non-GAAP operating income below $5.0 billion for the following year. This is the margin-discipline test: if operating income falls below this floor, the earnings power of the business has deteriorated enough to undermine the yield-based valuation.

Latest reading: FY2025 GAAP operating income was $6,065 million, 13.9% higher than FY2024 and 10% above the $5.5 billion floor. Q1 2026 GAAP operating income was $1,488 million (18% margin), down from $1,530 million (20% margin) in Q1 2025, primarily because of a higher mix of Braintree processing — which carries higher transaction costs — rather than because of broad pricing deterioration. The floor is not in danger at current run rates.

The Share Price

The shares trade at $56. By the Rules governing this evaluation, the fair-value anchor — the price at which the business's owner-earnings stream is capitalised at the threshold yield of 10.5% — corresponds to $48 per share. The current share price is approximately 16% above that anchor, which places it in the upper half of the hold band: above fair value but well short of the trim line at $60.

To see the full picture, consider what the yield numbers mean in plain terms. At $56, the business is generating around $4.5 billion a year in earnings on an economically adjusted basis (operating cash flow minus capital spending, minus stock-based compensation that dilutes existing shareholders), and the market is paying roughly 11 times that figure. Historically, markets have paid 15 to 20 times comparable earnings for a business with PayPal's profile. The current multiple implies either that the market expects earnings to decline from here, or that the business deserves a structurally lower multiple because its growth engine is impaired. The financial data through FY2025 does not confirm the first expectation; the question of whether the second is warranted turns on branded checkout, which is exactly the condition that cannot yet be measured cleanly.

The share price is not cheap by the Rules — it is above fair value. But it is also not expensive enough to trigger a trim: that line sits at $60, and patience is called for in the meantime.

What's Wobbling

Three drivers returned a mixed reading in the Q1 2026 filing, and not one of them crosses a written exit condition — but the composition of the wobbles is worth understanding, because they all trace to the same underlying question.

Branded checkout disclosure gap. PayPal does not separately report branded checkout volume in its quarterly filings, which means the thesis's most important condition cannot be measured directly each quarter. What is visible is that total payment volume grew 11%, that PayPal-branded revenues grew, and that Braintree (the enterprise payment-processing arm) is growing faster than the PayPal-branded side. The two pending class-action lawsuits add a layer of concern: if the allegations that management withheld material information about the branded checkout trajectory are eventually substantiated, that would be a governance issue on top of an operational one. At the pleadings stage, they are accusations, not findings. The wobble is a missing signal rather than a negative one — but missing signals on the thesis's central condition are not comfortable.

Active account engagement. Active accounts grew 1% year-over-year to 439 million, and the number of transactions per active account fell 1% to 58.7 from 59.4. Neither reading is a deteriorating trend in isolation — one quarter of flat engagement is not a structural shift. But the combination of flat account growth and slightly declining engagement per account means the network is not expanding its per-user intensity, which is what the bull case requires. This wobble belongs in What's Wobbling rather than in the exit-condition list because the thesis's bet is not on engagement intensity per se — it is on per-share earnings compounding through buybacks. That compounding can work even with flat engagement if buybacks continue.

Operating margin at the quarterly level. The 18% GAAP operating margin in Q1 2026 versus 20% in Q1 2025 looks like margin compression. The underlying cause is Braintree's higher transaction expense rate — every dollar of Braintree volume costs more to process than a dollar of branded PayPal volume. Full-year FY2025 operating income was $6,065 million, 14% above FY2024 and 10% above the exit-condition floor of $5.5 billion. The quarterly dip does not break any rule. The merchant net charge-off rate, which rose to 7.0% from 4.6% a year earlier, is worth tracking — not because it is near an exit condition (the written threshold is on consumer credit, not merchant credit) but because merchant credit quality can signal broader platform stress before it shows up in consumer metrics.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Device-native disintermediation accelerates. If Apple Pay and Google Pay extend their reach beyond the iPhone and Android ecosystems to include in-app and in-shop flows that currently run through Braintree, the branded checkout share loss could cross the 2-percentage-point exit threshold in a single fiscal year. This is the scenario that the current filing cannot rule out.
  • New chief executive pursues a value-destructive acquisition. Enrique Lores took the chief executive role in February 2026 with a mandate for strategic review. The $14 billion net cash balance and the history of a poorly received acquisition (Honey, 2020) are both present in the background; a large acquisition at a premium price would consume the buyback capacity that powers the current per-share compounding.
  • Consumer credit cycle turns. The 3.0% charge-off rate in Q1 2026 is healthy, but buy-now-pay-later credit quality is sensitive to unemployment and consumer stress. A recession scenario that pushes charge-offs toward 7–8% would compress earnings and potentially force the company to reduce its buyback to rebuild loan-loss reserves.
  • Regulatory proceedings escalate to operational restrictions. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau civil investigative demands and the Federal Trade Commission investigations are open. An escalation to a consent order restricting fee structures or product features would directly reduce the earnings yield that underpins the valuation.
  • Merchant net charge-off rate continues to rise. The 7.0% rate in Q1 2026 is not covered by the exit conditions, but if it rises to the levels that would impair the merchant lending book, it could force provisioning that reduces reported earnings below the operating income floor in exit condition 5.

Gaps in the Picture

Two caveats attach to the numbers underlying this evaluation.

First, the earnings-based calculation deducts stock-based compensation from operating cash flow to arrive at an economically dilution-adjusted figure. The maintenance capital expenditure component is not separately disclosed by PayPal — the filing does not distinguish between capital spending to maintain existing infrastructure and capital spending to build new capabilities. Total capital expenditure ($852 million in FY2025) is used as the maintenance proxy, which is conservative for an asset-light software platform; the true maintenance figure is likely lower, which would make the earnings figure somewhat higher than shown.

Second, the Fair-Value-Bands model — a separate three-scenario discounted cash flow computation that this pipeline also runs — produces a base-case central estimate of $125 per share, implying an earnings yield of 4.1% rather than the 10.5% used as the fair-value anchor in this evaluation. The two approaches are not in conflict: the earnings yield method uses a static capitalisation of current earnings, while the bands model uses a multi-year growth forecast. But the divergence of 61% in the implied yields is large enough to warrant a review of the fair-value parameter in the holding's settings before acting on the trim-edge signal. Neither number is wrong; they are asking different questions about how much growth credit to assign to future cash flows.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
