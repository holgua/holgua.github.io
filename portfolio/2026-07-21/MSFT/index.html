Microsoft: The Bet Still Works

The Bottom Line

Data as of 2026-07-20 · Latest filing 2026-04-29.

The bet is working. Azure grew 40% year-on-year for the third consecutive quarter ended March 2026, commercial committed backlog surged 99% to $627 billion, and operating margin held at 46.3% — each of the seven written exit conditions remains untriggered. At $392, the price sits at the trim edge and well above fair value, as detailed in The Share Price. The long-run expected return on the position is about 14% a year at today's price — a healthy equity premium over the risk-free rate. The condition to watch is the cash-to-earnings conversion ratio: nine months into fiscal year 2026, the ratio of free cash flow to net income has compressed to 0.48 against a 0.65 floor, and the full-year verdict arrives when the fiscal year closes in June 2026.

Recent Development

The three months ended March 2026 produced the most important single data point in Microsoft's current investment thesis: Azure grew 40% year-on-year for the third consecutive quarter, exactly matching the prior quarter's rate and substantially above the 33% it achieved in the same period a year earlier. That consistency matters more than any single print — it confirms that the growth is structural rather than a one-period rebound.

The second load-bearing development was the commercial contracted backlog reaching $627 billion, a 99% increase year-on-year. This figure represents enterprise commitments to future Microsoft cloud spending that have already been signed but not yet recognised as revenue. At roughly two and a half years' worth of current revenue, it offers unusually long visibility into the forward trajectory.

Two signals are mixed rather than confirmatory. Free cash flow as a fraction of net income fell to approximately 0.48 over the first nine months of the fiscal year, well below the 0.65 monitoring threshold — but the written exit condition requires two consecutive full fiscal years below that level, and the prior full year came in at 0.70. Capital expenditure also reached 33% of revenue over the same period, exceeding the 30% bear trigger — but that trigger only fires in combination with Azure growth falling below 25%, which the 40% print prevents. Both are signals to track rather than signals that have crossed a line. A full assessment of the Share Price is in the section below.

About Microsoft

Microsoft makes its money by selling software subscriptions and cloud computing capacity to businesses that find it technically and commercially prohibitive to switch providers. The largest revenue stream is its cloud platform, Azure, which competes with Amazon Web Services and Google Cloud for the computing workloads that enterprises have been moving off their own data centres over the past decade. The second stream is the Microsoft 365 subscription — the bundle of email, document-editing, video-conferencing, and productivity tools used by roughly 400 million commercial seats worldwide. The third is gaming and consumer products, which is smaller and less strategically central.

The connective tissue between these businesses is Microsoft's identity and authentication system, Azure Active Directory — now branded Entra ID — which manages who can access what across an enterprise's entire digital estate. Because every application, device, and security policy is registered against this system, migrating away from Microsoft is not a subscription cancellation but a full rebuild of the organisation's technology foundation. That lock-in is the economic moat the investment thesis rests on. The company's return on invested capital has held between 22% and 27% for five consecutive fiscal years, far above the 8–10% cost of capital, which is the measurable consequence of that moat.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that Microsoft's enterprise identity lock-in is durable enough to sustain compounding earnings growth for a decade, and that the current surge in artificial-intelligence infrastructure spending is building a wider moat rather than destroying capital. The specific conditions that must hold for this to remain true are:

  1. Azure quarterly revenue growth stays above 25% while capital expenditure stays above 20% of revenue. The investment thesis treats the combination of decelerating Azure growth and sustained heavy capital spending as the primary signal that the artificial-intelligence infrastructure bet is failing to monetise. Either condition alone is not the trigger — both must be present simultaneously for two consecutive quarters.

Latest reading: Azure grew 40% year-on-year in the three months ended March 2026, matching the rate recorded in the three months ended September 2025. Capital expenditure ran at 37% of revenue in the same quarter. The Azure condition is comfortably met; the compound exit trigger is not approached.

  1. The ratio of free cash flow to net income stays above 0.65 on an annual basis, or Azure AI revenue contribution continues improving. A sustained collapse in cash conversion — below 0.65 for two consecutive fiscal years — combined with no improvement in the artificial-intelligence revenue contribution would signal that capital spending is permanently impairing earnings rather than temporarily front-running future revenue.

Latest reading: The nine-month free-cash-flow-to-net-income ratio was approximately 0.48 as of March 2026, below the threshold. The full fiscal year closes June 2026; the prior full year came in at 0.70. One data point does not constitute the required two-year sequence. Azure's 40% growth directly contradicts the "no AI revenue improvement" leg of the condition.

  1. Consolidated operating margin stays above 42%. Two consecutive quarters below this level would signal structural margin compression beyond the temporary effects of the infrastructure investment cycle.

Latest reading: Operating margin was 46.3% in the three months ended March 2026 and 47.4% over the full nine months — more than four percentage points above the exit threshold and expanding year-on-year.

  1. The United States Internal Revenue Service dispute stays out of Tax Court, and no single quarterly tax charge exceeds $5 billion. Escalation to Tax Court would introduce a timeline and an outcome range that the administrative appeals process does not. A discrete quarterly charge above $5 billion would signal that a settlement is being provisioned at a level that could strain the balance sheet.

Latest reading: The $28.9 billion outstanding claim remains in administrative appeals as of March 2026. Microsoft's disclosure language is unchanged from prior quarters. No Tax Court filing, no extraordinary charge. The condition is not triggered.

  1. No formal competition-law complaint seeking structural remedies is filed. A complaint from the United States Federal Trade Commission seeking to compel Microsoft to separate its cloud infrastructure from its identity products, or to divest specific businesses, would directly attack the switching-cost moat.

Latest reading: The latest quarterly filing acknowledges ongoing regulatory scrutiny in general terms but discloses no formal complaint seeking structural remedies. The condition is not triggered.

  1. Consolidated gross margin stays above 65%. Two consecutive quarters below this level would signal that the cost of artificial-intelligence infrastructure is being passed through in a way that compresses the economics of the core software franchise.

Latest reading: Consolidated gross margin was 67.6% in the three months ended March 2026 and 68.2% over the nine months. The Microsoft Cloud gross margin compressed to 66% due to infrastructure cost absorption, but the consolidated figure is comfortably above the threshold.

  1. Commercial contracted backlog continues growing above 30% year-on-year and does not decline in absolute terms quarter-on-quarter. Slowing or reversing backlog growth would signal that enterprise commitments are weakening — the earliest leading indicator that the monetisation thesis is failing.

Latest reading: Commercial contracted backlog reached $627 billion as of March 2026, 99% higher than a year earlier. The condition is not approached.

The Share Price

The share price of $392 sits at the trim edge of the valuation band — 2.4% below the $402 level at which the Rules call for trimming the position. The valuation method translates earnings into a yield: at $392, owner earnings (net income used as a proxy, since the cost of maintaining the existing business and the associated depreciation broadly cancel) divided by the total market value of the company produces a yield of approximately 2.1%. The fair-value anchor is 3.5%, which corresponds to a share price of $230.

That gap — $392 against a fair-value price of $230 — means the market is pricing in approximately a decade of continued earnings growth beyond what the existing asset base already earns. The market divergence does not meet the threshold that would require a dedicated reconciliation section, which reflects that the implied growth expectations, while elevated, are not implausible for a business compounding at 15–20% annually. In other words, the current price is not irrational on the surface — it simply offers no margin of safety.

For existing holders, the price sitting at the trim edge is an observation, not a trigger. The Rules call for trimming when the price closes at or above $402, not when it is 2.4% below that level. The standing orders are stated precisely in The Standing Orders below.

The Standing Orders

The Rules translate the share-price bands into three standing orders and two price alerts. Each operationalises a pre-committed decision.

Price alerts (informational).
- A first alert at $230, the fair-value anchor. The crossing means the share price has returned to a neutral reading — the price where an earnings yield of 3.5% is restored. No action follows automatically, but a fresh review of all nine conditions is warranted before any add.
- A second alert at $161, the cheap-edge. The crossing arms the buy order below; before adding, the latest re-validation must confirm no exit condition has been triggered.

Standing orders (actionable).
- A standing buy order at $161. Once the share price closes at or below this level and the conditions are still working, a position add proceeds in tranches. The order may be staged across one or two quarters rather than filled in a single transaction. At $161, an earnings yield of 5.0% is reached — the level at which the expected long-run total return, including assumed growth, rises to approximately 17% a year.
- A standing trim order at $402. When the share price closes at or above this level, 20–25% of the position is trimmed to harvest some of the premium while retaining the core compounding position. The trim does not reduce the holding below 50% of current size unless an exit condition also fires.
- A standing exit order at $1,607. When the share price closes at or above this level, the full position is closed. At that price, an earnings yield of 1.5% is all that remains — less than half the current risk-free rate and below the yield available on German government bonds. The market would be pricing in the full bull-case fair value with no further upside optionality. Proceeds are redeployed at the next available opportunity, with no re-entry within 90 days to defuse the psychological pull of a recently exited position.

The three order levels do not contradict the cost-basis verdicts above: those verdicts describe the current reading; the standing orders describe what executes when each price line is crossed.

What's Wobbling

Two of the nine conditions are reading mixed rather than confirmatory, and they are related.

The first is capital expenditure intensity. Spending on property and equipment reached 37% of revenue in the three months ended March 2026 — the highest rate in the company's history and well above the 30% bear-trigger level. The trigger is a compound condition: it only fires when Azure growth simultaneously falls below 25% for two consecutive quarters. Azure at 40% prevents that combination from being reached. But the spending itself is not trivial: $80 billion in the first nine months of the fiscal year, against $196 billion of data-centre leases not yet commenced, means the capital commitment extends years into the future. The thesis is that this spending is buying future contracted revenue, and the $627 billion backlog is the evidence. The wobble is that the evidence is forward-looking — it will take several more years of revenue conversion to confirm that the returns on this capital justify the outlay.

The second wobble is the cash-to-earnings conversion ratio. Free cash flow over the first nine months of fiscal year 2026 was approximately 48% of net income — compared with 70% for the full fiscal year 2025 and 84% for fiscal year 2024. The decline is mechanically explained by the capital expenditure surge: more spending means less cash left over after building. The written condition requires the ratio to remain below 0.65 for two consecutive full fiscal years before the exit review is triggered, and one year of compressed conversion does not constitute that. The full-year verdict arrives when the fiscal year closes in June 2026. If the ratio comes in below 0.65 for FY2026, the clock starts for the two-year sequence.

Neither wobble crosses a written line. Both require monitoring at the next annual reporting date. See The Case for the Bet for the precise thresholds.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Azure growth decelerates while capital spending stays elevated. If Azure's year-on-year growth rate falls below 25% for two consecutive quarters while capital expenditure remains above 20% of revenue, exit condition one fires. Open-source artificial-intelligence models from Meta, Google DeepMind, and others are eroding the differentiation advantage of Microsoft's Azure OpenAI partnership — if this commoditisation accelerates, the Azure growth engine could stall while the data-centre building costs remain fixed.

  • The cash-to-earnings conversion ratio stays depressed for two years. If the FY2026 full-year ratio comes in below 0.65 and FY2027 shows no recovery despite improving Azure revenue, exit condition two fires. The specific risk is that $196 billion of uncommenced data-centre lease commitments — which span to 2031 — continue absorbing cash at a rate that outpaces revenue growth.

  • The Federal Trade Commission files a formal antitrust complaint seeking structural remedies. The agency has been gathering documents and conducting interviews since late 2024 under a broad scope covering cloud bundling, artificial-intelligence integration, and licensing terms. A formal complaint seeking to separate Microsoft's identity products from its cloud platform would directly attack the switching-cost moat that underpins the thesis.

  • The Internal Revenue Service dispute escalates to Tax Court. The outstanding claim of $28.9 billion plus penalties and interest — if it moved from administrative appeals to litigation — would introduce a multi-year legal process and an uncertain settlement range that the current balance sheet, with $78 billion in liquid assets and $46 billion in debt, could absorb but not comfortably ignore.

  • The artificial-intelligence infrastructure bet fails to monetise before the lease obligations come due. $196 billion in uncommenced data-centre leases locks Microsoft into capital commitments through 2031. If enterprise adoption of artificial-intelligence services does not scale at the pace implied by current contracted backlog conversions, the fixed-cost base will weigh on margins for years beyond the current investment cycle.

Gaps in the Picture

Artificial-intelligence annual recurring revenue — cited in the written thesis at $37 billion as of an earlier period — is not disclosed as a formal figure in this quarterly filing. The filing references Copilot as a driver of revenue per user and as a cost driver (advertising expenses), but no standalone figure is available for comparison with the $37 billion baseline. The condition that monitors AI revenue scaling (condition two in The Case for the Bet) cannot be numerically verified from this filing alone.

The fair-value anchor in the analysis uses net income as a proxy for owner earnings — a simplification that assumes the company's annual depreciation and its annual maintenance capital expenditure roughly cancel each other out. The proxy is reasonable for Microsoft's software and subscription businesses, but the AI infrastructure surge has widened the gap between total capital expenditure and estimated maintenance capital expenditure substantially. At some point the depreciation charge will catch up with the past investment cycle, and the proxy will need revisiting.

The valuation context notes two fair-value references that do not agree with each other: the analytical anchor of 3.5% earnings yield (implying a share price of approximately $388 as fair value) and the forward-returns model's central estimate of $566 (implying a yield of approximately 1.4%). The two figures reflect different earnings bases — net income versus free cash flow net of stock-based compensation — and the gap between them is a data-quality caveat, not an error. Both are surfaced here for completeness; neither changes the band-position verdict at $392, which falls within the hold range on both bases.

The Rules

If the thesis is confirmed — all nine conditions working, no exit condition triggered, share price inside the hold band — then no action is taken. The position compounds at the rate of the underlying business.

If the thesis weakens — any condition deteriorates but no written exit threshold has been crossed for the required consecutive periods — then no position is added. The specific deteriorating condition is reviewed against the next quarterly filing before any reassessment of size.

If the thesis breaks — any one of the seven written exit conditions fires — then the full position is exited within 30 days, with no averaging down regardless of the price at that moment.

If the share price falls to or below $161 — where an earnings yield of 5.0% is reached — then a position add is initiated in tranches, provided no exit condition has triggered. The first tranche activates below $352 (which corresponds to the 40% margin of safety against the base-case fair value of $587); the second tranche activates below $322 (40% margin of safety against the discounted-cash-flow intrinsic value of $429).

If the share price rises to or above $402 — where an earnings yield of 2.0% is implied — then 20–25% of the position is trimmed to harvest the premium, with the remaining position retained unless an exit condition also fires.

If the share price rises to or above $1,607 — where an earnings yield of 1.5% would be implied, below half the current risk-free rate — then the full position is exited and the proceeds are redeployed. No re-entry within 90 days.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
