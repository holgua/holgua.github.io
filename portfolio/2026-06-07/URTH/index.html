All World: The Bet Is Wobbling

The Bottom Line

The bet on this global developed-markets fund — that the basket of about 1,300 large companies across 23 wealthy countries will, over a multi-year holding period, pay better than risk-free government bonds plus a meaningful cushion for the trouble of taking equity risk — is wobbling. The first measurement of that cushion came in negative: the basket's earnings yield sits below the 10-year government-bond rate, which means risk-free paper currently pays more than the underlying businesses' trailing profits. None of the safety-net conditions that would force an exit have triggered; the fund is cheap to own, it is large and liquid, and the businesses inside it are high-quality. The wobble is purely in the price paid for those earnings.

The Verdict

The latest reading puts the fund in "wobbling" territory: the price test that the bet hinges on is failing today, but every operational guardrail (fund size, fees, single-name concentration) is intact. What follows depends on the price paid for the shares.

  • As an existing investment bought below $200 per share — currently sitting on a gain of any size. Hold. The price test is failing today but no exit condition has triggered, the quality of the underlying businesses is high, and the fund itself is sound. A gain at this price-test reading is not a reason to harvest; the rules trim only when the basket gets meaningfully expensive or exit when an operational guardrail breaks. Neither has happened.
  • As an existing investment bought between $200 and $250 per share — currently somewhere between a small loss of about 20 percent and a tiny gain near break-even. Hold. Today's share price near $200 sits at the upper edge of this band. The fund is wobbling on price but not broken on structure. Adding more is not warranted; waiting for either the price test to recover or the next quarterly print of the cushion is the discipline.
  • As an existing investment bought between $250 and $300 per share — currently showing a loss of roughly 20 to 33 percent. Hold. A mid-range loss combined with a wobbling-but-not-broken bet does not call for selling. The basket's compounding engine is intact, fees are low, the fund is liquid, and the businesses inside continue to earn high returns on the capital deployed inside them. Capitulating now locks in the loss while the case still has time to mend.
  • As an existing investment bought above $300 per share — currently underwater by 33 percent or more. Hold. A deep loss paired with a still-functional fund and a still-high-quality basket does not justify selling. The structural guardrails are all in order; the wobble is on price, not on what the fund owns. Selling here would crystallise the loss precisely at the point where the underlying engine remains sound.
  • As a fresh investment at today's price of around $200 per share. Wait. There is no margin of safety at this entry point — the basket is priced richly enough that government bonds pay better today, and a meaningful new commitment requires the share price to fall into the clearly-cheap range around $135 before the price test gives a green light.

About the Fund

The fund is a passive index basket holding shares in roughly 1,300 large and mid-sized companies across 23 wealthy countries — the United States accounts for about 70 percent of the basket, with the remaining 30 percent spread across Japan, the United Kingdom, France, Switzerland, Germany, Canada, and others. It tracks an index that selects companies by the size of their freely-tradeable share base. The top of the basket is the same set of large American technology names that dominate other broad indexes — Nvidia, Apple, Alphabet, Microsoft, Amazon, Broadcom, Meta, Tesla, and Micron — though no single company sits at more than 6 percent of the fund.

The role this fund plays inside a portfolio is "global core equity": one decision that buys the whole developed-markets stock universe in one ticker, blending the dominant American technology weighting with a 30-percent slice of European and Asian companies that would otherwise be missing. That non-American slice is the reason to hold this fund rather than an American-only equivalent — it dilutes the regime-risk of betting everything on one country's stock market.

The Case for the Bet

The bet rests on six numbered conditions. As long as they hold, the fund is a sound vehicle to own. If any one breaks, the case shifts from "hold" to "exit".

  1. The earnings cushion above risk-free bonds returns to acceptable territory over the holding period. The bet does not require the cushion to be positive every quarter; it requires the average over the multi-year holding period to land at or above 2 percentage points. A formal exit triggers only if the basket's earnings yield falls below 3 percent at the same time as the cushion is negative, and that combination holds for two consecutive quarters.
  2. The fund's annual fee stays at or below 0.35 percent. Today it is 0.24 percent. A meaningfully cheaper sibling fund domiciled in Europe (the Irish "accumulating" version of the same index basket) charges 0.20 percent, so a fee hike above the 0.35 percent line would signal that the cheaper structure has become the obviously better choice.
  3. The fund stays large enough to trade tightly. Today it manages about $8.1 billion. Below roughly $2 billion, the gap between the buying and selling price of the shares (the "spread") would typically widen materially for a global blend basket, making the fund costlier to enter and exit in size.
  4. No single company comes to dominate the basket. The largest holding today (Nvidia) is 5.7 percent of the fund. A line at 10 percent marks the point where a cap-weighted global index has tilted into single-name regime-risk that is no longer normal market drift.
  5. The index methodology is not gutted by its publisher. The case assumes the underlying index continues to select large and mid-sized companies across the same developed countries using the same share-base weighting. A formal restructuring by the index publisher that dropped major countries, dropped major companies, or moved to a fundamentally different weighting scheme would undermine the mandate the fund was bought for.
  6. The underlying businesses keep earning well on the capital they put to work. The middle company among the top 10 currently earns 37 percent on its own book of equity — a high-quality reading. A collapse of that middle figure below 15 percent would mean the basket has stopped being a compounding vehicle.

The Rules

The rules that govern what action follows from each combination of conditions and share price are fixed.

  • If all conditions are intact. Hold. No action. Let the compounding continue.
  • If any condition is wobbling but none has clearly broken. Do not add. Watch the wobbling condition through the next quarterly read — then a second — before reconsidering. If the price cushion stays negative or the middle-business return-on-equity keeps falling, switch into the cheaper European-domiciled sibling fund, or move the capital to cash.
  • If any condition has clearly broken or an exit line is crossed. Exit. Move the proceeds into risk-free government bonds or into a holding that meets the price test.
  • If the share price has dropped into the clearly-cheap range and the conditions are still working. Add to the position. The basket is meaningfully cheap against risk-free bonds.
  • If the share price has run into the clearly-expensive range. Trim a quarter to a third of the position. The basket is meaningfully expensive against risk-free bonds; harvest the premium and keep a core holding.

What's Wobbling

The one open issue is the price cushion above risk-free bonds. The latest reading puts it at negative 0.32 of a percentage point — the basket's trailing profits pay less than the 10-year government-bond rate. The bet demands an average cushion of at least 2 percentage points over the multi-year holding period; today's snapshot is 2.3 percentage points short of that bar.

The case allows for this. The bet was never that the cushion must be positive on every snapshot — it was that it averages above 2 percentage points across the holding period. The thesis was written when the cushion was already slightly negative (a reading of negative 0.37 of a percentage point on 2026-06-05), and the today's snapshot is essentially the same shape. The formal exit trigger requires the cushion to stay negative for two consecutive quarters and the basket's earnings yield to fall below 3 percent — that compound trigger has not fired.

Watching the next quarterly print of the cushion is the single most important monitoring task. A continued negative reading combined with a falling middle return on capital would shift the verdict from wobbling to broken, and the rules would then call for switching to the cheaper European sibling fund or moving the capital to cash.

What Could Break the Fund

The structural conditions that, if breached, would force an exit are distinct from the share-price test above and operate on the fund itself rather than the market price.

  • The fee creeps above 0.35 percent. The sponsor (iShares, owned by BlackRock) currently charges 0.24 percent. A drift above 0.35 percent, given that a cheaper European-domiciled sibling at 0.20 percent already exists, would signal that the sponsor has stopped competing on price for this category and that the cheaper sibling is the obviously better vehicle.
  • The fund shrinks below $2 billion in assets. Today's $8.1 billion makes a 75-percent drawdown necessary to reach the floor. Below it, the gap between buying and selling prices on the shares would typically widen, making the fund costly to enter and exit at scale.
  • A single company climbs above 10 percent of the basket. Nvidia is the largest at 5.73 percent today. A move to 10 percent would mean the basket has tilted into single-name dominance that the cap-weighted global mandate was supposed to dilute, not concentrate.
  • The underlying index is restructured by its publisher. A formal change to the index — dropping countries, dropping major constituent companies, or moving to a fundamentally different weighting scheme — would undermine the mandate the fund was bought to express.
  • The middle business's return on capital collapses below 15 percent. The top-10 middle figure today is 37 percent. A collapse below 15 percent would mean the basket is no longer composed of high-quality compounders and the case for owning a passive global basket weakens substantially.

None of these is approached today. The wobble is purely on the share-price side; the fund itself is sound.

Gaps in the Picture

A few things in the underlying figures are worth flagging plainly.

The "earnings cushion" used to decide cheap and expensive is built on the basket's trailing 12-month profits — i.e. profits already booked. A cyclical-aware version would smooth those profits across a full economic cycle. A smoothed version is unavailable for an index basket using freely-available data; the conclusion is that the current cushion reading should be treated as one snapshot, not a verdict.

The 37-percent middle-business return-on-capital figure is the trustworthy read on basket quality, but it sits alongside a 69-percent weighted-average figure that overstates the truth. Apple's reported return on its own book equity is 141 percent; Nvidia's is 114 percent. These figures are mathematically inflated because both companies have shrunk their book equity through years of share buy-backs, making the denominator artificially small. The middle company in the top 10 is the trustworthy signal; the average is not.

The 30-percent technology weighting in the basket has narrowly crossed the level at which the basket can no longer be described as sector-diffuse. A sustained de-rating of the technology sector would drive the basket's returns disproportionately. This is the largest second-order risk inside an otherwise mandate-compliant fund.

The cushion reading is from one snapshot date. Quarter-on-quarter movements in the 10-year government-bond rate, or quarter-on-quarter movements in the basket's underlying profits, can shift the cushion materially. The wobble verdict is anchored to a single snapshot; the case will be re-read on the next quarterly snapshot.
