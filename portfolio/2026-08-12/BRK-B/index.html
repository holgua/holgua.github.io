Recent Development

The three months ending in June 2026 confirmed that Berkshire's compounding engine is broadly intact — and that one part of it is misfiring in a way that warrants close attention.

The headline from the filing is the buyback: management repurchased $4.5 billion of stock in the second quarter alone, the largest single-quarter repurchase in recent memory. That number matters because it is a direct statement from Abel and Buffett that the share price, in the $490s, sits below their estimate of intrinsic value. Book value per share grew approximately 12.1% over the twelve months ending in June 2026, comfortably ahead of any plausible broad-market benchmark — the primary test against which the thesis is measured. Operating earnings (excluding investment gains) grew 5.1% in the first half of 2026 versus the same period a year earlier, led by manufacturing and services (+15.1%), the freight-rail business (+9.5%), and the energy subsidiary (+11.5%). The insurance float reached $177.5 billion, up $1.1 billion from year-end, at a negative cost — meaning the insurance businesses collectively generated an underwriting profit, so the float is genuinely free.

The singular concern is Berkshire's car-insurance business, GEICO. Its combined ratio — the share of every premium dollar paid out in claims and expenses, where anything below 100% signals profit — deteriorated to 89.3% in the first half of 2026 from 81.7% in the same period a year earlier, a 7.6 percentage-point move in the wrong direction. Underwriting profit fell 40% to $2.4 billion. Both the frequency and the severity of claims rose, with bodily-injury claim frequencies up five to seven per cent and severities up ten to twelve per cent. Advertising spend surged, suggesting GEICO is paying aggressively to recover market share lost to competitors with more advanced pricing technology. Not one of these developments crosses a written exit condition — underwriting is still profitable, and the overall insurance result actually improved because the freight-rail and energy businesses recovered from wildfire losses that depressed last year's figure. But the deterioration now spans two consecutive reporting periods, which is what moved the thesis from stable to wobbling. About Berkshire

Berkshire Hathaway is a holding company that owns a diversified collection of operating businesses and a large portfolio of publicly traded shares, all assembled over six decades under Warren Buffett and now run by Greg Abel, who became chief executive at the start of 2026. The operating businesses span car insurance (Berkshire's car-insurance business, GEICO), freight rail (Berkshire's railway subsidiary, BNSF, which runs a 32,500-route-mile network across the western United States), regulated electricity and gas utilities (Berkshire Hathaway Energy, known as BHE), and a broad mix of manufacturers, retailers, and service businesses ranging from precision components to pilot training. On top of these operating subsidiaries, Berkshire holds roughly $400 billion in publicly traded equities — a concentrated portfolio funded partly by the insurance float, a pool of money that policyholders have paid in premiums but that Berkshire has not yet paid out in claims.

The insurance float is the structural engine of the business. At $177.5 billion, it costs Berkshire nothing — because the insurance operations generate an underwriting profit, the float is effectively free leverage that can be deployed into equities and owned businesses at management's discretion. No competitor can legally or economically replicate this: it took six decades of profitable underwriting to build, and its self-reinforcing nature (scale attracts more business, which deepens the statistical edge, which allows better pricing) means the moat compounds alongside the float itself. The operating businesses — rail, energy, and manufacturing — each carry their own durable advantages: BNSF's track network cannot be economically rebuilt; BHE's utility service territories give it a captive customer base; and several of the manufacturing subsidiaries operate in specialty markets where scale and reputation create lasting pricing power. Berkshire does not pay a dividend; the entire investment thesis rests on management's ability to keep reinvesting earnings and float into high-returning assets at above-market rates.

The Case for the Bet

The central bet is that Greg Abel and the management culture Warren Buffett built will continue to reinvest retained earnings and the insurance float into high-returning businesses and securities at rates exceeding what a passive allocation to a broad index could achieve. The specific conditions that must hold for this bet to keep working are:

  1. Book value per share grows at or above the broad market total return, measured over rolling five-year periods. If book value per share trails the MSCI World total return for five or more consecutive years, the compounding edge has gone and the holding's rationale disappears.

Latest reading: Per-Class-A-equivalent book value grew approximately 12.1% in the twelve months to June 2026 — well ahead of any plausible MSCI World total-return reading for that period. Shareholders' equity reached $747.9 billion at June 30, 2026, up from $670.3 billion a year earlier.

  1. Capital allocation discipline is intact through the Abel era. Any acquisition above $20 billion at a price exceeding 1.5 times the book value of the target, or that subsequently requires a goodwill impairment exceeding $5 billion, would signal that the culture of capital discipline had broken.

Latest reading: The quarterly filing is silent on any new large acquisition. The $4.5 billion buyback at approximately $490–500 per share is the most recent capital allocation decision on record, and it signals that Abel and Buffett regard current prices as below intrinsic value — the opposite of capital misallocation. No material goodwill impairments disclosed.

  1. No structural culture change — no regular dividend, no break-up, no activist capitulation. The decentralised owner-operator model must survive the succession intact. A dividend, break-up, or forced restructuring would signal that the model had been abandoned.

Latest reading: Nothing in the quarterly filing suggests any of the three triggers. Normal quarterly cadence, no succession commentary, no dividend proposal.

  1. The ratio of share price to book value per share (price-to-book) stays below 2.0 times. Above that threshold, the valuation fully prices in optimistic compounding and the opportunity cost versus a passive index becomes clearly negative.

Latest reading: The price-to-book ratio using FY2025 equity and the current share price of $497 is approximately 1.495 times — within the hold band (buy edge 1.25 times, trim edge 1.75 times, exit condition 2.0 times) and slightly above the fair-value anchor of 1.45 times. No exit condition is close to being triggered.

What's Wobbling

The single wobble that has moved the thesis from stable to wobbling is GEICO's underwriting performance. The combined ratio — the share of every premium dollar paid out in claims and expenses — deteriorated to 89.3% in the first half of 2026 from 81.7% in the same period a year earlier, a 7.6 percentage-point swing. Underwriting profit fell 40% to $2.4 billion. Both the frequency of claims (bodily injury up five to seven per cent) and their severity (bodily injury claims up ten to twelve per cent) rose. Advertising spend jumped sharply, suggesting GEICO is spending to defend and recover market share against competitors — primarily Progressive — that use telematics-based pricing to identify lower-risk drivers more precisely.

This does not cross any written exit condition: underwriting is still profitable, the float is growing, and the aggregate insurance result for the group actually improved year-on-year because the other insurance segments recovered strongly. But the deterioration now spans two consecutive quarters, and there is no disclosed evidence that GEICO's market share is recovering or that its competitive pricing disadvantage against Progressive's technology platform is narrowing. The wobble to watch in the Case for the Bet is condition one — specifically, whether GEICO's sustained drag begins to weigh on overall book-value compounding relative to the market benchmark.

A secondary, less acute wobble is BHE's regulatory environment. The Oregon Court of Appeals reversed a major wildfire verdict against BHE's electricity subsidiary, PacifiCorp, in April 2026 — a materially positive development that reduced the estimated wildfire liability from $1.2 billion to $572 million. But the Oregon Supreme Court granted review of that reversal, with oral argument scheduled for November 2026. The liability could be reinstated. BHE's earnings grew 11.5% in the first half of 2026, so the operational picture is good; the uncertainty is legal, not operational.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • GEICO's competitive disadvantage becomes structural. Progressive's telematics platform gives it the ability to identify and price for low-risk drivers that GEICO cannot match without building comparable capabilities. If GEICO's combined ratio continues to deteriorate toward the 95–100% range across multiple quarters — meaning the business barely breaks even or begins to lose money on underwriting — the free float advantage narrows materially, because float that costs money is no longer free leverage.

  • Greg Abel makes a large acquisition at too high a price. With $52.6 billion of cash on the balance sheet at year-end 2025, Abel has the firepower for a transformative deal. A transaction above $20 billion, at a price exceeding 1.5 times the target's book value, that subsequently requires a goodwill impairment above $5 billion would signal that the capital-allocation culture had changed — and that is a written exit condition.

  • The BHE wildfire liability revives. The Oregon Supreme Court is reviewing the appellate reversal of the Oregon wildfire verdict against PacifiCorp. New wildfire seasons add incremental liability. If BHE's total wildfire exposure returns toward $1 billion or beyond, the energy subsidiary's earnings would be meaningfully impaired and its capital position would require attention.

  • The equity portfolio drawdown. Berkshire holds roughly $400 billion in publicly traded shares, with a significant concentration in a single technology company. A 25% or greater drawdown in that portfolio would reduce book value by $50–100 billion — not an existential threat to a company with $333 billion in statutory surplus, but a material drag on the compounding rate that the thesis depends on.

  • BHE's energy-transition economics weaken. A 2025 law phases out wind and solar production tax credits for new projects placed in service after the end of 2027. BHE has invested $38 billion in renewable energy partly on the economics of those credits. The estimated impact is a 10 to 15 per cent reduction in BHE's long-run earnings trajectory relative to pre-law assumptions — roughly $400 to $600 million a year foregone.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
