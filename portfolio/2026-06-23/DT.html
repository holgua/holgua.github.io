About Dynatrace

Dynatrace makes the software that large enterprises use to monitor and understand what is happening inside their own technology stacks — when a payment system slows down at 3 a.m., when a cloud service is about to run out of capacity, when an artificial-intelligence model starts producing wrong answers. The company operates as a subscription service: customers pay an annual fee to run Dynatrace across their infrastructure, and once the software is embedded, replacing it requires years of re-instrumentation work across the entire enterprise. That is the core of the business proposition.

Revenue comes overwhelmingly from subscriptions — $1.93 billion of the $2.02 billion reported in the fiscal year ended March 2026, up 19% from the year before. The business has grown at roughly 20% a year for five consecutive fiscal years while consistently converting about a quarter of every revenue dollar into free cash flow (the money left over after running the business and investing in its infrastructure). Free cash flow reached $530 million in the most recent fiscal year — a compound growth rate of roughly 22% a year over four years — confirming that cash generation is growing faster than the business itself. The company carries $1.1 billion in net cash and no meaningful debt.

The company competes in what the industry calls observability — a category that includes monitoring application performance, infrastructure health, log management, and, increasingly, artificial-intelligence workloads. Its primary competitor by annual recurring revenue is Datadog; Cisco (which acquired Splunk in 2024) and Elastic represent the next tier. In 2026, Palo Alto Networks acquired Chronosphere, a smaller monitoring company, adding a new entrant to the bundled-competition threat named in the written exit conditions.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that Dynatrace's switching-cost moat is durable enough to sustain above-market growth and cash-flow margins for long enough that the current share price proves to have been far too cheap. At $41, the business is priced as though it will grow its cash generation at roughly 7–8% a year in perpetuity — a figure that is below even the most pessimistic revenue-growth projection for the company. If the business keeps doing what it has done for the past five years, the gap between price and estimated value closes over time.

The bet depends on five conditions holding:

  1. Net customer retention stays at or above 107%. Net revenue retention — the measure of how much existing customers spend in the current year relative to the prior year, expressed as a percentage — is the single most diagnostic number for a subscription software company. At 110%, existing customers collectively grew their spending by 10% without any new customers being added. The written threshold for concern is 107%; the exit condition requires two consecutive quarters below that line.

  2. Annual recurring revenue grows at 13% or faster in constant currency. Constant-currency growth strips out the effect of exchange-rate movements and measures the underlying expansion of the business. The exit condition fires if growth falls below 13% for two consecutive quarters.

  3. Free cash flow margin stays above 20%. The cash margin — the fraction of revenue that converts to free cash flow — must not fall below 20% for a full fiscal year. This condition guards against the business investing its way into unprofitability or losing pricing leverage to competitors.

  4. No large open-market selling by the chief executive or chief financial officer. If either executes open-market equity sales worth $5 million or more within any 90-day window, that is treated as a meaningful signal that management has lost confidence in the company's prospects. Routine tax-withholding transactions on restricted-stock awards are excluded from this condition.

  5. No confirmed customer displacement by a bundled competitor. Cisco's Splunk integration and Palo Alto's Chronosphere acquisition represent the primary bundled-suite threat: a scenario where a large rival offers observability as part of a broader security or networking deal, convincing enterprise customers to walk away from Dynatrace. This condition requires confirmed named customer wins by those rivals — not merely an acquisition — to fire.

The Rules

The five branches of the written plan translate directly into action-triggering conditions:

  • If the thesis is confirmed (all drivers working, no exit conditions triggered, price in the hold band): maintain the full position and continue monitoring quarterly figures. Consider adding modestly on dips below $38 while the retention rate holds.
  • If the thesis is weakening (retention declining toward 107%, or constant-currency growth decelerating toward 14%, but no exit condition yet confirmed): reduce to half the position within 30 days of the trigger, then wait two additional quarters before deciding whether to exit fully or rebuild.
  • If the thesis is broken (any exit condition confirmed): exit fully within one quarter. Do not average down.
  • If the price falls below the buy edge ($31): the cash-flow yield exceeds 5.9%, which the pre-committed plan treats as deeply undervalued relative to the base-case estimate of intrinsic value. Accumulate toward a full position.
  • If the price rises above the trim edge ($55): the cash-flow yield falls below 3.3%, approaching the base-case discounted-cash-flow estimate. Trim to a half-to-two-thirds position; do not exit fully unless the thesis is simultaneously breaking.

The Conditions Today

Condition 1 — Net customer retention at or above 107%. The annual filing confirmed dollar-based net retention of 110% for the fiscal year ended March 2026, measured on a constant-currency basis. This is unchanged from the prior year (also 110%) and above the year before that (111%). Three consecutive years at or above 110% represents the strongest streak since the company listed. The 107% watch threshold was not approached.

Condition 2 — Annual recurring revenue growth at 13% or faster. Annual recurring revenue reached $2.054 billion at 31 March 2026, an 18% increase from $1.734 billion a year earlier. The annual filing does not separately disclose a constant-currency figure for this metric — that presentation applies only to the retention figure — but 18% reported growth leaves a wide margin above the 13% constant-currency exit condition even after any reasonable currency adjustment.

Condition 3 — Free cash flow margin above 20%. Free cash flow was $529 million in fiscal 2026, representing a 26.2% margin on $2.018 billion of revenue, and up 23% from $431 million in fiscal 2025. The 20% floor was not tested. The operating model is generating cash at a faster rate than revenue is growing, confirming the leverage that the base case projects.

Condition 4 — No large insider selling. All Form 4 filings from the past 12 months are routine tax-withholding transactions on restricted-stock awards — the equivalent of selling just enough shares to cover the tax bill when vested equity becomes taxable income. No open-market sales by the chief executive or chief financial officer appear. The company separately repurchased $479 million of its own shares in fiscal 2026 and authorised a further $1 billion programme, with $849 million remaining as of 31 March 2026. Management conviction is, by this measure, unambiguous.

Condition 5 — No confirmed customer displacement. The annual filing discloses that Palo Alto Networks acquired Chronosphere in 2026, directly naming one of the bundled-suite competitors cited in the written exit conditions. The relevant condition requires confirmed named Dynatrace enterprise customer wins by the acquirer, not merely the acquisition itself. No displaced customers appear in the filing. The 110% net retention is the most direct counter-signal: if customers were actively defecting to bundled alternatives, that figure would be falling. It is not.

A sixth watch item — the proportion of annual recurring revenue on the Dynatrace Platform Subscription licensing model — was not updated in the annual filing. Quarterly earnings commentary had placed this figure at roughly 70% as of the quarter ending December 2025; the filing describes the model extensively but provides no updated percentage. The exit condition requires the metric to stall below 72% for three consecutive quarters and for management to stop disclosing it — neither confirmed, since the annual filing simply does not carry the number. The next quarterly report is the first opportunity to observe whether the disclosure continues.

The Share Price

At $41.42, Dynatrace's cash-flow yield sits at 4.39% — calculated as the $530 million the business generated in free cash flow last fiscal year divided by the roughly $12.1 billion market value of the company. The written fair-value anchor for this metric is 4.4%, meaning today's price lands almost exactly at fair value. The share price is not in the bargain range.

The buy edge is at $31, which implies a cash-flow yield of 5.9% — a level at which the implied long-run return rises to around 22% a year. The trim edge is at $55, where the yield falls to 3.3% and the business begins to price in much of its base-case potential. At $101, the exit edge, the market would be pricing in roughly six years of compounding at the base growth rate all at once — a return so fully anticipated that reallocation elsewhere makes mathematical sense.

The share has traded between $31.64 and $57.55 over the past 52 weeks, spending most of the recent period between $36 and $43. The current price is near the midpoint of that range.

What's Wobbling

The Palo Alto / Chronosphere acquisition. The annual filing explicitly confirms that Palo Alto Networks acquired Chronosphere in 2026 — one of the named bundled competitors in the written exit conditions. The relevant condition requires more than an acquisition; it requires confirmed named Dynatrace customer wins by the combined entity. That confirmation has not appeared. The 110% net retention is the most credible evidence that displacement is not yet occurring at scale. The condition is in an armed but unfired state, and the next quarterly earnings release is the logical checkpoint for any go-to-market announcements.

Subscription gross-margin compression. The cost to run the platform as a cloud-hosted service grew faster than revenue in the most recent fiscal year, compressing the subscription gross margin by one percentage point, from 86% to 85%. Total gross margin — which includes other revenue streams — expanded from 81% to 82%. None of the seven written exit conditions references gross margin directly; exit condition 3 references the cash-flow margin, which is 26.2% and comfortably above the 20% floor. The compression is a watch item in the sense that sustained increases in hosting costs could eventually reach the cash-flow margin, but there is no evidence of that today.

The platform subscription disclosure gap. The proportion of annual recurring revenue on the Dynatrace Platform Subscription model was last disclosed at 70% in quarterly earnings commentary from December 2025. The annual filing does not update this figure. The exit condition watches for the metric to stall below 72% for three consecutive quarters and for management to stop disclosing it — neither of which has been confirmed, since the annual filing simply does not carry the number. The next quarterly report is the first opportunity to observe whether the disclosure continues.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Observability commoditises. If Datadog's artificial-intelligence monitoring tools, cloud-native open-source frameworks, or general-purpose language-model agents prove good enough for enterprise monitoring at materially lower cost, the switching-cost moat erodes. Net retention would fall first; the exit conditions are calibrated to catch this early.
  • Bundled displacement at scale. Cisco's integration of Splunk (acquired in 2024) and Palo Alto's acquisition of Chronosphere create two large rivals with the financial resources to offer observability as part of a broader security or networking deal. If enterprise procurement teams consolidate contracts, Dynatrace loses deals not because its product is inferior but because it is not part of the winning bundle.
  • Cloud costs outrun revenue growth. Hosting a large-scale artificial-intelligence platform in the public cloud is expensive, and that cost grows with customer usage. The one-percentage-point subscription gross-margin compression in fiscal 2026 is attributed to exactly this dynamic. If that trend steepens, cash-flow margins follow down.
  • Tax and regulatory complications. Dynatrace recognised a $321 million deferred tax asset related to an intellectual-property transfer. The estimation involves significant uncertainty — a revision could produce a material charge or credit in a future quarter, distorting reported results without reflecting any change in the underlying business.
  • Customer concentration. One channel partner accounts for approximately 10% of revenue. The loss of that relationship, for any reason, would produce an immediate top-line shock without any corresponding deterioration in the underlying competitive position.
