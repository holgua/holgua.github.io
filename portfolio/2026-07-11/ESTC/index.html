Elastic: The Bet Still Works

The Bottom Line

Data as of 2026-07-11 · Latest filing 2026-06-08.

The bet is working. Every one of the seven written exit conditions came through the latest annual filing without a single trip wire, and the most closely watched metric — the rate at which existing customers expand their spending — stayed flat at a level comfortably inside the safety zone. The one thing worth watching is the competitive gap with Datadog, which does not appear in the filing itself and requires external tracking to close. Recent Development

The annual report for the year ending 2026-04-30 confirmed that all seven written exit conditions remain intact. The most load-bearing confirmation was the customer expansion rate: every hundred dollars customers spent on Elastic twelve months earlier had grown to a hundred and twelve dollars by April 2026 — the same reading as the prior year, and well above the hundred and five threshold at which the bet would begin to unravel. Elastic Cloud revenue, the cloud-hosted version of the software, grew twenty-two per cent over the year to $837 million, and the higher-value annual subscriptions within that segment accelerated to twenty-eight per cent growth — both far above the ten per cent floor that would signal platform commoditisation.

The path toward accounting profitability also continued at a measured pace. The operating loss narrowed from $55 million to $34 million — a thirty-nine per cent improvement — keeping the 2028 target of reaching breakeven on a full accounting basis on track. Cash generation, which is the more relevant measure for this type of capital-light software business, remained robust: $322 million of cash was converted from operations after minimal capital spending, putting the cash margin at 18.5 per cent of revenue. Stock-based compensation — shares granted to employees in lieu of cash, which dilutes outside shareholders — edged down to 17.2 per cent of revenue on a pure-compensation basis, below the eighteen per cent level the thesis treats as a ceiling.

One topic the filing cannot address is the competitive position of Datadog, the company's closest rival in the software monitoring segment. The filing acknowledges Datadog as a competitor but provides no comparative revenue numbers. That data must come from Datadog's own quarterly filings, and it matters because the seventh exit condition fires if Datadog's revenue growth exceeds Elastic's by more than fifteen percentage points for two consecutive years and Elastic simultaneously loses its top-tier standing in the annual Gartner ranking. That comparison remains open as of this filing. For the current share-price reading, see the Share Price.

About Elastic

Elastic makes software that helps large organisations search, monitor, and secure the data flowing through their systems. The central product is Elasticsearch — a search engine that can index and query enormous volumes of text and structured data at high speed. Over time, Elastic has extended that core into three connected applications: one for enterprise search (finding documents, products, or code across internal databases), one for observability (monitoring whether software systems are working correctly, by tracking logs, performance metrics, and traces from those systems), and one for security monitoring (detecting threats and suspicious behaviour by analysing the same data streams). All three applications run on the same underlying data layer.

Revenue comes primarily from subscriptions. Customers can run the software on their own servers (self-managed) or use Elastic's hosted cloud service, called Elastic Cloud, which removes the infrastructure overhead. The cloud version is growing faster — it accounted for forty-eight per cent of total revenue in the year ending 2026-04-30, up from forty-six per cent the year before, and the annual-subscription portion within that segment grew twenty-eight per cent. Revenue in total reached $1.74 billion in that fiscal year. The business does not yet earn a profit on a full accounting basis, but it generates significant cash; the gap between the two measures is almost entirely the cost of issuing shares to employees rather than paying them in cash.

The Case for the Bet

The central argument is that Elastic's three-use-case platform, built on one shared data layer, creates production-embedded pipelines that are expensive to remove — a form of stickiness that should allow the business to keep raising prices as it matures and to defend its renewal base even as open-source alternatives improve. The conditions below must keep holding for that argument to hold.

  1. Customer expansion rate stays above 105% for any rolling two-quarter period. This ratio — how much existing customers spend this year versus last year, as a percentage — is the sharpest leading indicator of whether the switching-cost argument is real. Below 105%, the business is contracting among its existing base, which would mean customers are either leaving or trimming usage, not expanding it. Two consecutive quarters below the threshold would be the first formal exit signal.

Latest reading: 112% as of 2026-04-30. This is the same level as the prior year, meaning the expansion rate neither accelerated nor deteriorated — it held steady well inside the safe zone. The number of customers spending more than $100,000 a year grew from approximately 1,510 to 1,720.

  1. Elastic Cloud revenue grows at least 10% in any full fiscal year. If the cloud-hosted product fails to grow at that pace over a full year, it would be a signal that AWS's open-source Elasticsearch fork is displacing the paid service in the segment where Elastic is most exposed to competition.

Latest reading: Elastic Cloud grew 22% in the year ending 2026-04-30, reaching $837 million. The annual-subscription portion, which carries higher renewal predictability, grew 28%. Both figures are well above the floor.

  1. Cash margin stays above 14% on a full-year basis. Cash generated from operations minus capital spending, divided by revenue — this is the measure of how capital-light the business actually is. Below 14%, the capital-light characterisation begins to break down.

Latest reading: 18.5% for the year ending 2026-04-30. Cash from operations was $327 million, capital spending $5 million, on $1.74 billion of revenue. The margin sits 450 basis points above the floor.

  1. Stock-based compensation stays below 18% of revenue for any two consecutive fiscal years. Issuing shares to employees dilutes outside shareholders; above 18% for two consecutive years, the compensation structure begins to conflict with the capital-light characterisation of the business.

Latest reading: 17.2% on a pure-compensation basis for the year ending 2026-04-30, down from 17.4% the prior year. Two consecutive years below 18% — this condition is not at risk.

  1. Any acquisition stays below $500 million in size, or comes with a disclosed path to cash accretion within three years. Large acquisitions at high revenue multiples without a credible accretion case are treated as a capital-discipline failure.

Latest reading: Two small purchases in the year ending 2026-04-30 — an artificial-intelligence search company called Jina AI for $43 million in total, and a data-processing company called Keep for $11 million. Both well below the $500 million threshold.

  1. GAAP operating margin turns positive by the fiscal year ending April 2028. This is the accounting profitability target that validates the trajectory from loss-making to self-funding on a full accounting basis, not just on a cash basis.

Latest reading: Operating loss of $34 million in the year ending 2026-04-30, equal to −1.9% of revenue. That is an improvement from −3.7% the prior year. The trajectory is on track for the 2028 target if the pace of improvement continues.

  1. Datadog's revenue growth does not exceed Elastic's by more than 15 percentage points for two consecutive fiscal years, while Elastic simultaneously holds a top-tier position in the Gartner annual observability ranking. This is the competitive-displacement condition — it fires only if both legs are true simultaneously.

Latest reading: Elastic's annual report names Datadog as a competitor but provides no comparative growth data. The Gartner ranking is published separately each year. This condition requires external data sources and cannot be assessed from the filing alone.

The Share Price

The current share price of $59 sits in the lower half of the band between the cheap edge and the trim edge. On the yield-based measure used to set those edges — annual cash generation divided by market value — the business is generating a 5.2% cash yield at today's price. The fair-value anchor for this bet sits at a 3.0% yield, which implies a share price of about $101. The gap between the two — roughly −41% — is what earns the "deeply below fair value" description.

Put simply: at $59, the business is priced as though its cash generation will grow at around 6% a year indefinitely. The bet is that 15% annual growth is more likely, which would put the business worth roughly $101 a share on a discounted-cash-flow basis and considerably more if the bull case plays out.

Why the Market Sees It Differently

The share price implies that the business will grow its earnings at roughly 6% a year — not 15%. The gap between these two views is about 9 percentage points, and it is wide enough to be called extreme rather than merely material.

Three stories could explain what the market is pricing. The first is that demand for Elastic's products will stall. The filing offers strong evidence against this: cloud revenue grew 22%, the customer expansion rate held at 112%, and the forward contract backlog grew about 20%. If demand were stalling, at least one of these would be showing early signs. None are.

The second story is that margins will compress, undoing the current cash generation. Again, the filing runs the other way: the cash margin expanded to 18.5% and the accounting loss narrowed by 39%. Nothing in the numbers supports a margin-compression story.

The third story — and this is the one that has some support — is that the share buyback programme has been poorly timed. Management spent $340 million repurchasing shares at an average price of roughly $77, which is above both today's price and the fair-value estimate of $101 produced by the discounted-cash-flow model. That is a real capital-allocation blemish. It is not an exit condition, and it does not change the underlying business outlook, but it does mean a meaningful amount of cash that could have compounded at the business level was deployed above intrinsic value.

For the market to prove right about 6% long-run growth, one of the two demand hypotheses — stalling cloud adoption or margin erosion — would need to materialise in a future filing. For the bet to prove right, the filing record needs to keep showing what the latest one showed: expanding cloud adoption, stable customer behaviour, and improving margins.

The Standing Orders

These orders translate the price-band edges into two standing orders and two price alerts. Each is the operationalisation of a pre-committed rule — not a new judgement made here. For the full conditions, see the Rules.

Price alerts (informational).
- A first alert at $101, the fair-value anchor. Crossing this level means the share price has returned to a neutral reading; no action follows automatically, but a fresh read of the conditions is warranted to decide whether to begin trimming or simply hold.
- A second alert at $56, the cheap edge. Crossing this level downward arms the buy order below; before adding, the latest re-validation should confirm that no exit condition has been triggered.

Standing orders (actionable).
- A standing buy order at $56. Tranches are added once the quoted price closes at or below this level and no exit condition has fired. The bear-case scenario implies a -49% permanent loss from current levels at 25% probability, which is destructive enough to require staged entry — not more than half the target position in a single tranche.
- A standing trim order at $140. Approximately a quarter of the position is trimmed once the quoted price closes at or above this level, to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding in place for continued compounding if the base case plays out.

No exit tier has been set for this holding beyond the trim order. A separate valuation floor of roughly $200 — where the cash yield would drop to 1.5% — is noted in the thesis as the point at which the asymmetry inverts, but this is not a formal rule in the current structure.

What's Wobbling

Two flagged inconsistencies in the valuation model are worth naming plainly. The first is that the fair-value anchor used in the Standing Orders — $101 per share, derived from a discounted-cash-flow model at a 15% growth rate — differs substantially from the fair-value range produced by a separate model that compares Elastic's valuation multiples to those of its closest peers. The peer-multiple model produces a base-case midpoint of around $241 per share. The two models use different methodologies and are not meant to agree; the thesis deliberately anchors on the more conservative discounted-cash-flow number. But the gap is large enough that anyone relying on the peer-multiple figure would reach a very different conclusion about the margin of safety at $59.

The second flag is that the current cash yield of 5.2% diverges materially from the 3.0% fair-value yield used to set the band edges. That divergence exists precisely because the market is pricing a much lower growth rate than the thesis assumes. It is a real signal — it says either the market is wrong or the growth assumption needs revisiting. Before adding materially to a position, a sober assessment of whether 15% annual cash growth for five years remains defensible is warranted.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • The bear case, at 25% probability, implies the business is worth roughly $29 per share — a permanent loss of about 51% from today's price. This is not a tail risk; it is an assigned probability with a credible mechanism: AWS's open-source fork displaces the self-managed renewal base and enterprise artificial-intelligence spending consolidates on hyperscaler tools that do not benefit Elastic.
  • Datadog could widen its revenue-growth lead over Elastic beyond fifteen percentage points. If that gap persists for two consecutive fiscal years and Elastic simultaneously loses its top-tier position in the Gartner observability ranking, the seventh exit condition fires — a full exit within thirty days is required under the Rules.
  • Stock-based compensation, while declining, remains the single largest reconciling item between cash generation and accounting losses. If headcount pressure — from competition for engineering talent or from acquisition — pushes the rate back above 18% for two consecutive years, the fourth exit condition fires.
  • The buyback programme has already deployed $340 million above fair value. A continuation of buybacks at above-fair-value prices, or a large acquisition priced above eight times revenue without a disclosed accretion case, would trigger the fifth exit condition.
  • The FedRAMP High authorisation, which opens the federal government market for Elastic's security-monitoring product, is an opportunity but not yet revenue. If the expected federal ramp via the CISA programme and GSA channels does not materialise within two to three fiscal years, the bull-case scenario loses its primary accelerant.

Gaps in the Picture

The risk-free rate used to set the hurdle in the score section was read from a related holding's market-data file rather than from Elastic's own file, which does not carry the field. The number used — approximately 4.4% — is the ten-year government yield as of 2026-07-11 and is directionally correct, but a dedicated field for this holding would remove the dependency.

The competitive-position driver — exit condition number seven — cannot be assessed from Elastic's own filings and requires external tracking of Datadog's quarterly revenue reports and the annual Gartner observability ranking. As of this cycle, no data from those external sources has been evaluated; the driver is marked as producing no signal in the current filing, not as confirmed safe.

Two fair-value anchors coexist: the analysis-derived $101 (cash-yield method) and the peer/self-multiple model's $208 or $241 central estimates. A future schema migration will resolve the divergence. For the purposes of the Standing Orders and the price alerts, the more conservative $101 anchor governs.

The Rules

The pre-committed rules, stated as conditions:

  • If the bet is intact and the share price is within the hold band: hold the position. Monitor the customer expansion rate and cloud revenue growth quarterly; confirm the stock-compensation trajectory at each annual filing. Do not add at current prices.
  • If the bet weakens — the customer expansion rate drifts toward 107% or cloud growth decelerates below 15% for two consecutive quarters — do not add. Require two consecutive quarters of deterioration before considering a reduction; one quarter is insufficient to confirm a structural break.
  • If any exit condition fires: close the full position within thirty days. No averaging down after a thesis break.
  • If the share price falls below the buy edge (cash yield crosses above 5.5%, implying roughly $56 per share): confirm no exit condition has fired, then add in tranches. No more than half the target size in a single tranche — the bear-case loss scenario is large enough to require staged entry.
  • If the share price rises above the trim edge (cash yield falls below 2.2%, implying roughly $140 per share): trim approximately a quarter of the position and keep the core holding.
  • If the share price rises above approximately $200 (cash yield falls below 1.5%): close the full remaining position. At that price, the market is pricing the bull-case scenario in full, and the asymmetry has inverted.
