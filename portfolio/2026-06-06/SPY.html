SP500: The Bet Is Wobbling

The Bottom Line

The bet on the SPDR S&P 500 ETF Trust — that the basket of 500 large American companies will, over a long enough holding period, pay a reasonable premium above risk-free Treasury bonds — has just hit the moment the case explicitly anticipated. The basket's earnings yield (the inverse of its price-to-earnings ratio) has fallen to 3.5 cents per dollar invested, which is roughly one percentage point less than what 10-year United States Treasury bonds pay for no risk. The structural quality of the basket remains intact, the fund's mechanics are clean, and no single written exit condition is fully crossed. But the rules call for harvesting at this valuation — trimming 25 to 33 percent of the position to reduce exposure while the premium evaporates.

The Verdict

The latest reading puts the basket clearly past the expensive line. That translates differently depending on the price paid for the units, with one cost-basis cohort that diverges from the others.

  • As a fresh investment at today's price (around $754 per unit). Wait. A fresh buyer is, in effect, being paid less than government bonds to take stock-market risk. The rules call for setting a price alert near $426 — the level that would mark the units clearly cheap — and waiting.
  • As an existing investment bought below $700 per unit — currently sitting on a gain of about 8 percent or more. Trim 25 to 33 percent of the position. This is the textbook setup for harvesting: the rules say the basket is meaningfully expensive against risk-free, and a big-gain holding has the slack to release some of the position back toward the size target.
  • As an existing investment bought roughly between $700 and $800 per unit — currently anywhere between a small loss of 6 percent and a small gain of 8 percent. Trim 25 to 33 percent. Today's price sits in this cohort; the yield is below the expensive line and the bond-vs-stock comparison is negative, which means harvesting is the call regardless of whether the position is currently up or down a few percent.
  • As an existing investment bought between $800 and $900 per unit — currently showing a loss of roughly 6 to 16 percent. Trim 25 to 33 percent. A mild-loss position past the expensive line still calls for harvesting; the action shrinks exposure without locking in a deep loss.
  • As an existing investment bought above $900 per unit — currently showing a loss of about 16 percent or more. Hold. A deep-loss cost basis paired with a wobbling-but-not-broken case is the spot where harvesting is most expensive. Capitulating here would crystallise a structural loss while no specific written line has been crossed. Wait for either an exit condition to fire or the basket to recover.

About the Fund

This is the SPDR S&P 500 ETF Trust (ticker SPY), the largest and oldest exchange-traded fund in the world. It was launched by State Street Investment Management in 1993 and tracks the S&P 500 index — a basket of 500 of the largest publicly traded American companies, weighted by company size. About $735 billion sits in the fund today.

The plain-English consequence of size-weighting is that the largest companies dominate. NVIDIA, Microsoft, Apple, Amazon, and a handful of others make up roughly a quarter of the basket between them. Owning one unit of the fund means owning a slice of every company in the S&P 500, but with the biggest weight in the very largest names.

The fund charges 0.0945 percent per year — about one cent for every $10 invested annually. That fee is one of the lowest in the industry and is part of the case for using the fund as a passive American-large-cap core holding.

The fund pays out the dividends that the underlying companies generate, currently about 1.1 percent of the basket's value each year.

The Case for the Bet

The bet on this fund is not that the S&P 500 is always a buy — Warren Buffett's Berkshire sits on over $370 billion in cash today, an explicit statement that even history's most successful equity investor does not always find equities priced to deliver. The case is more disciplined: over a long enough holding period, the basket's earnings yield (its earnings divided by its price) should exceed the risk-free rate by at least 2 percentage points. That cushion — the equity risk premium — is what pays for the volatility, the drawdowns, and the years in which equities lag bonds.

For the bet to keep working, the following conditions must keep holding:

  1. The basket pays a real premium over risk-free. The basket's earnings yield should not fall below 3 cents per dollar invested AND simultaneously sit below the 10-year Treasury yield for two consecutive quarterly snapshots. Either condition alone is a warning; both together is the structural fail.
  2. The annual fee stays low. State Street should not raise the expense ratio above 0.15 percent, and not without a competitive fee response from cheaper alternatives.
  3. The fund stays large enough to survive. Assets under management should not fall below $100 billion — a level that would indicate an extraordinary liquidity event in the world's most liquid equity vehicle.
  4. No single name dominates. The largest holding should not exceed 13 percent of the basket. The S&P 500 has historically allowed concentration to drift up to roughly 7 to 8 percent at the top; 13 percent would mean a regime change, not normal drift.
  5. The index methodology stays consistent. S&P Dow Jones Indices should not materially restructure the S&P 500 in a way that undermines its broad-American-large-cap mandate.
  6. The visible top of the basket keeps quality. The median return on equity (a measure of how much profit each dollar of company net worth generates) across the deduped top-10 should not collapse below 15 percent. Below that line, even the visible top has lost its quality.

The Rules

The rules that govern what action follows from each combination of conditions and basket price are fixed:

  • If all conditions are intact. Hold. The basket is reasonably priced and the fund's mechanics are sound.
  • If any one condition is wobbling but none has clearly broken. Do not add. Re-check after the next quarter. If the bond-vs-stock comparison stays negative for a second snapshot, or the top-10 quality keeps falling, swap to a cheaper rival fund (such as IVV or VOO) or move to cash according to the position-sizing rule.
  • If any one condition has clearly broken. Sell. The fund's mechanics have failed or an exit condition has fired. Move proceeds to risk-free assets or to a holding that meets the value test.
  • If the basket price has dropped into the bargain range. Add to the position. The basket is meaningfully cheap against risk-free.
  • If the basket price has run into the expensive range. Trim 25 to 33 percent of the position to harvest the premium. The basket is meaningfully expensive against risk-free.

What's Wobbling

The valuation. Specifically, the relationship between what the basket pays in earnings and what risk-free Treasuries pay in interest.

The basket's earnings yield of 3.53 cents per dollar would, in isolation, be a stretched but defensible level for a high-quality equity index. The problem is the comparison: 10-year United States Treasury bonds pay 4.49 percent. A passive equity bet should reasonably pay at least two percentage points above the risk-free rate to compensate for the risk of equities. Right now the comparison is roughly 100 basis points the wrong way — equities pay less than bonds.

This is the precise regime the case explicitly anticipated. The reference to Buffett's $370 billion cash pile in the thesis was the analogy: even history's most successful equity investor backs away from equities at these readings. The rules say the right answer is not to exit (the basket's structural quality is intact, the fund's mechanics are clean) but to harvest — to take 25 to 33 percent of the position off the table.

The condition that would actually break the bet — earnings yield below 3 percent AND a negative bond-versus-stock cushion across two consecutive snapshots — has only one of its two legs in place. One more snapshot is needed before the case is structurally broken.

Two minor caveats are worth filing. The basket's weighted-mean return on equity has drifted unusually high (66 percent) because of buyback-driven equity shrinkage at Apple and NVIDIA. The median (33 percent) is healthy. And the fund mechanics — fee, assets, concentration — are all comfortably inside their ceilings. The wobble is the price, not the product.

What Could Break the Fund

The case for this fund could break in a small number of structurally distinct ways:

  • The basket becomes decisively expensive. A move to an earnings yield below 3 percent at a moment when Treasuries still yield more — and the combination sustained across two consecutive quarterly snapshots — would be the full structural fail. That is one snapshot away from the current reading.
  • State Street raises the fee. A fee hike above 0.15 percent would eliminate the case for using SPY specifically over its cheaper rivals (BlackRock's IVV at 0.03 percent, Vanguard's VOO at 0.03 percent). The mechanical response is to switch funds, not to switch out of the asset class.
  • Single-name concentration crosses 13 percent. If NVIDIA or another mega-cap rallies to roughly twice its current weight, the basket would no longer be the diversified American-large-cap exposure the case relies on.
  • A liquidity event. A sustained drawdown in fund assets below $100 billion would mean a once-in-a-generation event has overtaken the world's most-traded equity vehicle. This is improbable but defines the floor.
  • S&P Dow Jones changes the rules. A material restructuring of the index — for example, removing a category of constituents, changing the weighting rule, or narrowing the universe — would mean the underlying product has changed without the holder choosing.
  • Top-10 quality collapses. If the median return on equity of the basket's largest holdings falls below 15 percent, the visible top has lost its quality. That line is currently well above the trigger.

The most likely break is the first one — the basket simply remains expensive against bonds for another quarter. The mechanical response is not exit but a managed wind-down of exposure, swapping into cash or into a cheaper passive vehicle as the position is trimmed.

Gaps in the Picture

A few things in the underlying figures are worth flagging plainly.

The earnings yield used here comes from the trailing 12-month price-to-earnings ratio published by Yahoo Finance. A forward-earnings basis would put the yield slightly higher (because analysts project earnings to grow), but the rules use the trailing measure deliberately — it is what the basket has actually delivered, not what analysts hope it will.

The median return on equity for the top-10 holdings looks healthy at 33 percent, but the weighted-mean reading at 66 percent reflects buyback distortion at Apple and NVIDIA. Buyback-funded equity shrinkage mathematically inflates the return-on-equity calculation. The median is the cleaner read.

The index methodology condition has no automated signal. Changes to S&P 500 construction rules are announced by S&P Dow Jones Indices and propagate slowly; the case relies on external monitoring between automated runs. The same applies to changes in the State Street trust structure.

The 10-year Treasury yield reference rate is from a live market quote on the snapshot day. It can move 20 to 40 basis points in either direction over a short period; the bond-versus-stock cushion is therefore an estimate, not a frozen reading. Sustained measurement across more than one quarterly snapshot is what the rules use to filter noise from signal.
