PayPal: The Bet Still Works

The Bottom Line

The bet is working: the underlying business is generating over $5 billion a year in cash earnings, buybacks are reducing the share count at a meaningful pace, and none of the written exit conditions has triggered. The share price sits at the buy edge, modestly below fair value — the market has priced in structural deterioration that the filings do not yet confirm. The one condition to watch is branded checkout volume growth, which PayPal has stopped disclosing separately, making it harder to know whether the competitive slide is temporary or self-reinforcing.

About PayPal

PayPal runs a two-sided digital payments network that sits between the merchant who wants to get paid and the consumer who wants to pay. A consumer stores payment credentials with PayPal once; from that point on, they can check out at tens of millions of online and in-person merchants without re-entering card details. The merchant, in turn, accepts PayPal because consumers already trust it. That mutual dependency — merchants need consumer scale, consumers need merchant acceptance — is the core of the business.

The network spans roughly 200 countries and 439 million active accounts as of early 2026, processing approximately $1.79 trillion in total payment volume in the full-year 2025. Revenue comes from a small percentage of each transaction (the "take rate"), which PayPal earns for handling the payment, the fraud check, and in many cases the currency conversion. The more volume flows through the network, the more PayPal earns, even if the take rate is flat or slightly declining.

Three product lines sit under the PayPal umbrella: the branded PayPal checkout button, which is the highest-margin product and the one most exposed to competition; Braintree, an unbranded payment-processing service used by larger merchants who want to handle the consumer relationship themselves; and Venmo, a peer-to-peer payment app popular in the United States that PayPal is gradually monetising through merchant integrations and debit-card features. Buy Now Pay Later products and the PayPal USD stablecoin round out a portfolio of adjacent services.

The business is capital-light: it does not own physical infrastructure, it does not take custody of consumer deposits in the traditional sense, and its largest cost is the transaction expense it pays to card networks and banks for processing the underlying payments. Over the past three years, that cost structure has allowed management to expand operating profit from around $3.8 billion in 2022 to $6.1 billion in 2025, while simultaneously returning roughly $6 billion a year to shareholders through share repurchases.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that PayPal's published share price has overestimated the threat from competitors, and that the company's cash-generation engine is durable enough to compound per-share earnings at a double-digit rate even if top-line growth stays modest. The market appears to be pricing in sustained earnings decline; the most recent annual results do not support that reading.

Seven conditions must keep holding for this bet to remain valid:

  1. Branded checkout volume. The percentage of online checkouts completed through the PayPal button — rather than through Apple Pay, Google Pay, Shop Pay, or a competitor's native flow — must not fall by more than two percentage points in any single fiscal year. That level of loss would signal structural displacement rather than temporary softness.

  2. Total payment volume growth. The network's overall transaction volume must continue growing. A sustained decline in the total volume flowing through PayPal's systems would indicate that the network effect is weakening, not merely that the mix is shifting.

  3. Active account base. The number of accounts actively transacting through PayPal must hold steady or grow. A sustained decline of more than 2% year-on-year would indicate that consumers are moving away from the network.

  4. No binding regulatory action. A consent order or enforcement action by the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau or the Department of Justice that materially restricts a core PayPal product would change the risk profile of the bet. Civil investigative demands and lawsuits do not themselves trigger this condition; only an action that limits what PayPal can offer its customers would.

  5. Consumer credit quality. PayPal's Buy Now Pay Later business carries consumer credit risk. If the annualised charge-off rate on that book rises above 10% without a disclosed recovery path, the credit portfolio becomes a drag that changes the earnings-per-share story.

  6. Buyback discipline. The bet relies on management continuing to retire shares at depressed multiples. If the buyback programme is abandoned, or if the company takes on debt beyond twice its operating earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortisation — without a compelling strategic reason — the per-share compounding thesis breaks.

  7. Operating income floor. Annual operating income must not fall below $5.5 billion on a reported basis, nor must management guide the following year's adjusted operating income below $5 billion. Either signal would indicate that the margin recovery is reversing.

The Rules

The rules that govern the position were written before the latest filing was read. They are reproduced here as written.

If the thesis is confirmed — no exit condition has fired and the evidence is broadly in line with expectations — hold without action and let the per-share compounding continue.

If the thesis is weakening — one or more conditions are showing stress but no exit condition has triggered — do not add. Run a fresh check on the specific deteriorating driver. Two consecutive quarters of confirming deterioration are required before acting; one quarter is not enough.

If the thesis is broken — at least one exit condition has fired — exit the full position within 30 days. No averaging down. Record which condition fired.

If the share price falls below the buy threshold (around $41) — and the thesis is confirmed intact — add in tranches over one to two quarters.

If the share price rises above the trim threshold (around $60) — trim 25–33% of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.

The Conditions Today

Branded checkout volume. This is the condition that matters most and the one with the least transparency. PayPal stopped reporting branded checkout volume separately in its quarterly filings, so there is no direct read from the most recent period. Total payment volume grew 11% year-on-year in the three months ending March 2026 — that is the network-level figure. Revenue from PayPal-branded products grew by roughly $140 million year-on-year in the same period, while Braintree (the lower-margin, unbranded service) grew by roughly $410 million. The mix shift is visible even without the branded-checkout number. A shareholder lawsuit filed in February 2026 alleges that management withheld information about impediments to the branded-checkout strategy. No exit condition has fired, but this driver will determine whether the bet is intact or weakening.

Total payment volume and network scale. Total payment volume grew 11% year-on-year and payment transactions increased 7% to 6.5 billion in the three months to March 2026. The network is still growing. This is the central premise of the thesis and it holds.

Active accounts. Active accounts grew 1% year-on-year to 439 million. That is a plateau rather than growth, but it is not a decline, and no exit condition references account counts. Transactions per active account ticked down slightly to 58.7, suggesting that engagement is not accelerating. Worth watching, but not yet a concern.

Operating margin. Full-year 2025 operating income was $6.1 billion, up 14% from 2024 and comfortably above the $5.5 billion exit floor. The three months to March 2026 showed operating income of $1.49 billion at an 18% margin, down from 20% in the same period of 2025. The decline reflects the faster growth of Braintree relative to the higher-margin branded business. Annualising the quarterly run-rate puts full-year 2026 on track for around $5.95 billion — still above the exit floor. The direction of travel bears watching.

Buyback discipline. PayPal repurchased 34 million shares for $1.5 billion at an average of $44.60 per share in the three months to March 2026. The remaining authorisation stands at $12.4 billion. The programme is running at pace and at distressed multiples. This condition is clearly intact.

Regulatory status. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau has two open civil investigative demands against PayPal. No consent order has been issued. A separate securities class action (the Darcy case) was filed in February 2026 and is at the pleadings stage. The 2025 legislation that removed the Bureau's supervisory jurisdiction over payment apps is a net positive for PayPal. No exit condition has triggered.

Consumer credit quality. The annualised net charge-off rate on consumer credit improved to 3.0% in the three months to March 2026, down from 4.5% in the same period of 2025. The transaction loss rate was stable at 0.06%. Both are well below their respective exit-condition thresholds. This condition is clearly intact.

The Share Price

At $42 per share, PayPal trades just above the level the analysis defines as the buy threshold — the point where the yield on the business's cash earnings, relative to the share price, is high enough to justify adding to the position. That yield currently stands at roughly 12%, against a threshold of 12.5%.

The analysis places fair value at $48 per share — the price at which the cash-earnings yield would be at a neutral reading of 10.5%. At today's price, the shares trade about 13% below that level. The gap is modest, not dramatic: PayPal is not in distressed territory, but it is not fairly priced either. The market has priced in enough pessimism about the branded-checkout story to push the share below the fair-value anchor, but not enough to breach the defined buy threshold.

The trim threshold sits at $60, about 42% above today's share price. The exit level sits at $78. Neither is near.

A separate scenario analysis using historical earnings multiples and projected earnings across three outcomes (detailed in the Gaps section) produces a base-case estimate of $142 per share — roughly three times the yield-based fair value. The two approaches measure different things, and the yield-based method takes precedence for the verdict; the discrepancy is flagged in Gaps in the Picture.

The Standing Orders

The rules translate the share-price bands into three standing orders and two price alerts. Each is the operationalisation of a pre-committed rule — not a new judgement made here.

Price alerts (informational).
- A first alert at $48, the fair-value anchor. The crossing means the share price has returned to a neutral reading; no action follows, but a fresh read of the conditions is warranted.
- A second alert at $41, the buy threshold. The crossing arms the standing buy order; before adding, the latest re-validation should confirm the conditions are still working.

Standing orders (actionable).
- A standing buy order at $41. The rules call for adding in tranches once the share price closes at or below this line and the conditions are still working. The order may be staged across one or two quarters rather than filled in a single transaction.
- A standing trim order at $60. The rules call for trimming 25–33% of the position when the share price closes at or above this line, to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.
- A standing exit order at $78. The rules call for closing the full position when the share price closes at or above this line. At that level, the market would be pricing in roughly 11 years of base-case compounding instantly; the implied forward return falls below the long-term equity premium, and reallocation is the better option. No re-entry within 90 days.

The three order levels do not contradict the cost-basis verdicts above: those verdicts describe the current reading; the standing orders describe what the rules will execute if the share price moves to each line.

What's Wobbling

Two conditions are generating noise without triggering any exit rule.

Branded checkout disclosure gap. The most important condition in the bet is also the one the company has stopped reporting directly. Without a branded-checkout-specific volume figure, it is not possible to say definitively whether the share of PayPal-button checkouts is stable, recovering, or declining further. The proxy — revenue from PayPal-branded products versus Braintree — suggests continued mix shift toward the lower-margin service. The Darcy class action alleges that management had information about impediments to the branded-checkout strategy that it did not disclose publicly. This is a risk signal, not confirmed evidence of an exit-condition trigger. Two consecutive quarters of explicit confirmation that the brand is losing share by more than two percentage points in a fiscal year would be required before the exit condition could fire. The Q2 2026 earnings call is the next data point.

Operating margin compression. The quarterly margin fell two percentage points year-on-year to 18%, driven by the faster growth of Braintree. The annual floor in the exit conditions is $5.5 billion; the quarterly run-rate implies roughly $5.95 billion for the year, which clears that floor. But the direction — margins declining as the lower-margin product grows faster — is the structural risk the thesis acknowledged from the outset. If this continues for two or three more quarters, the annualised run-rate will begin to approach the exit threshold. It is not there yet.

Active account plateau. A 1% year-on-year growth rate in active accounts is not a decline, but it is not a growth story either. The thesis does not depend on account growth; it depends on per-account value growing through higher transaction frequency and higher-margin product adoption. Transactions per active account are moving in the wrong direction. No exit condition references this metric, but it is a leading indicator of the revenue trajectory.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Branded checkout displacement accelerates. If Apple Pay, Shop Pay, or Stripe's native checkout takes two or more percentage points of share in a single fiscal year, the exit condition fires. The current disclosure gap makes this harder to monitor.
  • CEO transition costs time the business cannot afford. Enrique Lores has no fintech background. The three months to March 2026 were his first quarter as CEO. Prior to him, PayPal had three chief executives in five years. Another period of strategic drift — even a brief one — would allow competitors to widen their lead in the branded-checkout segment.
  • Consumer credit cycle turns. The charge-off rate has improved, but the overall consumer credit environment remains uncertain. If the portfolio deteriorates sharply — the exit threshold is 10% annualised — the earnings story changes materially.
  • Agentic commerce routes around PayPal. Artificial-intelligence shopping agents increasingly complete purchases autonomously, choosing the payment method without consumer input. If those agents route natively to card networks or embedded wallets rather than PayPal's checkout layer, the two-sided network's value proposition weakens structurally.
  • Regulatory action with operational restrictions. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's two open investigations could escalate. A consent order that restricts what PayPal can offer — particularly in consumer credit or cross-border payments — would change both the earnings trajectory and the risk profile of the bet.

Gaps in the Picture

Branded checkout volume is not separately disclosed. The most important condition in the thesis cannot be directly verified from the available filings. The company stopped breaking out branded-checkout-specific volume; the available proxy (PayPal-branded revenue versus Braintree revenue) is an imperfect substitute. If PayPal resumes disclosure, this gap closes automatically.

Two fair-value anchors coexist. The yield-based method used for the buy, trim, and exit thresholds places fair value at $48 per share (based on the cash-earnings yield at a 10.5% neutral rate). A separate scenario analysis using historical earnings multiples triangulated across three scenarios places the base-case fair value at $142 per share. The two estimates diverge by 69%. The divergence reflects a fundamental methodological difference: the yield method values the business as it is today, at current earnings and current multiples; the scenario method projects earnings three years forward under a base case of 20% operating margins and 6.5 billion dollars of annual cash generation, then applies the historical range of earnings multiples PayPal has traded at since 2022. A future calibration exercise would reconcile the two anchors; in the interim, the yield-based thresholds govern the verdict, and the scenario-based estimate is noted here for context.
