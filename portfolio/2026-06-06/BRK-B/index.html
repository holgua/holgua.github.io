Berkshire: The Bet Is Wobbling

The Bottom Line

The bet on Berkshire Hathaway — that Greg Abel and the culture Warren Buffett built will keep reinvesting retained earnings at higher rates than a passive index — is still working at the centre. Book value, insurance float, the railroad, and the manufacturing-and-retail collection all advanced. But one condition is wobbling: GEICO, the car-insurance arm and one of the most important branded businesses inside the group, posted its worst quarterly underwriting result in five quarters. The share price near $484 sits exactly at the fair-value anchor — neither cheap nor expensive on the rules used here. The right discipline now is to hold, not add, and read the next quarterly filing carefully for either a confirmation or a recovery.

The Verdict

The latest reading puts Berkshire in "still working, but one important arm just slipped" territory. That translates differently depending on the price paid for the shares.

  • As a fresh investment at today's price (around $484 per share). Wait. The shares are at fair value, not cheap, and a meaningful condition is now under pressure. The rules call for setting a price alert around $417 — the level that would mark the shares as clearly cheap — and waiting for either a thesis re-confirmation in the next quarterly filing or a pullback.
  • As an existing investment bought below $400 per share — currently sitting on a gain of 21 percent or more. Hold. Harvesting a gain because one arm of a many-armed compounder just had a soft quarter would be premature. The book-value engine, the float, and the railroad are all advancing.
  • As an existing investment bought roughly between $400 and $500 per share — currently anywhere between a small loss of 3 percent and a gain of about 21 percent. Hold. The case for the bet is still intact; the GEICO question changes the right action from "do nothing" to "do nothing and do not add."
  • As an existing investment bought between $500 and $600 per share — currently showing a loss of roughly 3 to 19 percent. Hold. A mild-loss position with one condition wobbling does not call for capitulation. Watching one more quarterly filing is the prudent path.
  • As an existing investment bought above $600 per share — currently showing a loss of 19 percent or more. Hold. Even at a meaningful loss, the underlying compounding engine is intact and no written line has been crossed. Capitulating into a wobble that has not become a break is the worst available choice.

About Berkshire

Berkshire Hathaway is, in plain terms, a giant holding company that owns a collection of operating businesses and a portfolio of public-company shares. Warren Buffett built it over six decades; Greg Abel took over as chief executive at the start of 2026 and now runs the operating side day to day.

The operating businesses break into four pillars. The first is insurance — GEICO (car insurance), General Re and Berkshire Hathaway Reinsurance Group (large global reinsurance), plus a basket of primary insurance lines. Insurance generates "float": cash collected as premiums today that will be paid out as claims years from now. Berkshire holds about $177 billion of this float and invests it in the meantime; in 2025 and again in early 2026, the cost of that float was negative, meaning the underwriting business itself was profitable and was effectively paying Berkshire to hold the money.

The second pillar is BNSF, one of the two big North American freight railroads. The third is Berkshire Hathaway Energy, a sprawling utilities and pipelines business that runs power and gas networks across several Western states. The fourth is the manufacturing, service, and retail collection — Precision Castparts, Lubrizol, Dairy Queen, See's Candies, Pampered Chef, and dozens more.

Beyond the operating businesses, Berkshire holds about $373 billion in cash and short-term Treasuries, a portfolio of public-company shares (Apple, American Express, Coca-Cola, Bank of America, and others), and various other minority investments. The shareholders' equity — book value — was $727 billion at the end of March 2026.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is straightforward: Berkshire is a capital-allocation compounder. Each year it generates a large pool of retained earnings and incoming insurance float; each year, management reinvests that pool into operating businesses or marketable securities at rates of return higher than a passive index fund would produce. The position is sized as a long-duration core holding, not a trade.

For the bet to keep working, the following conditions must keep holding:

  1. Book value per share keeps growing — and keeps pace. Growth in book value per share should not trail the global stock-market index (the MSCI World total return) for five or more consecutive years. A five-year lag would mean the compounding edge has gone.
  2. The insurance arm keeps generating profitable float. The pool of float should keep growing, and the underwriting that produces it should stay profitable enough that the cost of float remains negative or zero. GEICO, the largest branded insurance franchise, should keep writing at a healthy underwriting margin.
  3. The operating businesses keep growing earnings together. The combined after-tax earnings of insurance underwriting, insurance investment income, BNSF, the energy business, and the manufacturing-service-retail collection should keep advancing year on year.
  4. Capital allocation stays disciplined. No acquisition above $20 billion at more than 1.5 times the seller's book value; no goodwill impairment above $5 billion on past acquisitions; no structural culture change such as a regular dividend, a corporate break-up, or capitulation to activists.
  5. The share price doesn't run to where it pays more than $2 of price per $1 of book value. A price-to-book ratio above 2.0 would mean the market is pricing in unrealistic compounding, and a passive index becomes clearly the better long-run choice.

The Rules

The rules that govern what action follows from each combination of conditions and share price are fixed:

  • If all conditions are intact. Hold. No action. Let it compound.
  • If any one condition is wobbling but none has clearly broken. Do not add to the position. Watch the deteriorating condition through the next quarterly filing before reconsidering.
  • If any one condition has clearly broken. Sell the full position within thirty days. No averaging down.
  • If the share price has dropped into the bargain range and the conditions are still intact. Add to the position.
  • If the share price has run into the expensive range. Trim 25 to 33 percent of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.

The Conditions Today

The latest quarterly filing — covering the three months ending in March 2026 — reads condition by condition as follows.

Book value. Shareholders' equity rose by $9.8 billion in the quarter to $727.2 billion, on top of a 10.5 percent advance over the full prior year. The compounding engine is functioning as it should.

The insurance arm. Float grew by roughly $500 million in the quarter to $176.9 billion, and management explicitly states that the cost of float was negative in both the latest quarter and the same quarter a year earlier — meaning the underwriting business was profitable on its own. But the picture inside the insurance arm split sharply. GEICO had a difficult quarter: pre-tax underwriting earnings fell 35 percent to $1.4 billion, the combined ratio (losses plus expenses as a share of premiums earned) jumped from 79.8 percent to 87.3 percent, and management attributes the slip to higher claim frequencies and severities — including bodily-injury severities up 12 to 14 percent year on year. The reinsurance and primary insurance arms looked better, but mostly because the quarter had no significant natural-catastrophe events whereas the same quarter a year earlier did. The deterioration at GEICO is the wobble.

The operating businesses. After-tax earnings across the five reporting buckets totalled $10.1 billion versus $9.6 billion a year earlier, a 5 percent advance. BNSF, the railroad, performed unusually well — pre-tax earnings up 13.5 percent, volumes up 2.2 percent, and the operating ratio (expenses as a share of revenue) down 2.3 percentage points. The manufacturing-service-retail collection grew. Berkshire Hathaway Energy was about flat in aggregate; underneath, the United States utilities arm fell 16 percent on higher wildfire-prevention spending and lower tax-credit recognition, partly offset by stronger natural-gas pipelines.

Capital allocation. Abel's first major acquisition — OxyChem, completed at the start of January 2026 for $9.5 billion — sits well below the $20 billion size threshold that the discipline condition guards against, and was paid for in cash. Share repurchases during the quarter were $235 million, described by management as "relatively minor". The cash and short-term Treasury pile sits at about $373.5 billion and continues to grow. No dividend has been declared; no break-up rumour has surfaced. The discipline is intact, though the size of the cash pile is itself a quiet question.

Price-to-book. The current price implies that the market is paying $1.45 per dollar of book value, sitting exactly at the fair-value anchor. The trim line (where the rules call for harvesting) is at $2 per dollar of book value — comfortably far away.

The Share Price

At $484 the share price implies that, for every dollar of book value the company holds, the market is paying about $1.45. The rules anchor a fair price at $1.45 per dollar of book value — which is exactly where the shares sit today. Clearly cheap territory starts when the figure falls to $1.25 (a share price around $417); clearly expensive territory starts at $2.00 (a share price around $667 at today's book value).

Put plainly: today's share price is at parity with what the rules consider fair. There is no margin of safety for a fresh purchase, and no premium worth harvesting. That much is settled — Berkshire is neither a bargain nor a bubble at the moment.

What's Wobbling

One condition has clearly shifted from working to wobbling, and two secondary items are worth noting.

The primary wobble is GEICO. A jump of nearly eight percentage points in the combined ratio over twelve months — driven by both more claims and more expensive claims, alongside rising customer-acquisition costs — is the largest single quarterly deterioration in five quarters. The combined ratio is still profitable in absolute terms (a number under 100 means underwriting itself makes money), but the trajectory is the concern. The next quarterly filing will determine whether this was a single bumpy quarter or the first sign that Progressive and other rivals are taking share faster than expected.

Two smaller items sit in the same notebook. The first: the United States utilities arm of the energy business posted a 16 percent decline in earnings, weighed down by ongoing wildfire-prevention spending (and ongoing wildfire-related litigation in California and the Pacific Northwest from 2020 and 2022). The second: management bought back only $235 million of stock during the quarter, while sitting on $373 billion of cash. That choice tells its own story — the chief executive does not see the current share price as materially below his conservative reading of intrinsic value.

Neither of these crosses a written line. The case has moved from "intact" to "intact but wobbling on one piece" — which is precisely the situation the rules answer with "hold, do not add, wait for the next filing."

What Could Still Go Wrong

The case for Berkshire could still break in a small number of structurally distinct ways:

  • GEICO permanently loses share to Progressive. A single soft quarter is one thing; sustained share loss to a faster-moving rival on pricing technology and customer acquisition would erode the most important branded insurance franchise inside the group.
  • A successor allocation blunder. A $40 billion acquisition at a high multiple, or a goodwill impairment running into multiple billions on a past purchase, would mean the discipline that defines the bet has broken under new leadership.
  • A culture-shifting move. A regular dividend, a break-up, or any signal that the decentralised owner-operator model is being abandoned would each be reason to leave the position regardless of the operating numbers.
  • The cash pile keeps growing without being deployed. $373 billion sitting in short-term Treasuries earns a respectable yield today but is a structural drag on book-value compounding. A multi-year stall in deployment would slowly erode the case.
  • A major insurance catastrophe. Berkshire's reinsurance arm absorbs the world's biggest insurance shocks; a once-in-a-generation event (a major Florida hurricane, a California earthquake) could compress book value by a meaningful percentage in a single quarter. The business has weathered these before; the cushion absorbs but does not eliminate the hit.
  • The share price runs to clearly-expensive territory. A price-to-book ratio above 2 would trigger the rules to trim 25 to 33 percent. That is currently a long way off.

The worst plausible drawdown — a serious catastrophe, a GEICO confirmation of share loss, and a successor allocation misstep coinciding — could plausibly take the shares down 30 percent or more. The chance of permanent capital impairment over a ten-year horizon is unusually low for a business of this size, because the spread of operating businesses, the $373 billion cash cushion, and the float structure all push in the same direction. The chance of a soft three-to-five-year stretch during which the case is being re-proven is more realistic.

Gaps in the Picture

A few things in the underlying figures are worth flagging plainly.

The valuation method used here — paying $1.45 per dollar of book value — leans on book value as the relevant anchor. Book value is a meaningful number for Berkshire because so much of the asset base sits in marketable securities and operating businesses that are individually appraisable. It is not a perfect proxy for intrinsic value (Buffett himself stopped emphasising it years ago), but it is the most stable observable anchor the rules can use.

The GEICO read is based on a single quarter. The condition that would trigger a sale requires the deterioration to confirm across at least the next filing. One bumpy quarter on bodily-injury severity could be a one-time correction (a settlement-environment shift) or the first leg of structural share loss; the second-quarter filing will start to distinguish.

The successor question — whether Greg Abel allocates capital with Buffett's discipline — is unanswerable in twelve months. The first major test, the OxyChem acquisition, sits well inside the discipline lines (size, price). The harder tests are still ahead.

The shares-outstanding figure used in the valuation calculation is derived from market value divided by share price, because Berkshire's two-class share structure makes the raw filing data harder to read. The figure has been cross-checked against the quarterly report's own share-count disclosure and is consistent within a fraction of a percent.
