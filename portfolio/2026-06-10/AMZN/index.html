Amazon: The Bet Still Works

The Bottom Line

Amazon's latest quarterly filing leaves the central bet on the company intact, and by a comfortable margin. The cloud business is accelerating rather than slowing, advertising is compounding at the pace the bet always required, and the international shop is turning a profit for a second year. The share price, at around $250, is neither cheap nor stretched — it sits squarely inside the range the rules call fair. The one thing worth watching is the eye-watering scale of new spending on data centres, which would matter only if the cloud business stopped absorbing it.

The Verdict

The overall call, on the company as a business, is to stay the course. The bet is working, the share price is not in the bargain range, and the one condition to watch has not crossed any line yet.

What that translates to depends on where a reader is starting from. Five situations, in order of best outcome to worst:

  • As a fresh investment at today's price (around $250 per share). Wait. The share price sits above the fair-value mark of about $206, and well above the bargain line of about $165 where the rules would call for buying. The bet is working, but a fresh purchase here would carry no cushion at all — the price is paying for the bet to keep working rather than offering a discount for the risk that it might not.
  • As an existing investment bought below $250 per share — currently sitting on a gain. Hold. The bet is intact, the cloud business is accelerating, and nothing in the latest figures argues for booking the gain. Harvesting would require either the share price reaching the trim line (much higher) or one of the conditions breaking (none have).
  • As an existing investment bought roughly between $250 and $300 — currently around break-even, give or take a sixteenth. Hold. The same logic applies: the figures support patience and there is no compelling reason to take the position off the table.
  • As an existing investment bought between $300 and $350 — currently showing a loss of up to about 17 per cent. Hold. The mild loss is not a signal; the conditions behind the bet are working, and selling here would lock in a loss the figures do not justify.
  • As an existing investment bought above $350 per share — currently showing a loss of roughly 29 per cent or more. Hold. The temptation when a position is deep underwater is to capitulate, but the cloud business is accelerating and no exit condition has been crossed. The rules call for patience, not capitulation, while the bet is intact.

The same plain-English verdict, then, applies to every existing holder: hold. The only situation in which the call differs is the fresh buyer, for whom waiting is the prudent answer at today's price.

About Amazon

Amazon makes its money on three things that look like one company from the outside but behave like three separate businesses inside.

The first is the shop everyone knows — the website, the warehouses, the brown vans, the Prime membership. This is the largest business by sales (about $588 billion last year, counting the American and international sides together) but the thinnest by profit margin. It is the engine the rest of the company rides on.

The second is the cloud business, called Amazon Web Services. It rents computing power and storage to other companies — every start-up, most banks, much of the streaming industry, large parts of the American federal government. It sold about $129 billion of cloud services last year at a 35-cents-on-the-dollar operating margin, which means it generated more than half of Amazon's profits on less than a fifth of its sales. The bet on Amazon is, more than anything else, a bet on this business.

The third is advertising — the sponsored placements on the shop, the ads inside Prime Video, the spaces sold across the wider Amazon surface. This grew to about $69 billion last year and is growing at roughly twenty-two cents on the dollar per year, which makes it the fastest-growing major advertising business outside the obvious ones at Google and Meta.

The capital allocator binding all three together is the management team, with Andy Jassy as chief executive and Jeff Bezos as executive chair. Bezos still personally owns close to nine per cent of the company — roughly $234 billion at today's price — which is unusually large skin in the game for a chief at this scale.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is a single idea: that Amazon is the most diversified compounding flywheel in big technology — a retail and logistics core that funds two profit pools, the cloud and advertising, whose unit economics are still well below maturity.

For that bet to keep working, eight specific conditions must keep holding. Each has a defined line that, if crossed, breaks the bet:

  1. The cloud business keeps growing fast. Its quarterly sales must keep expanding by at least 15 per cent against the same quarter a year earlier. Two consecutive quarters below that line breaks the bet.
  2. The cloud business keeps its profit margin above 28 cents on every dollar of sales. Two consecutive quarters below that line — without a one-off explanation — would mean the heavy investment is no longer earning what it costs.
  3. No court-ordered structural break-up. The Federal Trade Commission is suing Amazon on monopoly grounds, with a trial set for the autumn of 2026. A court order forcing Amazon to separate parts of itself would break the bet.
  4. The capital-spending wave eventually pays for itself. If consolidated capital spending stays above $120 billion a year through both 2027 and 2028 without a matching acceleration in cloud revenue, the bet is broken — the spend has overshot demand.
  5. Advertising keeps growing. If its annual growth falls into single digits for two consecutive years, the third compounding engine has saturated and the bet is broken.
  6. No accounting trouble. Any disclosed weakness in the company's internal financial controls, or an unexpected change of auditor, breaks the bet on the grounds that earnings can no longer be trusted at face value.
  7. No abnormal insider selling. Bezos sells regularly under pre-announced plans; that is fine. A single filing flagging the sale of more than 40 million shares would be an acceleration outside the normal cadence and would break the bet.
  8. The earnings yield does not collapse. Owner earnings — operating cash minus the capital spending the company chooses to disclose — divided by today's market value must stay above 3 per cent. Below that line the price has gone too far ahead of the fundamentals to be defended.

The bet sits at the edge of comfortable understanding. The shop and the ads are easy enough to underwrite. The cloud business carries higher uncertainty because nobody yet knows how much of the current artificial-intelligence build-out will convert into durable revenue. That is why the conditions above are written tightly, and why the rules below are set in advance rather than improvised.

The Rules

The rules translate four states of the bet into four actions, and translate the share price into two more:

  • If the conditions are all working, hold. No action — let the compounding continue.
  • If one condition is bending but none has fully broken, do not add to the position. Run the same check again over the next two quarterly reports — one report to register the problem, a second to confirm whether it is a trend or a blip.
  • If a condition has fully broken, exit the full position within thirty days. Note which condition broke for future learning. The cloud-related conditions require two consecutive quarters of evidence first; an antitrust break-up order or an auditor change is immediate.
  • If the share price falls below the bargain line, add to the position in tranches over one or two quarters — but only after confirming that the conditions are still working.
  • If the share price rises above the trim line, trim a quarter to a third of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.
  • If the share price rises above the extreme exit line, exit the full position. At that price the market is paying for years of base-case growth instantly, and the implied forward return falls below what a less stretched investment would offer.

These rules are not advice. They are decisions made in advance, when the facts are dry and emotion is absent, so they can be relied on when the facts get loud.

The Conditions Today

The latest quarterly filing was published on 2026-04-30 and covers the three months ending 2026-03-31. Each condition reads as follows:

The cloud business is growing fast. Cloud sales reached $37.6 billion in the quarter, against $29.3 billion a year earlier — an increase of 28 per cent. The bet asked for 18 to 20 per cent; the line that would break the bet is 15 per cent. The condition is working, with thirteen points to spare.

The cloud business is keeping its margin. Operating profit on those sales was $14.2 billion, a margin of roughly 38 cents on every dollar of cloud revenue. That is slightly below the year-earlier figure of about 40 cents but still about ten cents above the line that would break the bet. The condition is working.

The antitrust case is unresolved. The filing acknowledges the litigation but discloses no court ruling, no settlement, and no structural remedy. The trial date remains in the autumn of 2026. The condition is intact for now, but the binary event sits ahead.

Capital spending is enormous. Cash spent on infrastructure was $43.2 billion in the quarter, against $24.3 billion a year earlier — almost double. Trailing twelve-month spending stands at about $151 billion, well above the $120 billion line. But the rule has a hinge: the condition only breaks if that spending continues without the cloud accelerating. The cloud just accelerated from 17 per cent growth to 28. The line, as written, has not been crossed. This is the one to keep an eye on.

Advertising is compounding. Advertising sales were $17.2 billion against $13.9 billion a year earlier — an increase of about 24 per cent. Comfortably above the single-digit line that would break the bet.

No accounting trouble. Ernst & Young has been the auditor since 1996. No restatements, no material weakness, no surprise. The condition is intact.

No abnormal insider selling. Bezos's selling continues at the normal cadence, none of it through a single filing above the 40-million-share line. The condition is intact.

Earnings yield is comfortable. Owner earnings divided by market value sit at 4.94 per cent — well above the 3 per cent floor and above the trim threshold of 4 per cent. The condition is intact.

The international shop, separately, has also kept turning a profit at a growing margin — about 3.6 cents on every dollar of sales, up from 3.0 a year earlier, on sales 19 per cent higher. The supporting bet on the international business is extending.

The Share Price

At about $250 a share, the rules read the price as fair. The owner-earnings yield — owner earnings divided by market value, the figure the rules use to score the price — comes to 4.94 per cent. The fair line is 6 per cent; the bargain line is 7.5 per cent; the expensive line is 4 per cent.

Put plainly: a higher yield means a cheaper price for the same earnings, so a yield of 4.94 sits modestly below fair value but still inside the band the rules call acceptable. If the yield were translated back into a share price at exactly fair value, the line would land at about $206. The market is paying roughly 22 per cent above that, which is well short of the 50 per cent premium that would trigger the trim rule.

The share price has ranged between $196 and $279 over the past year. Today's $250 is closer to the upper end than the lower end, but not dramatically so. The rules' verdict is unchanged: fair, neither cheap nor expensive enough to demand action.

The Standing Orders

The rules translate the share-price lines into two price alerts and three standing orders. Each is the operationalisation of a pre-committed rule — not a new judgement made here.

Price alerts (informational).
- A first alert at $206, the fair-value anchor. The crossing means the share price has returned to a neutral reading; no action follows, but a fresh read of the conditions is warranted.
- A second alert at $165, the bargain line. The crossing arms the buy order below; before adding, the latest re-validation should confirm the conditions are still working.

Standing orders (actionable).
- A standing buy order at $165. The rules call for adding in tranches once the share price closes at or below this line and the conditions are still working. The order may be staged across one or two quarters rather than filled in a single trade.
- A standing trim order at $309. The rules call for trimming a quarter to a third of the position when the share price closes at or above this line, to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.
- A standing exit order at $494. The rules call for closing the full position when the share price closes at or above this line. The market would be pricing in roughly 11 years of base-case compounding instantly at that reading; the implied forward return falls below the long-term equity premium even before growth, and reallocating elsewhere beats continuing to hold.

The three order levels do not contradict the verdicts above: those verdicts describe today's reading; the standing orders describe what the rules will execute if the share price moves to each line.

What's Wobbling

Two readings in the latest filing were mixed rather than clean. Neither has crossed a line that would break the bet, but both are worth naming.

The first is capital spending. The pace — roughly $43 billion in a single quarter and $151 billion over the trailing year — is well above the $120-billion-a-year line that would, if sustained for two full years without a matching cloud acceleration, break the bet. The cloud just accelerated. The line, as written, holds. But two more quarters of the same spending pace, paired with even a modest cloud slowdown, would move this from mixed to broken. It is the single condition to watch over the next two filings.

The second is capital return. The company bought back none of its own shares this quarter, while putting tens of billions into equity stakes in artificial-intelligence partners (OpenAI, Anthropic) and entering a $10.9 billion agreement to acquire the satellite operator Globalstar. The thesis explicitly tolerates aggressive reinvestment over buybacks. But two consecutive quarters of multi-tens-of-billions in non-cloud equity stakes would begin to look less like reinvestment and more like over-extension; the rules would then mark this condition down.

What Could Still Go Wrong

The bear case the underlying analysis lays out runs as follows:

  • The cloud business decelerates from 28 per cent growth back toward the mid-teens as Microsoft Azure and Google narrow the gap on artificial-intelligence workloads.
  • Some of the enormous capital spending creates excess data-centre capacity, forcing write-downs.
  • The Federal Trade Commission case produces a structural remedy or a very large settlement.
  • The mark-up on Amazon's stake in the artificial-intelligence company Anthropic partially reverses, reducing reported earnings.
  • The retail business sees margin compression from tariff pressure and competition from Chinese discount marketplaces such as Temu and Shein.

If that combination played out, the fair value of the shares could fall to around $151 — a decline of about 39 per cent from today's $250. Amazon would still be a high-quality business at that lower price; the loss would be a mark-to-market loss rather than a wipeout. But it is the realistic worst case the figures point to, and it is the reason the rules ask for a margin of safety before adding fresh capital.

Gaps in the Picture

A handful of caveats sit behind the figures.

The owner-earnings calculation strips out only the small slice of capital spending that the company tags as routine in its formal financial filings (about $6.7 billion last year). It does not strip out the much larger figure for total cash spent on infrastructure (about $128 billion last year), the majority of which is the cloud build-out. The valuation line therefore reads the price as fair on the strict accounting-tagged measure; on the broader cash-out measure it would read considerably more expensive. The two figures are not in conflict — they are answering different questions — but a reader should know that the 4.94 per cent yield rests on the narrower definition.

The owner-earnings figure also includes a non-cash mark-up of about $15 billion on Amazon's stake in Anthropic. Strip that out and underlying earnings are closer to $62 billion, putting the adjusted yield closer to 2.3 per cent — which sits below the yield available on a ten-year government bond. The bet that the shares offer a better risk-adjusted return than government debt rests entirely on growth from here.

Finally, the antitrust trial date is the single largest known unknown sitting ahead of the bet. The condition reads intact today because no ruling has been issued; the calendar will eventually move.

That much is settled. The bet is working, the share price asks for patience rather than action, and the next two quarterly filings will say whether the capital-spending wave is paying its way.
