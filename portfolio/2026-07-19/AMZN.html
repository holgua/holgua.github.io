Amazon: The Bet Still Works

The Bottom Line

Data as of 2026-07-19 · Latest filing 2026-04-30.

The bet is working. Amazon's two primary compounding engines — its cloud infrastructure business and its digital advertising operation — delivered another quarter of strong growth in early 2026, and all eight written exit conditions remain untripped. The share price sits in the upper half of the hold band, well above fair value, which means the rules call for holding the existing position rather than adding to it. The one condition to watch is whether the surge in data-centre construction spending — up nearly a hundred percent year-on-year in the first quarter — begins to convert into commensurate cloud revenue over the next two years, as the thesis requires.

How the Score Adds Up

Amazon scores 22.19, ranking 2 of 19 active holdings by this measure — it sits near the top of the portfolio, second only to PayPal.

score   = quality-weight × (expected return − fx/tax drag − hurdle rate)   = 1.20 × (24.85% − 1.46% − 4.90%)   = 1.20 × +18.49%   = 22.19

Quality-weight: 1.20. Amazon sits in the strongest of three moat tiers, which lifts the score by 20 per cent. The ranking reflects the combination of AWS switching costs (embedded long-term contracts confirmed in the latest filing), the two-sided marketplace network effect (third-party seller services above $170 billion a year), and cost advantages from custom silicon — each independently a meaningful barrier, together constituting one of the few genuine wide-moat structures in large-cap technology.

Expected return: 24.85%. The expected return is a probability-weighted blend of three forward scenarios from the value analysis, looking 3 years ahead. Each scenario's annualised return is computed as: (scenario fair value ÷ today's price) ^ (1 ÷ 3) − 1.

Bear (25% likelihood) — annualised return −14.7%. The bear case assumes Amazon Web Services growth decelerates to mid-teens as cloud competitors narrow the gap on artificial-intelligence workloads, the current infrastructure build strands capacity whose depreciation burden compresses margins, and a regulatory ruling imposes restrictions on how Amazon can use marketplace data to advantage its own products.

Base (50% likelihood) — annualised return +28.6%. The base case has cloud revenue sustaining 18–22 per cent annual growth as enterprise adoption broadens from model training to inference, the retail and advertising segments continue delivering steady margin expansion, and data-centre spending normalises toward 13–15 per cent of revenue by 2027 as the current construction cycle completes.

Bull (25% likelihood) — annualised return +56.9%. The bull case has Amazon Web Services accelerating above 25 per cent on custom AI chip adoption, the satellite internet project reaching commercial revenue for the first time, and advertising compounding toward the scale where the market re-rates it on a standalone basis rather than inside a blended retail multiple.

The likelihoods are analyst-assigned at thesis-write time. 0.25 × (−14.7) + 0.50 × 28.6 + 0.25 × 56.9 = +24.85%.

Hurdle rate: 4.90%. The hurdle rate is 4.90% — the 0.90% real yield on the German inflation-linked government bond (real cost of capital), plus the 2.0 percentage-point inflation target (to put the comparison on a nominal footing), plus a 2.0 percentage-point equity premium (the extra return required for holding a single company rather than a broadly diversified fund).

A score of 22.19 places Amazon second in the portfolio ranking — high enough that the expected return comfortably clears the hurdle even after the 1.46 percentage-point currency-hedging drag applied to a dollar-denominated holding.

Recent Development

Amazon's first-quarter 2026 filing showed the two engines that justify the bet running above the thresholds set when the thesis was written. Cloud revenue reached $37.6 billion in the three months ending March 2026, up 28 per cent from a year earlier, with a $364 billion committed backlog from enterprise customers disclosed in the filing. Digital advertising reached $17.2 billion in the same period, up 24 per cent. Neither figure has crossed any of the eight exit conditions.

The development that deserves the closest watch is not what confirmed but what was mixed. The cloud segment's operating profit margin — the share of cloud revenue that becomes operating income — compressed by about 175 basis points year-on-year, from 39.4 per cent in the first quarter of 2025 to 37.7 per cent in the first quarter of 2026. The margin sits 970 basis points above the 28 per cent exit threshold, so no action is required, but the direction matters: the thesis holds that heavy infrastructure investment causes a temporary compression and that margins recover as the installed capacity depreciates and workloads fill. The first quarter of 2026 is one data point in that story, not yet the confirmation.

Total infrastructure additions — new property and equipment — reached $54.8 billion in the quarter, up 99.5 per cent year-on-year. Exit condition four monitors whether this level of spending persists through 2027 and 2028 without commensurate revenue acceleration; that gate is two years away. For now, cloud growth at 28 per cent provides the revenue side of the equation. See the Share Price for where the valuation stands against all this.

About Amazon

Amazon operates three businesses under one roof, each at a different stage of maturity. The oldest and largest is the North American retail and logistics network — $426 billion of annual sales flowing through Amazon's own fulfilment centres and through the marketplace where independent sellers pay fees to reach Amazon's customers. Retail is a thin-margin, high-volume machine: its value lies not in the profit it generates directly but in the first-party data, the Prime subscription base, and the logistics infrastructure it creates for the other two businesses.

The second business is Amazon Web Services, the cloud computing platform that rents computing capacity, storage, and artificial-intelligence tools to companies and governments. At $129 billion of annual revenue and a 35 per cent operating margin, it supplies 57 per cent of Amazon's total operating profit on 18 per cent of its revenue. The third is digital advertising, which earns $69 billion a year by selling sponsored placements inside Amazon's own shopping and streaming surfaces — a business that sits on transaction-intent data that neither Google nor Meta can replicate because it is generated at the moment someone is ready to buy something. Advertising grew 22 per cent in the most recent full year. All three businesses share infrastructure, brand, and customer data in ways that make the combination more valuable than any one element on its own.

The Case for the Bet

The bet rests on the idea that Amazon's retail base is funding two profit pools whose economics are still maturing — and that both will compound operating income faster than revenue for several more years. The specific conditions that must hold for this to remain true are:

  1. Cloud revenue growth stays above 15 per cent for any rolling two-quarter window. Below 15 per cent for two consecutive quarters would signal that the AI thesis has not absorbed the large infrastructure investment, and the bet would require reconsideration.

Latest reading: +28.4% in the three months ending March 2026. The cloud segment's $364 billion committed backlog from enterprise customers, including a large artificial-intelligence expansion commitment, reinforces the near-term growth trajectory.

  1. Cloud segment operating margin stays above 28 per cent. A sustained compression below 28 per cent — absent a clearly one-off explanation — would indicate the reinvestment economics have broken, meaning new capacity is not generating enough revenue to cover its cost of capital.

Latest reading: 37.7% in the three months ending March 2026, compared with 39.4% a year earlier. The compression is consistent with infrastructure investment ahead of revenue recognition; 970 basis points of headroom above the exit line remains.

  1. No court-ordered structural remedy in the antitrust case. A ruling requiring Amazon to divest or structurally separate business units would alter the combined economics that the thesis prices in.

Latest reading: The latest filing references antitrust exposure only in generic legal risk disclosures. No new trial timeline or remedy has been announced.

  1. Capital expenditure stays below $120 billion in both 2027 and 2028, or is matched by commensurate cloud acceleration. Sustained high spending without revenue evidence would indicate infrastructure demand has been overestimated.

Latest reading: Infrastructure additions were $54.8 billion in the first quarter of 2026 alone, on track to exceed $120 billion for the full year. The exit condition is a forward gate on 2027 and 2028, not 2026; cloud growth at 28 per cent currently provides the revenue side of the test.

  1. Advertising revenue growth stays above single digits on an annual basis. Two consecutive years of single-digit growth would indicate the third compounding engine has saturated.

Latest reading: +23.9% in the three months ending March 2026. The advertising business is far from the single-digit line.

  1. No material weakness in internal controls, and no unexpected auditor change. EY has been the long-standing auditor of record; an unexpected departure would be a significant integrity signal.

Latest reading: No disclosure of material weakness or auditor change in the latest filing.

  1. No single insider filing disclosing more than 40 million shares. A filing of that size from the executive chair would signal abnormal acceleration beyond the established pre-planned cadence.

Latest reading: Insider sales continue through pre-planned disposal programmes; the latest disclosures are within the established cadence.

  1. Owner earnings yield stays above 3.0 per cent. A yield below 3 per cent (owner earnings divided by the total market value of all shares) would indicate the market has priced in optimistic assumptions about future capital spending normalisation.

Latest reading: 4.22% as of the most recent calculation, using full-year 2025 owner earnings of $113.3 billion. The yield sits above the 3 per cent exit threshold.

The Share Price

At $250, Amazon's owner earnings yield — the ratio of cash earnings to the total market value of all shares — stands at 4.22 per cent. The rules define fair value as 6.0 per cent (which corresponds to a share price of roughly $176), a buy opportunity at 7.5 per cent (roughly $140), and a trim signal at 4.0 per cent (roughly $263). At 4.22 per cent, the share price sits in the upper half of the hold band: above the fair-value anchor and approaching but not yet at the trim line.

In plainer terms: the business is generating real cash, and the pre-committed plan acknowledges that — there is no exit signal. But the share price has run ahead of what a conservative valuation would call fair, and a prospective buyer is paying a 42 per cent premium over that estimate. The market is implying slightly faster earnings growth than the thesis base case assumes, a gap examined in Why the Market Sees It Differently.

Why the Market Sees It Differently

The price says the business will earn more over time than the thesis thinks it can. The current share price of $250 sits 42 per cent above the thesis fair-value estimate of $176. That gap is substantial enough that it warrants a plain statement of what the market would have to believe to be right.

The first hypothesis is structural demand acceleration: the market could be pricing in cloud revenue remaining above 25 per cent growth for several years on the back of artificial-intelligence adoption, with the $364 billion committed backlog and large enterprise expansion commitments as evidence. Cloud growth was 28 per cent in the most recent quarter — this hypothesis is supported by current data.

The second hypothesis is margin recovery: once the current infrastructure build depreciates and workloads fill the installed capacity, cloud margins could recover above 40 per cent. The most recent quarter showed margins falling slightly, not rising, but the thesis argues the direction reverses once the investment cycle completes. The evidence here is partial — it does not contradict the recovery story, but it has not yet confirmed it either.

The third hypothesis is that a combination of factors — advertising reaching $100 billion, the international retail segment expanding its margins toward the levels North America already achieves, and the cloud contract pipeline — is worth more than the thesis currently prices. Both advertising growth (24 per cent) and international profitability (margin improving from 3.0 to 3.6 per cent year-on-year) support this reading.

For the market to give back its premium, AWS growth would need to decelerate meaningfully and the margin recovery story would need to stall. Neither has happened yet.

What's Wobbling

Two observations from the first-quarter 2026 filing are mixed rather than confirming, and each is worth naming.

The first is cloud margin compression. The cloud segment's operating margin fell from 39.4 per cent to 37.7 per cent year-on-year — a compression of roughly 175 basis points. The thesis anticipated this as part of an infrastructure investment cycle and does not set an exit condition until margins breach 28 per cent. The wobble is not the direction (expected) but the magnitude and persistence: if margin compression continues at this pace for another two or three quarters, the gap to the 28 per cent threshold narrows from 970 basis points today to something that deserves closer attention. No line has been crossed in the data to date, and the wobble does not change the current verdict as set out in the Bottom Line.

The second is capital expenditure. Infrastructure additions of $54.8 billion in a single quarter place full-year spending well above $120 billion — the level at which exit condition four begins to apply in 2027 and 2028 if unaccompanied by revenue acceleration. The condition is explicitly forward-looking, and cloud growth at 28 per cent currently provides the revenue side of the test. But the gap between infrastructure spending and cash earnings is real: owner earnings as a percentage of market value have fallen relative to where the thesis was anchored. The valuation context section of Gaps in the Picture addresses this directly.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Cloud competition accelerates faster than assumed. Microsoft and Google are investing comparable sums in AI infrastructure. If one of them delivers a model-quality or price-performance advantage that pulls enterprise workloads away from Amazon, cloud growth could decelerate below the 15 per cent exit threshold within 12–18 months. The bear case assigns 25 per cent probability to a scenario where this happens alongside a wider earnings compression.
  • The infrastructure build strands assets. At $128 billion of capital spending in 2025 guided higher in 2026, the depreciation burden of this investment will hit earnings regardless of revenue. If AI workload adoption is slower than the timeline the capex implies, operating margins will compress for longer than the thesis expects without the revenue to show for it.
  • A regulatory remedy restructures the marketplace. The antitrust case against Amazon's marketplace conduct is ongoing. A ruling requiring algorithmic neutrality on seller rankings — preventing Amazon from using its own sales data to advantage its branded products — would structurally cap operating leverage in North American retail even if no structural separation is ordered.
  • The Anthropic investment reverses. Amazon's stake in Anthropic generated a large non-cash gain in the 2025 annual accounts. If the valuation of that stake falls, the reversal flows through reported earnings as a loss, depressing the headline numbers even if the underlying operating businesses are performing well.
  • Infrastructure spending continues at peak levels through 2028. Exit condition four is designed to catch a scenario where the current spending level continues for two more years without commensurate revenue. The cash flow implications of sustained $120 billion-plus capital programmes would materially compress owner earnings yield even if cloud revenue grows strongly.

Gaps in the Picture

Owner earnings are calculated using total capital expenditure as reported under standard accounting definitions ($6.7 billion in 2025), which covers only narrowly defined property purchases. Amazon's actual infrastructure investment — which includes long-term lease obligations for data-centre equipment — is approximately $128 billion on management's definition. The two methods produce very different figures: $113 billion on the standard definition versus approximately $136 billion on the narrow definition. This analysis uses the narrow-definition figure as the basis for the cash-earnings yield calculation, which produces a more conservative result than the management definition would. Readers should be aware that the gap between the two methods is material and that the choice of method meaningfully affects both the yield calculation and the implied fair value.

The Fair-Value-Bands base scenario — a multi-method estimate using historical and peer valuations — implies a fair-value price around $368, corresponding to an owner earnings yield of 2.86 per cent. This diverges substantially from the thesis fair-value anchor of 6.0 per cent used in the valuation band calculations above. The disparity reflects different assumptions about what multiple of cash earnings the business should trade at over the long run; both are defensible, and the difference between them is a legitimate analytical question rather than an error. The thesis anchor is more conservative; the multi-method estimate is more generous. Both are flagged here because anyone comparing these figures to external sources will encounter both.

The Rules

The following rules govern what action follows from each combination of conditions and share price. They were set in advance and apply regardless of how the share price has moved since the thesis was written.

  • If the thesis is confirmed — all primary conditions holding, no exit condition triggered — hold the existing position without action and continue to let the compounding engines run.
  • If the thesis is weakened — a driver is deteriorating but no exit condition has yet triggered — do not add to the position. Conduct a fresh re-validation focused on the specific deteriorating driver, most commonly cloud growth or cloud margin. Reach a conclusion only after two quarterly data points, not one.
  • If the thesis is broken — any written exit condition triggers — close the full position within 30 days. No averaging down. AWS revenue or margin exits require confirmation across two consecutive quarters before acting; an antitrust structural remedy or an auditor replacement is an immediate exit with no waiting period.
  • If the share price falls below the buy edge ($140) — first confirm that the thesis is intact and that cloud growth is at or above 18 per cent. If both conditions are met, add to the position in tranches over one to two quarters.
  • If the share price rises above the trim edge ($263) — trim 25 to 33 per cent of the position to harvest the premium while retaining the core holding. Re-evaluate the remaining position size after the trim.
  • If the share price rises above the exit edge ($421) — close the full position. The market would be pricing in roughly 11 years of base-case compounding immediately; the implied forward return falls below the long-term equity premium, and redeploying proceeds to a cheaper opportunity dominates continuing to hold. No re-entry within 90 days.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
