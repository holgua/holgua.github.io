Bund Linker 2033: The Bet Still Works

The Bottom Line

The inflation-linked bond issued by the Federal Republic of Germany and maturing in April 2033 is doing exactly what it was bought to do. All four conditions that underpin the holding are intact, the real return embedded in today's price is comfortable above the fair-value anchor, and none of the three named triggers that would require an exit has fired. The compound mechanism that defines the most demanding exit condition — sustained negative real yield combined with EUR cash offering a better real return — is inactive by a meaningful margin. The one condition to watch is the real yield, which sits between the fair-value anchor and the level that would prompt adding to the position; it is neither cheap enough to warrant scaling in nor expensive enough to warrant trimming.

About the Bond

The Federal Republic of Germany issued this inflation-linked bond (ISIN DE0001030583, German treasury registration number 103058) in February 2021 with a maturity date of 2033-04-15. It carries a nominal coupon of 0.10% a year, but that figure understates the actual cash received: both the coupon payments and the repayment of principal at maturity are scaled upward in line with the German harmonised index of consumer prices, excluding tobacco (a European Union price index published by Eurostat). In plain terms, if prices rise 10% between today and 2033, the bond pays out 10% more principal than was originally lent.

The quoted market price — €95.47 as of 2026-06-16 — is the "clean price," expressed per €100 of original face value in real, purchasing-power-adjusted terms. The actual euros that change hands at settlement are higher, because the face value itself has grown with inflation since issuance. To get the settlement amount, multiply the clean price by the index ratio (currently 1.25675): a buyer pays roughly €120.09 per €100 of original face value at settlement, not €95.47. The €95.47 is the quoted clean price in real terms — the same purchasing-power units as the issuance date. To get the actual euros that change hands, multiply by the index ratio (1.25675), then add accrued interest (€24.62 per €100 face as of the snapshot date).

The issuer carries a AAA rating from Fitch and S&P, and Aaa from Moody's. Germany has never defaulted on a euro-denominated obligation. Issuance volume stands at €10.65 billion, making this a liquid instrument in the European sovereign bond market.

The Case for the Bet

The holding is structured as an inflation hedge, not as a rates trade. The bet is simple: a AAA-rated sovereign bond whose real return is guaranteed in purchasing-power terms is a sound allocation when the alternatives — EUR cash at the ECB deposit rate, or conventional nominal German government bonds — do not reliably preserve real purchasing power.

The bet holds as long as four conditions remain true:

  1. Real yield remains attractive relative to alternatives. The real yield at the current market price should stay above −1.0% and should compare favourably with EUR cash after accounting for inflation. The 0.50% level is the fair-value anchor; above 1.50% is the zone where adding makes sense.

  2. Break-even inflation remains consistent with the ECB's target. The break-even rate (the market's implied expectation for inflation over the bond's life, derived by comparing its real yield with that of conventional German government bonds of similar maturity) should stay close to the ECB's 2% target. Large deviations — either a sharp deflationary episode pricing negative inflation, or speculative overshoot — would distort the hedge's effectiveness.

  3. Germany's sovereign credit rating stays above AA−. The exit trigger here is a downgrade below AA− by any two of the three major rating agencies (Fitch, Moody's, and S&P). A downgrade of that magnitude would signal a structural shift in issuer creditworthiness — a scenario that has never occurred in the post-war history of the Federal Republic.

  4. The HICP ex-tobacco index methodology remains intact. If Eurostat restructured the index in a way that severed the link between the benchmark and actual consumer prices — for instance by substituting an entirely different reference index — the inflation-protection function of the bond would be impaired.

The Rules

The pre-committed rules, mapped to each state of the evaluation:

  • If all conditions are working and the real yield is within the band: hold the position at its current size. Real purchasing-power protection is operating as intended.
  • If any condition is showing strain but no exit trigger has fired: do not add. Reassess if the real yield moves below 0% but before exit condition (1) activates; compare the real after-tax return against EUR cash and conventional German government bonds before taking any action.
  • If any exit condition fires: close the position. Move proceeds to short-duration nominal German government bonds or EUR cash.
  • If the real yield rises above 1.50% (bond price falls below €91): add to the position in proportion to the inflation-hedge allocation target. A real yield above 1.50% on a seven-year AAA EUR sovereign linker is highly attractive relative to alternatives.
  • If the real yield falls below −0.50% (bond price rises above €104): trim 25% of the position on the first crossing. If the real yield is still below −0.50% at the next quarterly review — at least 90 calendar days later — trim a further 25%. Park proceeds in EUR cash or short conventional German government bonds. The trim ladder is bounded: further crossings do not trigger additional trims until exit condition (1) fires.
  • If the compound exit trigger fires (real yield below −1.0% for at least 90 days AND EUR cash offers a superior real return): close the full position. Switch proceeds to EUR cash or short-duration nominal German government bonds; at that point the inflation-hedge function is no longer additive relative to the alternative.

The Real Yield Today

The real yield is the return above inflation locked in at the current market price, expressed as a percentage per year and computable from the clean price, the coupon, and the maturity date. It is the bond equivalent of what equity investors call an earnings yield: higher real yield means the bond is cheaper in purchasing-power terms, lower means it is more expensive.

At €95.47, the real yield of 0.78% sits in the holding zone — above the 0.50% fair-value anchor derived from the ECB deposit rate plus a 30-basis-point spread, and well above the −0.50% trim threshold. The bond is neither cheap enough to warrant adding nor expensive enough to trigger any part of the trim sequence.

The fair-value anchor of 0.50% was calibrated on 2026-06-15 using the ECB deposit rate of 2.25% less break-even inflation of roughly 1.98%, plus a 30-basis-point quality spread reflecting the instrument's superior inflation-linkage relative to cash. At a 0.78% real yield, the market is pricing the bond at a small discount to that anchor — in other words, at a slight premium to fair value from the buyer's perspective, but nowhere near the levels that would concern the rules.

The relevant comparison is EUR cash. The European Central Bank's deposit facility rate stands at 2.25%. With break-even inflation at 1.98%, EUR cash earns a real yield of approximately +0.27%. The linker's 0.78% real yield is therefore +51 basis points ahead. This comparison is pre-tax — German income tax applies to both legs (Abgeltungssteuer on cash interest and on the linker's coupon plus index accrual); the after-tax direction is not modelled here.

What's Wobbling

There are no active wobbles. All four drivers are reading positively against their conditions. The sole monitoring note in the latest re-validation is that the credit and index-integrity data was sourced from a sidecar dated 2026-06-15 — one day prior to the yield snapshot — which is well within any reasonable staleness window. No change on a one-day lag is expected or observed.

That said, two structural items are worth keeping in view. First, the real yield of 0.78% is not far from the 0.50% fair-value anchor; a modest compression — driven by a further ECB rate cut or a rise in break-even inflation — could push the bond toward the lower half of the holding zone without triggering any rule. Second, the ECB deposit rate used in the compound trigger check (2.25%) reflects the most recent Governing Council decision; any further ECB easing would reduce the advantage the linker holds over EUR cash, even if neither the 0.50% fair-value nor the −0.50% trim threshold were breached.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • A sharp drop in the real yield driven by falling nominal rates. If the ECB cuts rates aggressively and break-even inflation remains stable, the nominal Bund yield falls, the real yield compresses, and the clean price rises toward (or past) the €104 trim threshold without any inflation event occurring. The holding rules handle this mechanically — trim on the first crossing of €104 — but it would reduce the portfolio's inflation-hedge allocation at a moment when the hedge might still be needed.
  • A break-even overshoot. If market participants price in substantially more than 2% inflation, the break-even rate rises, the implied real yield compresses further for a given clean price, and the linker becomes more expensive without inflation necessarily materialising. The rules do not have a separate break-even ceiling; the real-yield band handles the net effect.
  • ECB rate cuts outpacing inflation. If the ECB eases faster than inflation falls, EUR cash real yields drop toward zero or below, reducing the cash alternative's attractiveness — but also compressing the linker's relative advantage. The compound exit trigger's two-leg design means a scenario where the linker real yield falls below −1.0% while cash real yield also falls below −1.0% would not fire the exit, because cash would not be offering a superior real return.
  • A sovereign credit event. While the probability of a German downgrade is remote by any historical standard, the exit trigger is defined precisely so that a decision can be made without deliberation if it occurs.
  • Eurostat index restructuring. The HICP ex-tobacco index is a mature, politically stable benchmark. Methodology changes occur at a glacial pace and are well-signalled. The risk is low but not zero — it is the kind of risk that materialises slowly and with warning, giving time to act before exit condition (3) formally fires.
