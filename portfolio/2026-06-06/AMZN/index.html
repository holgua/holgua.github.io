Amazon: The Bet Is Wobbling

The Bottom Line

The bet on Amazon as a long-term compounding machine is still mostly working, but one condition is wobbling: the pace at which Amazon is spending on artificial-intelligence infrastructure has stepped up faster than the original case assumed. The other conditions are working — cloud growth, advertising, international retail — as well as the case requires, or better. The share price near $250 sits between the fair-value anchor and the expensive line: no bargain, no obvious reason to harvest.

The Verdict

The latest reading puts Amazon in a position the rules call "still working, but watch one thing." That translates differently depending on the price paid for the shares.

  • As a fresh investment at today's price (around $250 per share). Wait. The current share price is above the fair-value anchor of about $206 and well above the bargain price of roughly $164 that would justify a new purchase. The rules call for setting a price alert around $164 and waiting.
  • As an existing investment bought below $250 per share — currently sitting on a gain. Hold. No reason to add at today's price and no reason to harvest. The compounding engines (cloud and advertising) are still running as the case requires.
  • As an existing investment bought roughly between $250 and $300 per share — currently around break-even, give or take 17 percent. Hold. Adding shares is off the table until either the share price drops to the bargain range or the spending question resolves.
  • As an existing investment bought between $300 and $350 per share — currently showing a loss of roughly 17 to 29 percent. Hold. A mild-loss position sits out a wobble; the prudent path is to watch one more quarterly filing before reconsidering.
  • As an existing investment bought above $350 per share — currently showing a loss of about 29 percent or more. Hold. A deep-loss position paired with a still-working-but-wobbling bet does not call for selling. Locking in the loss while the cloud business is still growing at 28 percent would be premature.

About Amazon

Amazon is three businesses welded together by a single capital allocator.

The first is the online retail operation — the website most people know, plus the marketplace where independent sellers list goods that Amazon ships through its own fulfilment network. This is the cash engine: $426 billion in sales in 2025, throwing off about seven cents of operating profit on every sales dollar, up from roughly two cents three years earlier.

The second is Amazon Web Services (AWS), the cloud-computing arm that rents out data-centre capacity to other companies. This is the small-but-disproportionate profit pool: $129 billion of revenue in 2025 — only about a fifth of the total — but 35 cents of operating profit on every sales dollar, contributing more than half of company-wide operating income.

The third is advertising. Amazon sells sponsored placements on its own retail pages, on Prime Video, and across its other surfaces. It is a $69 billion business growing in the low-twenties percentage range, and most of the revenue drops to the bottom line because the platform — the website and the audience — already exists.

The international side of the retail business is part of the first leg but worth flagging separately: it turned profitable only in 2024, and the margin has been climbing since. That climb is part of the case for staying invested.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that Amazon is the most diversified compounding machine in mega-cap technology — a retail engine that funds two younger, higher-margin businesses (cloud and advertising) whose economics still have years of room to run.

For the bet to keep working, the following conditions must keep holding:

  1. AWS keeps growing fast and earning healthy margins. Cloud revenue should grow at least 15 percent year on year — ideally 18 to 20 percent — and the cloud segment should keep earning at least 28 cents of operating profit on every revenue dollar, ideally 30 cents or more.
  2. Advertising stays in double-digit growth and international retail keeps improving. Advertising should not slip into single-digit growth; the international retail operation should keep widening its profit margin from the recently positive base.
  3. The AI infrastructure spending pays off. Total spending on property and equipment should not stay above $120 billion per year in both 2027 and 2028 unless cloud revenue is accelerating to match. In plain terms: the spending and the resulting cloud sales should keep moving together.
  4. No court-ordered break-up. The unresolved antitrust trial brought by the Federal Trade Commission should not produce a structural remedy requiring Amazon to separate AWS or the marketplace from the rest of the company.
  5. Accounting and governance stay clean. No surprise change of auditor (Ernst & Young has held the engagement since 1996), no disclosed internal-control failure, and no unusually large or unscheduled share sales by Jeff Bezos that fall outside the established pre-arranged trading plan.

The Rules

The rules that govern what action follows from each combination of conditions and share price are fixed:

  • If all conditions are intact. Hold. No action. Let the cloud-and-advertising flywheel compound.
  • If any one condition is wobbling but none has clearly broken. Do not add to the position. Watch the deteriorating condition through one more quarterly filing — then a second — before reconsidering.
  • If any one condition has clearly broken. Sell the full position within thirty days. No averaging down. The cloud-revenue and cloud-margin conditions require two consecutive quarters of confirmation before triggering a sale; a court-ordered break-up or a sudden auditor change is grounds for immediate exit.
  • If the share price has dropped into the bargain range and the conditions are still intact. Add to the position in stages over one or two quarters.
  • If the share price has run into the expensive range. Trim 25 to 33 percent of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding, then reconsider sizing.

The Conditions Today

The most recent quarterly filing — covering the three months ending in March 2026 — reads condition by condition as follows.

AWS growth and margin. Cloud revenue grew 28 percent year on year, comfortably above the 18 to 20 percent target and well above the 15 percent floor that would mark a problem. Segment operating profit was 37.7 cents on every sales dollar, again above both the 28-cent floor and the 30-cent target. That much is settled: the central engine of the case is running hot, not slowing.

Advertising and international retail. Advertising revenue grew about 24 percent year on year — far above the single-digit line that would signal saturation. International retail operating profit jumped from about $1.02 billion to $1.42 billion, with the margin widening from 3.0 percent to 3.6 percent. Both supporting engines are doing what the case requires.

AI infrastructure spending. This is the wobble. Cash spending on property and equipment was $43.2 billion in those three months alone — 78 percent higher than the $24.3 billion in the comparable quarter a year earlier. Trailing-twelve-month spending was $151 billion. Management explicitly says spending will rise again in 2026. Annualised, that runs around $172 billion — well above the $120 billion ceiling that condition three sets for 2027 and 2028. The catch: cloud revenue is also accelerating (28 percent versus 17 percent a year earlier), which means the condition's escape clause — "unless cloud revenue accelerates to match" — is currently being met. The condition is being approached, not crossed.

The antitrust trial. The latest filing repeats boilerplate language about ongoing monopolisation claims brought by the Federal Trade Commission and state attorneys general, with no update on a specific trial outcome or remedy. The binary risk remains pending; nothing has changed.

Accounting and governance. No auditor change. No internal-control issues. Bezos disposed of about 27.86 million shares over the trailing twelve months — a figure worth roughly $5.65 billion — but every transaction was conducted under a pre-announced trading plan adopted in November 2025, and the pace is well within the historical cadence. Nothing here triggers a line.

The Share Price

At $250 the share price implies that, for every dollar paid for Amazon stock, the business currently generates about 4.9 cents of free cash flow each year. The rules anchor a fair price at 6 cents per dollar — roughly $206 per share — and call the shares clearly cheap only when the figure reaches 7.5 cents per dollar (a price near $164), or clearly expensive when it falls to 4 cents (a price around $308).

The current 4.9 cents sits inside the acceptable range, but closer to the expensive edge than to the cheap one. Put plainly: today's price pays full freight for the cloud-and-advertising case to deliver. There is no cushion against disappointment, and no premium worth harvesting either.

Two things to note. First, the "free cash flow" used here is the strict accounting figure, before subtracting the much larger headline capital-spending number management uses in its own commentary; that distinction is what makes today's free-cash-flow figure look healthier than the headline. Second, the alert price of $164 — the cheap edge — is the level at which the rules would call for buying again, conditions permitting.

What's Wobbling

Two of the five conditions are showing pressure.

The first is AI infrastructure spending. As noted above, the pace is running well above what the case originally assumed. The escape clause is currently doing its work — cloud revenue is accelerating in step — but if cloud growth slows even modestly while spending stays elevated, the condition's logic breaks down. This is the condition to watch, and the rules call for at least one more quarter of data before any change.

The second is tariff and trade-policy pressure. Amazon's most recent filing flags unpredictable shifts in global tariff and trade policies as a material factor and elevates the language on Chinese sellers and suppliers as a specific exposure. So far this is forward-looking risk language; no segment-level revenue or margin damage is visible in the latest quarter. The retail business in North America still grew 12 percent and international 19 percent. The wobble lives in the wording, not yet in the figures.

Neither pressure crosses a written line this quarter. The case has moved from "intact" to "intact but wobbling on one thing" — which is precisely the situation the rules answer with "hold, do not add, wait for confirmation."

What Could Still Go Wrong

The case for Amazon could still break in a small number of structurally distinct ways:

  • Cloud share lost to Microsoft Azure or Google Cloud for artificial-intelligence workloads. AWS was the original leader; Microsoft and Google have been closing the gap on the specific workloads that drive new AI projects. A multi-quarter loss of share would compress the profit pool that contributes the majority of operating income.
  • The AI spending wave fails to convert into revenue. If the data centres and chips built in 2025 and 2026 do not earn back their economics, the result is impairment charges, depressed return on capital, and a re-rating of the whole AWS thesis. This is the same risk that condition three guards against, viewed from the loss side.
  • A court-ordered structural remedy in the antitrust case. A judicial order forcing Amazon to separate AWS or the third-party marketplace from the retail core would re-rate the entire business. The trial timing has slipped into mid-2026 or later.
  • Consumer recession plus escalating tariffs. A combined macroeconomic and trade shock would compress retail margins quickly. The cloud business would keep growing through it, but a twelve-to-eighteen-month margin squeeze in the retail engine would matter.
  • A governance or accounting failure. A material internal-control disclosure, an unexpected auditor change, or an unusually large Bezos share sale outside the existing plan would each be a reason to leave the position regardless of business performance. The trip-wires are deliberately set: clean accounts and disciplined insider behaviour are part of the bet.

The worst plausible drawdown — combining an AI-spending impairment with an adverse trial outcome — is a 45 to 55 percent fall in the share price, comparable to what happened during 2022. The chance of permanent capital loss over a ten-year horizon is low given the balance-sheet strength (about $90 billion of cash and marketable securities) and the spread of businesses. The chance of holding underwater capital for three to five years at today's entry price is real.

Gaps in the Picture

A few things in the underlying figures are worth flagging plainly.

The free-cash-flow yield used to anchor "cheap" and "expensive" treats every dollar of spending on property and equipment as the narrow accounting line — about $6.7 billion in 2025 — rather than the headline capital-spending figure management talks about in its own commentary, which was roughly $128 billion. The two figures are reconciled in the filings, and the gap is largely an artefact of how lease-funded assets are classified, but it does mean today's "4.9 cents on the dollar" reading looks healthier than the headline cash story.

The AI-spending wobble is based on a single quarter. The condition that triggers a sale requires the elevated pace to persist across two full fiscal years — the next two quarterly filings are the first chance to confirm whether the first three months were an inflection or a one-off.

The antitrust trial has no scheduled outcome on the page. The binary risk it represents — a small probability of a structural break-up, a larger probability of a financial settlement — sits in the background of every quarter until it resolves.

The figures behind the bear, base, and bull cases referenced above come from operating-income and free-cash-flow projections derived from the underlying drivers rather than pulled from a pre-built scenario table. The narrative around them is consistent; the per-share fair-value midpoints (roughly $110 in the bear case, $200 in the base, $310 in the bull) carry the uncertainty that any three-year projection carries.
