About Berkshire

Berkshire Hathaway is a holding company, but that description understates what it is. The business model has three reinforcing layers.

The first is an insurance operation — Berkshire's car-insurance business, GEICO; the reinsurance arm, General Re; and Berkshire Hathaway Reinsurance Group — that collects premiums from policyholders before any claim is paid. The gap between when premiums come in and when claims go out is called the insurance float, and Berkshire's float stood at $176.9 billion at the end of March 2026. That float is invested, giving Berkshire a pool of money that costs little or nothing to hold and that grows as the insurance businesses grow.

The second layer is a collection of wholly-owned operating businesses acquired over sixty years: Burlington Northern Santa Fe Railway (one of two dominant freight railroads in the western United States), Berkshire Hathaway Energy (regulated electricity and gas utilities across six US states), Precision Castparts (aerospace components), and hundreds of smaller manufacturers, retailers, and service companies. The third layer is a roughly $298 billion portfolio of publicly traded shares — Apple, Bank of America, American Express, and Coca-Cola among the largest — whose value fluctuates with the market.

The business was built over six decades by Warren Buffett, who handed the chief executive role to Gregory Abel on 2026-01-01. Buffett, now 94, remains chairman and holds approximately 14% of the economic interest. Abel spent thirty years building Berkshire Hathaway Energy from a small Iowa utility and is regarded as Buffett's chosen successor. The central question for the coming decade is whether the capital-allocation culture transfers intact.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that Gregory Abel and the management culture Buffett built will keep reinvesting retained earnings and the insurance float into high-returning businesses and securities at rates exceeding what could be achieved through a passive index. The position is sized as a long-duration core holding.

Four conditions must hold for the bet to work:

  1. Book value per share growth exceeds the MSCI World total return over rolling five-year windows. Five or more consecutive years of underperformance would signal that the compounding edge has been lost.

  2. Acquisitions remain disciplined. No single deal above $20 billion at a reported price above 1.5 times the book value of the acquired business, and no subsequent goodwill write-down exceeding $5 billion on any single deal. A breach would confirm that Abel lacks Buffett's capital discipline.

  3. The decentralised owner-operator model is preserved. No regular dividend, no break-up, no capitulation to activist pressure. Any of these structural changes would signal an abandonment of the model that generates Berkshire's compounding edge.

  4. The price-to-book ratio stays below 2.0 times. Above that level, the valuation fully prices in optimistic compounding, and the opportunity cost versus a passive index becomes clearly negative.

The Rules

The rules that govern what happens under each combination of conditions and share price:

  • If the thesis holds: hold, no action — let the position compound.
  • If the thesis shows strain: add nothing to the position; run a fresh analysis on the specific deteriorating driver; decide after the next quarterly filing.
  • If the thesis breaks: exit the full position within 30 days; no averaging down.
  • If the share price falls below the buy edge: confirm the thesis is intact first; if confirmed, add to the position.
  • If the share price rises above the trim edge: trim 25 to 33% of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.

The Conditions Today

Book value growth. Berkshire's shareholders' equity grew 10.5% in the twelve months ending December 2025, from $649 billion to $717 billion. In the three months ending March 2026 it grew a further $9.8 billion to $727 billion, despite $1.2 billion of after-tax investment losses. That rate of growth comfortably exceeds the MSCI World's recent returns. Condition 1 is working.

Acquisition discipline. Abel's first significant acquisition since taking the chief executive role was the January 2026 completion of the OxyChem deal — purchasing Occidental Petroleum's chemicals business for approximately $9.5 billion. The acquired assets were estimated at $10.8 billion at the acquisition date, implying the purchase price was below asset value. That is well within the discipline threshold. Buybacks in the three months ending March 2026 were minimal: $235 million, confined to March only, with nothing in January or February. Management's stated reason is that the share price, at around 1.46 times book, is at or above its conservatively determined intrinsic value estimate. Condition 2 is working.

Cultural continuity. The most recent quarterly filing contains no announcement of a dividend, no restructuring, no sign of activist engagement. The decentralised model — subsidiaries run by their own managers, capital allocated at the parent level — appears unchanged. Condition 3 is working.

Valuation ceiling. The price-to-book ratio stood at approximately 1.46 times at the time of the latest filing, well below the 2.0 times exit ceiling. Condition 4 is working.

The Share Price

At around $484, Berkshire trades at approximately 1.45 times its book value per share. The rules set a fair-value anchor at 1.45 times book, a buy edge at 1.25 times (around $417), and a trim edge at 1.75 times (around $584). The share price sits at fair value — not in the bargain range and not in the harvest range.

The price-to-book method is appropriate here because Berkshire is a financial holding company where the balance sheet is the business: the investment portfolio is marked to market, the insurance float is a liability with a known cost, and the combined equity base is the most accurate single measure of what the business is actually worth. A profit-based measure would be distorted by accounting rules that require gains and losses on Berkshire's investment portfolio to pass through the income statement each quarter, producing swings of tens of billions of dollars that have nothing to do with the operating performance of the underlying businesses.

What's Wobbling

One condition is showing a live strain signal: the GEICO loss ratio — the percentage of every dollar of premiums paid out in claims — increased by 4.9 percentage points in the three months ending March 2026, to 73.9%. The cause was higher claims frequency (more accidents) and higher severity (more expensive repairs and medical costs), particularly in bodily injury coverage. Frequency in that category rose five to seven per cent year-on-year; severity rose twelve to fourteen per cent.

The group-level picture looks better: overall pre-tax insurance underwriting earnings rose to $2.27 billion from $1.72 billion a year earlier, partly because the comparable quarter in 2025 included $1.1 billion of losses from the Southern California wildfires that do not repeat. Stripping that comparison effect out, the underlying trend at GEICO is less reassuring.

One quarter of loss-ratio deterioration does not trigger any of the written conditions. GEICO's combined ratio — a broader measure that includes operating expenses alongside claims — still came in below 90% for the quarter. But GEICO's competitive position is already the most contested part of the thesis: Progressive surpassed State Farm as the largest US personal auto insurer in early 2026 while GEICO held third. A further two or three quarters of loss-ratio deterioration would constitute a genuinely wobbling signal on condition 1, even if no written exit condition fires outright.

Investment income from Berkshire's Treasury bill and fixed-income holdings fell 7.2% in the quarter from a year earlier, reflecting lower interest rates. This is a mechanical consequence of rate movements, not a signal about the operating businesses, but it does reduce one of the cleaner cash-flow inputs to the earnings picture.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Abel makes a value-destroying acquisition. The most important single risk is that the new chief executive deploys Berkshire's $369 billion cash reserve into one or more large businesses at prices that destroy value — either by paying too much or by acquiring businesses with poor economics. The OxyChem deal at $9.5 billion was sensibly priced; a deal of $50 billion or more at above book value with no clear competitive advantage would be the red flag.

  • GEICO continues to lose ground to Progressive. Progressive's data-driven pricing gives it a structural advantage in personal auto insurance. GEICO confirmed technological parity in May 2025, but parity in technology does not automatically translate to parity in accumulated customer data or pricing precision. If GEICO's combined ratio rises above 100% for two consecutive quarters, the most profitable segment of Berkshire's insurance operation would be in structural underwriting loss.

  • The BNSF western duopoly ends. A proposed merger between Union Pacific and Norfolk Southern, if approved by the Surface Transportation Board (a decision expected in 2027), would create a combined railroad with over 40% of US freight rail traffic. Burlington Northern Santa Fe's western corridor pricing power — which supports an operating ratio (the share of revenue consumed by costs) below 70% — would face meaningful competitive pressure for the first time in decades.

  • PacifiCorp wildfire liabilities re-escalate. Berkshire Hathaway Energy's regulated utility PacifiCorp faces ongoing legal claims related to wildfires in the Pacific Northwest. Accruals were $100 million in 2025, down sharply from $1.7 billion in 2023. A return to the 2023 level would be a material charge against the energy segment's earnings and net worth.

  • The equity portfolio marks down. A 30% decline in Berkshire's roughly $298 billion public equity portfolio would produce an approximately $90 billion paper loss under current accounting rules. This would not impair the operating businesses, but it would reduce shareholders' equity and push the price-to-book ratio upward — potentially reaching the valuation ceiling at condition 4 before the business itself has deteriorated.

Gaps in the Picture

The diluted share count for Berkshire is not available through the standard machine-readable filing data, because Berkshire's unusual two-class share structure (Class A and Class B, with a 1,500-to-one conversion ratio) is not mapped in the standard extraction pipeline. The share count used throughout the analysis — approximately 2.15 billion Class B equivalents — is implied from dividing the reported market capitalisation by the current share price. This is a reliable cross-check but is not the figure management discloses directly in the filing.
