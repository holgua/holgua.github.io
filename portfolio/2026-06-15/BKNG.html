About Booking

Booking Holdings runs Booking.com, the world's largest accommodation marketplace by property count. The business model is simple: a hotel, apartment, or rental property lists on the platform at no upfront cost; a traveller books through Booking.com; the platform collects a commission — roughly 10% of the transaction — when the stay occurs. The company also operates Priceline (focused on the US market), Kayak (a search aggregator), and OpenTable (restaurant reservations).

The economic character of the business is determined by two things: the depth of its two-sided network, and the efficiency of its marketing spend. The network — approximately 4.5 million listed properties, of which around 4 million are non-hotel accommodation (apartments, holiday homes, bed-and-breakfasts) — is worth more to each new property listing because of the travellers already using it, and worth more to each new traveller because of the properties already there. The marketing efficiency refers to the balance between bookings that arrive organically through the platform's own reputation and those that require paid advertising to acquire. The more bookings arrive without paid advertising, the more profitable each one is.

The Case for the Bet

The central claim is that Booking.com's two-sided network is durable enough to survive the three principal threats to its position: AI systems that could replace the booking interface, Google's push to direct travel searches toward its own booking products, and hotel chains' ongoing efforts to pull customers to their own websites. The supporting claim is that management returns substantially all the company's free cash — the cash generated after running the business and making necessary investments — to shareholders through buybacks and dividends, compounding the value per share over time.

Seven conditions must keep holding for the bet to work:

  1. Room-night growth must not fall to low-single-digit or negative levels across multiple regions for two or more consecutive quarters, absent an isolated temporary shock — which would signal the network effect is losing force.
  2. Marketing spend as a share of gross bookings must not rise meaningfully across two or more consecutive years — which would signal the platform is buying growth through advertising rather than earning it through reputation.
  3. The revenue take-rate — the percentage of each booking retained as revenue — must not compress materially or persistently — signalling that competitive pressure, regulation, or AI intermediaries are squeezing the economics.
  4. Buybacks must not slow or stop while the earnings yield is attractive, and must not be redirected into a large acquisition that dilutes the capital-return programme.
  5. The earnings yield at the current share price must not fall below 4% — at which level the valuation prices in many years of optimistic compounding and the risk-reward no longer justifies the AI and regulatory exposure.
  6. By the three months ending in September 2027, management must have articulated and visibly executed a coherent AI-first strategy for how Booking.com competes when AI agents become the primary way travellers plan and book trips — with concrete products showing measurable engagement or margin results, and explicit acknowledgement of disintermediation risk in public communications. The current approach (Smart Filters, AI-powered traveller experience features) does not yet clear this bar.
  7. EU enforcement under the Digital Markets Act or Digital Services Act must not result in a formal decision imposing structural product restrictions, or fines exceeding 3% of annual global revenue in a single year.

The Rules

The pre-committed rules for this holding:

  • If the bet is working: hold — no action. Continue compounding.
  • If a driver is weakening but no exit condition is crossed: do not add. Revisit after the next two quarterly filings.
  • If an exit condition fires: exit the full position within 30 days. No averaging down.
  • If the share price falls to $156 (earnings yield above 7.0%): confirm first that the bet is intact, then add to the position in tranches over one to two quarters.
  • If the share price rises to $273 (earnings yield below 4.0%): trim 25–33% of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.
  • If the share price rises to $437 (earnings yield extreme): exit the full position. At that level the market is pricing in approximately 6.5 years of base-case compounding in advance; redeploy proceeds at the next cheap-edge crossing or into a cheaper alternative. No re-entry within 90 days.

The Conditions Today

Room-night growth and network integrity. Global room nights booked in the three months to March 2026 reached 338 million, up 6% from 319 million a year before. That is well above the low-single-digit or negative threshold required for exit condition (1) to apply. March room-night growth came in at approximately 1%, which the filing attributed to the conflict in the Middle East — a geographically contained, sudden-onset disruption that fits precisely the kind of isolated shock the exit condition carves out. Excluding the affected region, the company estimated underlying growth at approximately 8%. Exit condition (1) requires a multi-region, multi-quarter deteriorating pattern; Q1 2026 delivers neither.

Marketing efficiency. Marketing spend as a share of gross bookings was 3.8% in the three months to March 2026, unchanged from the same period a year earlier. The filing noted that paid-channel costs were temporarily pressured by conflict-related cancellations and rebooking activity; underlying, the platform described modest marketing leverage supported by direct-channel growth. The two-consecutive-year rising pattern that would trigger exit condition (2) is not present.

Take-rate integrity. Revenue as a share of gross bookings rose to 10.3% in the three months to March 2026, from 10.2% a year before. The direction is expansion, not compression. Exit condition (3) — material or persistent compression — is the opposite of what the filing reports.

Capital return. Buybacks in the three months to March 2026 were $3.8 billion, up 73% from $2.2 billion in the same quarter a year before. The remaining repurchase authorisation stands at $18.2 billion. The quarterly dividend rose from $0.38 to $0.42 per share. No acquisition is disclosed. Exit condition (4) — capital-return discipline breaking — is not approached.

Valuation. The earnings yield at the current share price of $170 is approximately 6.9%. The exit condition (5) threshold is 4.0%, which corresponds to a share price of approximately $273. See the share-price section.

AI strategy. The company's public communications describe its AI effort as the "Connected Trip" — an AI-powered traveller-experience platform shipped across Booking.com and the mobile app. The mobile-app share of room nights advanced into the high-fifties percentage range. No measurable agentic-traffic volumes, direct-engagement uplift attributable to AI, or margin uplift from AI features appears in the quarterly filing. The deadline for a clear, evidence-backed strategy is the three months ending in September 2027 — more than five quarters away. The clock is running; the call is open; it is not yet late.

EU regulatory exposure. Italy opened a new competition investigation in April 2026. Spain's regulatory fine remains suspended on appeal. Proceedings are active in Switzerland, France, Greece, Hungary, and the Netherlands. No formal decision has imposed structural product remedies, and no fine approaching 3% of annual global revenue has been issued. Exit condition (7) is not triggered, though the regulatory environment is elevated.

What's Wobbling

Three signals arrived softer than the underlying trend this quarter, though none crosses a written exit condition.

Room-night growth came in at 6% at the headline level — the March figure in particular was only 1%, reflecting the Middle East conflict. The filing is explicit that the disruption was regional, sudden, and isolated; ex-conflict growth runs at approximately 8%. One quarter, one region, one named external cause: that is not the multi-region, multi-quarter pattern exit condition (1) requires, and the filing's language leaves little room for alternative interpretation.

Marketing efficiency held at 3.8% year-on-year, which counts as stable. Intra-quarter, paid-channel costs were temporarily pressured by the conflict-related cancellations. The annual trend required for exit condition (2) is not in the data.

The AI strategy remains the most material open item. "Connected Trip" is a vision with shipped features; it is not yet a documented strategy backed by measurable outcomes — no agentic-traffic numbers, no measurable margin contribution from AI-specific features. The Q3 2027 deadline is more than five quarters away, and the pace of development in AI-powered travel will determine how much of that runway is genuine.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • AI disintermediation. An AI agent that plans and books a trip without opening a booking website removes Booking.com from the transaction entirely. This is what exit condition (6) was written to catch; no measurable defensive product exists yet, and the window for building one is not unlimited.
  • Google deepens its travel product. Google already shows hotel prices and availability in search results. If it extends this to seamless end-to-end booking, the paid-search channel that Booking.com uses to acquire customers turns into a direct competitor simultaneously.
  • Hotel chains tighten direct-booking advantages. Major chains offer loyalty-only rates and check-in privileges for direct bookings. A meaningful increase in rate disparity could erode the value proposition of aggregating through Booking.com.
  • EU enforcement escalates. The volume of active regulatory investigations is elevated across multiple European jurisdictions. A formal decision imposing structural product changes — rather than fines — would alter the business model in ways difficult to reverse.
  • Global travel demand softens. A recession or a significant demand shock would compress gross bookings across the industry. Booking.com would lose revenue proportionally; its cost structure would not adjust as quickly.
