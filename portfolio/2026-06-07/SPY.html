SP500: The Premium Has Vanished

The Bottom Line

The bet on the SPDR S&P 500 ETF Trust — that the basket of 500 large American companies will, over a long enough holding period, pay a reasonable premium above risk-free Treasury bonds — has slipped past the line that calls for harvesting. The basket's earnings yield (the earnings the companies produce, divided by the price paid for them) has fallen to 3.75 cents per dollar invested, which is roughly four-fifths of a percentage point less than what 10-year United States Treasury bonds pay for no risk. The structural quality of the basket remains exceptional and the fund's mechanics are clean, but the rules answer this combination with a specific action: trim 25 to 33 percent of the position. One cost cohort — a deep-loss position — is the exception.

The latest reading puts the basket clearly past the expensive line. That translates differently depending on the price paid for the units, and the verdict varies cohort by cohort rather than reading as one blanket call.

The rules call for setting price alerts: one at $553, which would mark fair value, and one at $443, which would mark the basket clearly cheap.
  • As an existing position bought below $700 per unit — currently sitting on a gain of about 5 percent or more. Trim 25 to 33 percent of the position. The basket is meaningfully expensive against risk-free, and a sizeable-gain cohort has the cushion to release some of the position back toward the size target while locking in the premium.
  • As an existing position bought between $700 and $800 per unit — currently anywhere between a small loss of about 8 percent and a small gain of about 5 percent. Trim 25 to 33 percent. Today's price sits in this cohort; the basket's earnings yield (3.75 cents per dollar) is sliding toward the 3.0 cent exit floor, and the rules call for a partial harvest of the premium at this position.
  • As an existing position bought between $800 and $900 per unit — currently showing a loss of roughly 8 to 18 percent. Trim 25 to 33 percent. A mild-to-moderate loss past the expensive line still calls for harvesting; the action shrinks equity exposure without crystallising a capitulation loss.
  • As an existing position bought above $900 per unit — currently showing a loss of about 18 percent or more. Hold. Capitulating from a deep loss to harvest a premium that no longer exists is the wrong direction. The structural case is wobbling but not broken; wait for either an exit condition to fire or for the basket to recover.

About the Fund

This is the SPDR S&P 500 ETF Trust (ticker SPY), the largest and oldest exchange-traded fund in the world. State Street Investment Management launched it in 1993, and it tracks the S&P 500 index — a basket of 500 of the largest publicly traded American companies, weighted by company size. About $784 billion in assets sits in the fund today.

The plain consequence of size-weighting is that the largest companies dominate the basket. NVIDIA, Apple, Alphabet (Google's parent), Microsoft, and Amazon together make up roughly 30 percent of the holdings. Owning one unit of the fund means owning a thin slice of every company in the S&P 500, but with the heaviest weights in the very largest names. Technology businesses now account for about 39 percent of the basket — a tilt that has drifted well beyond the sector-balanced index of historical memory.

The fund charges 0.0945 percent per year — about one cent for every $10 invested annually — which sits among the lowest fees in the industry and is the operational reason the fund earns its place as a passive American-large-cap core holding. The fund pays out the dividends that the underlying companies generate, currently about 0.98 percent of the basket's value each year.

The Case for the Bet

The bet on this fund is not that the S&P 500 is always a fair purchase — Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway sits on roughly $300 billion in cash today, an explicit signal that even history's most successful equity investor does not always find shares priced to reward the risk. The case is more disciplined: over a long enough holding period, the basket's earnings yield (its annual earnings divided by its price) should exceed the risk-free Treasury rate by at least 2 percentage points. That cushion — the premium paid for taking equity risk — is what compensates for the volatility, the drawdowns, and the years in which shares lag bonds.

For the bet to keep working, these conditions must keep holding:

  1. The basket pays a real premium over risk-free. The basket's earnings yield should not fall below 3 cents per dollar invested AND simultaneously sit below the 10-year Treasury yield for two consecutive quarterly snapshots. Either condition alone is a warning; both together is the structural fail.
  2. The annual fee stays low. State Street should not raise the expense ratio above 0.15 percent without a competitive fee response from cheaper rivals.
  3. The fund stays large enough to survive. Assets in the fund should not fall below $100 billion — a level that would indicate an extraordinary liquidity event in the world's most liquid equity vehicle.
  4. No single name dominates. The largest holding should not exceed 13 percent of the basket. Historical drift has crept up toward 7 to 8 percent at the top; 13 percent would mean a regime change, not normal drift.
  5. The index methodology stays consistent. S&P Dow Jones Indices should not materially restructure the S&P 500 in a way that undermines its broad-American-large-cap mandate.
  6. The visible top of the basket keeps its quality. The median return on equity — the share of profit each dollar of company net worth generates — across the deduped top-10 holdings should not collapse below 15 percent. Below that line, even the visible top has lost its quality.

The Rules

The rules that govern what action follows from each combination of conditions and basket price are fixed:

  • If all conditions are intact. Hold the position. The basket is reasonably priced and the fund's mechanics are sound.
  • If any one condition is wobbling but none has clearly broken. Do not add. Re-check after the next quarter. If the bond-versus-stock cushion stays negative for a second snapshot, or the top-10 quality keeps falling, switch to a cheaper rival fund (such as IVV or VOO) or move to cash according to the position-sizing rule.
  • If any one condition has clearly broken. Sell the position. The fund's mechanics have failed or an exit condition has fired. Move the proceeds to risk-free assets or to a holding that meets the value test.
  • If the basket price has dropped into the bargain range. Add to the position. The basket is meaningfully cheap against risk-free.
  • If the basket price has run into the expensive range. Trim 25 to 33 percent of the position. The basket is meaningfully expensive against risk-free; harvest the premium.

What's Wobbling

The valuation. Specifically, the relationship between what the basket pays in earnings and what risk-free Treasuries pay in interest.

The basket's earnings yield of 3.75 cents per dollar would, in isolation, be a stretched but defensible level for a high-quality equity index. The problem is the comparison: 10-year United States Treasury bonds pay 4.54 percent. A passive equity bet should reasonably pay at least two percentage points above the risk-free rate to compensate for the risk of holding shares. Right now the comparison is roughly 80 basis points the wrong way — shares pay less than bonds.

This is the precise regime the case explicitly anticipated. The reference to Buffett's $300 billion cash pile in the case was the analogy: even history's most successful equity investor steps back from shares at these readings. The rules say the right answer is not to sell (the basket's structural quality is intact and the fund's mechanics are clean) but to harvest — to take 25 to 33 percent of the position off the table.

The condition that would actually break the bet — earnings yield below 3 percent AND a negative bond-versus-stock cushion across two consecutive snapshots — has only one of its two legs in place, and only for one snapshot. Another snapshot at this reading would crystallise the structural fail.

Two minor caveats are worth filing. The basket's weighted-mean return on equity has drifted unusually high (68.3 percent) because of buyback-driven equity shrinkage at Apple and NVIDIA. The median (37.3 percent) is the cleaner read and is healthy. The fund's mechanics — fee, fund size, concentration — are all comfortably inside their ceilings. The wobble is the price, not the product.

What Could Break the Fund

The case for this fund could break in a small number of structurally distinct ways:

  • The basket becomes decisively expensive. A move to an earnings yield below 3 percent at a moment when Treasuries still yield more — and the combination sustained across two consecutive quarterly snapshots — would be the full structural fail. One snapshot is already at the wrong leg of that combination; another such snapshot would crystallise the exit.
  • State Street raises the fee. A fee hike above 0.15 percent would eliminate the case for using this fund specifically over its cheaper rivals (BlackRock's IVV and Vanguard's VOO, both near 0.03 percent). The mechanical answer is to switch funds, not to switch out of the asset class.
  • Single-name concentration crosses 13 percent. If NVIDIA or another mega-cap rises to roughly 1.6 times its current weight, the basket would no longer be the diversified American-large-cap exposure the case relies on.
  • A liquidity event. A sustained drawdown in fund assets below $100 billion would mean a once-in-a-generation event has overtaken the world's most-traded equity vehicle. This is improbable but defines the floor.
  • S&P Dow Jones changes the rules. A material restructuring of the index — for example, removing a category of constituents, changing the weighting rule, or narrowing the universe — would mean the underlying product has changed unilaterally, not by choice.
  • Top-10 quality collapses. If the median return on equity of the basket's largest holdings falls below 15 percent, the visible top has lost its quality. That line is currently well above the trigger.

The most probable break in the near term is the first one — the basket simply stays expensive against bonds for another quarter. The mechanical answer is not a full sale but a managed wind-down of exposure, switching into cash or into a cheaper passive vehicle as the position is trimmed.

Gaps in the Picture

A few things in the underlying figures are worth flagging plainly.

The earnings yield comes from the trailing 12-month price-to-earnings ratio published by Yahoo Finance. A forward-earnings basis would put the yield slightly higher, because analysts project earnings growth, but the rules use the trailing measure deliberately — it is what the basket has actually delivered, not what analysts hope it will.

The median return on equity for the top-10 holdings reads healthily at 37.3 percent, but the weighted-mean reading at 68.3 percent reflects buyback distortion at Apple and NVIDIA. Buyback-funded equity shrinkage mathematically inflates the calculation. The median is the cleaner read.

The index-methodology condition has no automated signal. Changes to S&P 500 construction rules are announced by S&P Dow Jones Indices and propagate slowly; the case relies on external monitoring between automated runs. The same applies to changes in the State Street trust structure.

The 10-year Treasury yield comes from a live market quote on the snapshot day. It can move 20 to 40 basis points in either direction over short periods; the bond-versus-stock cushion is therefore an estimate, not a frozen reading. Sustained measurement across more than one quarterly snapshot is the rules' filter for noise versus signal.

The earnings figure that drives the basket's price-to-earnings ratio is the simple cap-weighted aggregate of constituent earnings. A cyclically-adjusted measure — which would average earnings across a full economic cycle to filter out boom-and-bust distortion — is not currently produced from the available data. A cyclically-adjusted yield is typically lower than the trailing reading at late-cycle valuations, so the wobble is, if anything, understated by the figure used.
