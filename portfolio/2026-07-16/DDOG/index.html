Recent Development

The three months ending 31 March 2026 delivered the clearest confirmation the thesis has seen in several quarters. Revenue grew 32% year-on-year to just over $1bn for the first time in a single quarter, re-accelerating above the base-case range and ahead of every scenario the analysis had modelled. About three-quarters of that growth came from customers Datadog already had — existing accounts spending more, not a surge in new-logo wins.

The expansion within the existing customer base is the operational heart of the bet. Net revenue retention — the rate at which the installed base grows its own spending, without counting new customers — rose to the low-120% range for the trailing twelve months, up from the high-110% range a year earlier. The 4,550 enterprise accounts spending at least $100,000 a year grew 21% year-on-year and now represent 90% of all recurring revenue. The share of customers using ten or more products climbed from 6% a year ago to 11% — each additional product deepens the integration footprint and raises the practical cost of switching to a competing platform.

The one mixed signal was cash generation. In the three months ending March 2026, the company converted 28.7% of revenue into free cash — the cash left after running the business and paying for infrastructure — down from 32.1% in the same period a year earlier. The compression came from higher spending on cloud infrastructure hosting and additional headcount. The first quarter tends to carry heavier onboarding costs than the fourth, so the comparison is not perfectly like-for-like, and 28.7% remains well above the 25% floor that would prompt a formal review. Still, the direction — two consecutive quarters of declining margin — is the single data point worth watching before the next results cycle. For a fuller picture of how this sits within the valuation, see The Share Price.

About Datadog

Datadog sells software that watches over computer systems while they run. Every application a company operates — a web server, a database, a machine-learning model, a mobile app backend — generates a continuous stream of signals: how fast it is responding, whether it is throwing errors, how much memory it is consuming, what requests it is handling. Datadog collects all of those signals in one place, across every cloud environment a customer uses, and makes them searchable and alertable in near-real time. The core value is visibility: an engineer on call at 2am, dealing with a system that has slowed to a crawl, can open a single screen and see precisely where the problem originated — which service, which version, which downstream dependency.

The business model is largely usage-based: the more data customers send, the more they pay. Because cloud infrastructure tends to grow over time as companies scale their applications, and because each new Datadog product a customer switches on generates more data streams, revenue compounds within the customer base without requiring a proportional increase in sales effort. The company now offers more than 25 distinct products — ranging from infrastructure monitoring and application tracing through security threat detection and AI workload observability — and the deepening of the platform within existing accounts is the primary growth engine.

The Case for the Bet

The central wager is that Datadog's integration depth — agents embedded in every host, container, and serverless function in a customer's environment — creates a switching cost high enough to sustain expansion within the installed base, and that the AI observability wedge widens the platform before hyperscaler-native tools reach the same level of cross-cloud, cross-signal capability. The conditions that must keep holding are:

  1. Net revenue retention stays above 112% for any two consecutive quarters. Retention at or above this level means the existing customer base is still expanding its own spending faster than it churns. Below 112% for two quarters in a row is the first signal that the land-and-expand flywheel has stalled — most likely from customers substituting AWS CloudWatch, Azure Monitor, or Google Cloud's native monitoring tools for single-cloud workloads, or from AI-native customers trimming their telemetry budgets once their initial usage spike normalises. The watch threshold is 115%: a drop below that level without recovery would prompt a formal reassessment.

Latest reading: Low-120%s trailing twelve months as of 2026-03-31, up from high-110%s a year earlier. The flywheel is re-accelerating, not slowing.

  1. Gross margin stays above 77% for any two consecutive quarters. Gross margin — the share of each revenue dollar left after paying the cloud infrastructure costs that Datadog incurs to deliver its service — has to stay above 77% for two consecutive quarters before an exit condition fires. The watch threshold is 78%. Compression below 77% would signal that rising third-party hosting costs are being absorbed rather than passed on, structurally impairing the unit economics of each new customer.

Latest reading: 79% in the three months ending 2026-03-31 and the year-ago quarter, flat year-on-year. Revenue and cloud infrastructure costs grew proportionally. No deterioration.

  1. Share-based compensation stays below 25% of revenue, or management credibly commits to bringing it below 25% within two years. Share-based pay is a real cost that dilutes existing shareholders even though it does not consume cash. At 22% of revenue in the full year ending January 2026, it sits close enough to the 25% ceiling that the gap is meaningful. If SBC rises above 25% without a credible management commitment to normalise it within two years, the distance between cash generation and genuine per-share value creation becomes too wide to ignore.

Latest reading: 19.6% in the three months ending 2026-03-31, improved from 21.6% in the same quarter a year earlier. Revenue is growing faster than share-based pay, which is the right direction.

  1. Revenue growth stays above 20% for any two consecutive quarters. Below 20% for two consecutive quarters is the signal that growth has decelerated beyond the natural slowing expected as the revenue base gets larger — indicating competitive displacement or a broad contraction in cloud and IT spending budgets.

Latest reading: 32% year-on-year in the three months ending 2026-03-31, re-accelerating from 28% in the full year ending January 2026. Well clear of the threshold.

  1. No single acquisition exceeds $2bn at premium multiples. The acquisition track record has been disciplined: small tuck-ins, typically between $10m and $220m, that extend the platform without introducing integration complexity at a scale that could impair execution. A departure from that pattern — a large acquisition at the valuations that software companies currently command — would introduce execution risk inconsistent with the current earnings base.

Latest reading: Acquisitions in the full year ending January 2026 totalled $118m. No large acquisition has been announced.

  1. No material regulatory enforcement action affecting cross-border data telemetry or AI model training. The business collects operational telemetry from customers in many jurisdictions and is subject to data-privacy laws including the EU's General Data Protection Regulation and its AI Act. The exit condition fires if a material fine, or a mandatory compliance shutdown that blocks more than 5% of revenue, occurs under GDPR, the EU AI Act, or an equivalent data-sovereignty framework in the Asia-Pacific region.

Latest reading: As of 2026-03-31, the latest filing contained extensive disclosure on applicable regulations but no enforcement actions, fines, or market-exit announcements. Exit condition 6 is not triggered.

The Share Price

The current share price of $258 implies an earnings yield of about 0.27% on an owner-earnings basis — that is, Datadog's free cash flow after deducting share-based compensation, divided by the total market value. The threshold for fair value in the analysis is 2.5%, which works out to a share price of approximately $28. The threshold for a cheap-enough price to act is 4.0%, which corresponds to approximately $18.

At $258, the share price is roughly nine times the fair-value anchor. That is not a rounding error in the methodology — it reflects a genuine and unresolved difference between what the analysis treats as fair value (built from the company's current cash generation, with no premium for future growth) and what a market participant buying or selling today must implicitly believe about Datadog's future growth rate and the multiple at which that growth will eventually be priced. The market is pricing in something closer to a decade of near-perfect execution; the analysis sets fair value at what the business generates today.

The free-cash-flow margin compression in the three months ending March 2026 — down from 32% to 29% — does not change the band position at this price level, but it is a reminder that the numerator in the yield calculation (the cash the business generates) is not moving in a direction that closes the gap.

The Standing Orders

Two price alerts are set: one at $28, which is where the share price reaches the fair-value anchor on an earnings-yield basis; and one at $18, which is where the price crosses into territory that the analysis treats as cheap enough to act.

At $18, the instruction is to buy — but only after confirming that the thesis remains intact: net revenue retention at or above 115%, free-cash-flow margin at or above 25%, and no exit condition triggered. The position is to be built in two tranches over one quarter.

At $47, the instruction for anyone holding a position is to begin trimming: reduce by 25–33% of the position. At the current price of $258 — already far above $47 — the instruction is not to initiate a new position and, if holding, to trim. At 0.27%, the current earnings yield on an owner-earnings basis is already below the 1.5% level that defines the trim threshold.

No exit-tier price is set. The band structure has three tiers — buy, hold, trim — and the standing orders follow that structure.

What's Wobbling

The one ambiguous signal from the three months ending March 2026 is free-cash-flow margin. It came in at 28.7%, down from 32.1% in the same quarter a year earlier. The compression was driven by two things: $41.8m in additional cloud infrastructure hosting costs (as the company expanded capacity ahead of demand), and increased headcount expenses as Datadog added engineers. Neither is an exit condition by itself — the 25% floor is the line that would trigger a formal reassessment, and 28.7% sits well above it.

The seasonal pattern matters here: the first quarter of the year historically carries heavier onboarding and infrastructure provisioning costs than the fourth quarter, which makes year-on-year comparisons at the same seasonal point more useful than sequential comparisons. On that basis, the compression is real but not alarming. The condition to watch is whether the second and third quarter of 2026 show the same pattern persisting, which would shift the signal from seasonal to structural. That matters for the thesis because free-cash-flow generation is the numerator in the yield calculation — if the margin compresses structurally, the price at which the yield crosses the buy threshold rises alongside it.

No other driver raised a concern. The hyperscaler competitive threat produced no measurable data in the filing — the latest quarterly report contained standard boilerplate competitive-risk language but no specific disclosure of market-share loss, win-rate deterioration, or pricing pressure attributable to AWS CloudWatch, Azure Monitor, or Google Cloud's monitoring tools. Absence of data is not confirmation of safety, but neither does it constitute a wobble. The data-privacy regulatory risk is similarly quiet: no enforcement actions, no new market-exit requirements.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • The biggest risk does not require anything to go wrong at the business level. At 94 times the trailing twelve months of free cash flow, the share price would fall dramatically if the market's assumed future growth rate were revised downward — even if Datadog continued executing well. A rerating to the median multiple of comparable software companies would imply a loss of roughly 70%, without any change in the underlying business.
  • Net revenue retention could compress toward 110% if AI-native customers — early adopters who ramped usage rapidly and now look for ways to optimise their cloud costs — reduce their Datadog spending or fail to renew. The latest filing explicitly named this group as a forward risk and flagged that some large AI-native customers had already optimised usage. If this pattern broadens, the compounding mechanism at the core of the bet weakens.
  • AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud each offer monitoring tools built into their own platforms at little or no additional cost. Customers who run their entire infrastructure on a single cloud have decreasing incentive to pay a separate vendor for what the cloud provider offers for free. Multi-cloud customers remain Datadog's strongest base, but the proportion of workloads concentrated on a single cloud could shift over time.
  • Share-based compensation running at 22% of revenue and growing slightly faster than revenue itself means each additional year of growth dilutes existing shareholders to a degree the cash generation numbers do not fully reflect. This does not impair the business, but it does mean that per-share value grows more slowly than the headline free-cash-flow figures suggest.
  • The $1bn convertible note maturing in 2029 adds headline dilution risk if the share price at conversion is below the hedged ceiling. At the current price it is not a near-term concern, but it is one additional variable in the share-count equation.

Gaps in the Picture

The earnings yield calculation used throughout this analysis deducts share-based compensation from free cash flow before computing the yield. This gives a conservative figure of $249.9m rather than the $1,001m pre-SBC figure. Both numbers appear at various points in the source material and measure genuinely different things: one is the cash the business generates; the other is the cash that remains attributable to existing shareholders after accounting for dilution. Neither is wrong, but they produce very different yield figures and therefore very different price anchors.

The maintenance capital expenditure split — what fraction of the $49.6m in annual capital spending goes to keeping the existing infrastructure running versus expanding it — is not disclosed and has been estimated in the underlying analysis at approximately $25m. If the true maintenance fraction is higher, the earnings figure would be lower.

If the price is in exit territory — the market is pricing in base-case earnings compounding at no discount to fair value — exit the full position or remain out. Redeploy proceeds to a holding with a positive risk/reward. No re-entry within 90 days to defuse anchor bias.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
