About the Bond

Bund Linker 2033 is a German government bond with one unusual feature: both its principal and its periodic coupon payments are adjusted upward in line with the German Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices excluding tobacco — the official EU-standard consumer-price measure, produced by Eurostat. When prices rise, the bond pays more. When the bond matures in April 2033, the redemption amount equals the original face value multiplied by however much the price level has risen since the bond was issued in 2021. The stated coupon is 0.10% — tiny in nominal terms — but that 0.10% is applied to the inflation-adjusted principal, so the effective payment grows with prices.

Settlement of this bond is not straightforward. A buyer pays the dirty price, which includes accrued interest, applied to the inflation-adjusted face value. As of 2026-07-15, the quoted clean price is €94.92 per €100 original face value. However, a buyer pays the dirty price of €119.99 per €100 original face value at settlement — not €94.92. The €94.92 is the quoted clean price in real terms (the same purchasing-power units as the issuance date). To get the actual euros that change hands, multiply by the index ratio (1.26369), then add accrued interest. The bond has been issued in a volume of €10.65 billion and is traded freely on the European sovereign-bond market. The Federal Republic of Germany (the issuer) has never defaulted on a euro-denominated obligation.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is structural, not tactical: this bond is held as an inflation hedge sized to a specific portfolio allocation. The logic is that a German government bond linked to consumer prices eliminates the main risk that erodes the real value of a conventional bond — unexpected inflation — while carrying essentially no credit risk. Three numbered exit conditions define when the hedge ceases to do its job. Each must be assessed every quarter:

  1. Real yield falls persistently below −1.0% while EUR cash offers a better real return. This is the valuation exit: a bond trading at a price implying a real yield below −1.0% is priced so expensively that its holder is guaranteed a real loss. The trigger requires both legs simultaneously — the yield must stay below −1.0% for at least 90 calendar days, and EUR cash or nominal German government bonds must already offer a superior after-tax real return. A single-quarter dip is not enough; the rule requires persistence.

Latest reading: as of 2026-07-15, the real yield is 0.88% — far above the −1.0% threshold. The compound trigger has been satisfied for zero days. EUR cash (at the ECB deposit rate of 2.25%, less 2.06% break-even inflation) offers a real yield of approximately 0.19%, meaning the linker leads EUR cash by 68.98 basis points. Exit condition (1) is not triggered.

  1. Germany's sovereign credit rating is downgraded below AA− by any two of Fitch, Moody's, and S&P. Credit risk is not the rationale for holding this bond; it is held for the inflation linkage. But if two of the three major agencies simultaneously concluded that Germany had become meaningfully more likely to default, the safety of the principal would be in question and the holding would need to be re-assessed.

Latest reading: as of 2026-06-15, all three agencies rate Germany at their highest level — Fitch AAA, Moody's Aaa, S&P AAA. Exit condition (2) is not triggered.

  1. The HICP ex-tobacco index is materially restructured in a way that severs its link to realised consumer-price inflation. If Eurostat were to redefine the index — for instance, by replacing it with a different reference measure — the inflation-protection function of the bond would be broken even though the coupon payments would continue. This is a low-probability, high-salience scenario; it is monitored rather than expected.

Latest reading: no pending HICP methodology restructuring is recorded as of 2026-06-15. Exit condition (3) is not triggered.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Sustained low real yield. If the European Central Bank cuts rates aggressively while inflation expectations remain anchored, the real yield on EUR cash could fall faster than the linker's yield, narrowing the linker's current advantage. In the bear scenario modelled, the real return over the holding period compresses to approximately −0.1% a year — a small real loss, primarily because the bond's price would need to fall for the yield to move that far.
  • German sovereign downgrade. Two simultaneous below-AA− ratings from the major agencies would fire exit condition (2). This is currently a very low-probability event; Germany's fiscal position is rated the highest available by all three agencies. But it is a binary trigger.
  • HICP methodology change. Eurostat has the authority to redefine the reference index. A substitution that replaced the current HICP ex-tobacco basket with a measure less representative of actual consumer-price experience would undermine the bond's inflation-protection function. This risk is monitored; there is no pending change.
  • Tax-treatment change. German Abgeltungssteuer (withholding tax) applies to both the coupon and the index-accrual gain on German inflation-linked bonds. A change to the tax treatment — for instance, treating the full nominal gain at maturity as ordinary income rather than capital gain — would alter the after-tax real return and could affect the cash-comparison calculation. This risk is not captured in the current model.

The Rules

Six pre-committed rules govern what happens under each combination of conditions and price:

  • If the thesis is confirmed — the real yield is inside its band, German credit is intact, and the HICP linkage is working — the rule is to hold. No action, no rebalancing.
  • If the thesis is weakened — the real yield has moved below 0% but the compound exit trigger has not yet fired — the rule is not to add. Before adding at any price, the real after-tax return should be compared against EUR cash and nominal German government bonds at the same maturity.
  • If the thesis is broken — any one of the three numbered exit conditions has fired — the rule is to exit the full position. Proceeds move to nominal short-duration German government bonds or EUR cash.
  • If the price falls to the buy edge (real yield at or above 1.50%) and no driver challenges the thesis, the rule is to add in proportion to the inflation-hedge allocation target. A 7-year AAA-rated EUR sovereign linker offering a real yield above 1.50% is highly attractive by the standards of recent history.
  • If the price rises to the trim edge (real yield at or below −0.50%), the rule is to trim 25% on first close above the line. A second 25% trim is available at the next quarterly review (at least 90 days later) if the yield has not recovered. No further trimming until exit condition (1) fires.
  • If the compound exit trigger fires — real yield below −1.0% for at least 90 days and EUR cash offering a superior real return simultaneously — the rule is to exit the full position and switch proceeds to EUR cash or short-duration nominal German government bonds.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
