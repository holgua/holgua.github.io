About Booking

Booking Holdings operates the world's largest online accommodation marketplace. Its primary brand, Booking.com, lists approximately 4.5 million properties — hotels, apartments, and short-term rentals — across more than 220 countries. When a traveller books a stay through Booking.com, the company takes a commission (known as the take rate, which measures how much of the total booking value flows through to Booking's revenue) from the property. In the three months ending in March 2026, Booking's take rate was 10.3 cents on every dollar of total bookings processed.

The company also owns Priceline (a second booking platform focused on the United States), Agoda (South-East Asia), KAYAK (a search aggregator that compares prices across travel sites), and OpenTable (restaurant reservations). These brands share technology and data but largely operate independently. The bulk of the economic value sits in Booking.com, which dominates European accommodation bookings and is building in alternative accommodations — apartments, houses, and unique properties that compete with Airbnb.

Revenue in the twelve months ending in December 2025 was $26.9 billion, up 13% on the prior year. The operating profit margin (the share of revenue left after all operating costs) was 32.8%. Free cash flow — the cash the business generates after paying for its physical assets — was $9.1 billion, representing a margin of 33.8 cents for every dollar of revenue. That cash largely finds its way back to shareholders: Booking returned $6.4 billion in share buybacks and $1.2 billion in dividends in 2025.

The Case for the Bet

The single bet is this: Booking.com's two-sided network — the fact that 4.5 million properties need Booking to fill beds, and hundreds of millions of travellers trust Booking to find them — is sticky enough to remain the dominant middleman in global travel even as AI-powered search and hotel-chain direct booking create new competitive pressure. The supporting mechanism is that management compounds per-share value by aggressively buying back shares, so even modest revenue growth translates into meaningfully larger earnings per share over time.

For the bet to keep working, seven conditions must hold:

  1. Room-night growth must not fall to low-single-digit or negative levels across multiple regions for two or more consecutive quarters in the absence of an isolated shock — that would signal the network effect is leaking.
  2. Marketing spend as a percentage of total bookings must not rise meaningfully for two or more consecutive years — that would signal that Booking is having to pay for growth the moat used to generate for free.
  3. The take rate (revenue as a percentage of total bookings) must not compress materially or persistently — that would signal that competitors, regulators, or AI agents are squeezing the economics.
  4. Capital-return discipline must hold — buybacks and dividends must continue while free cash flow remains high, with no large value-destructive acquisitions.
  5. The owner-earnings yield (the company's owner earnings divided by its market value, expressed as a percentage) must not fall below 4% — below that line, the share price is pricing in optimistic compounding that no longer justifies the AI-disintermediation risk.
  6. By the third quarter of 2027, Booking's management must have articulated and visibly executed a coherent AI-first strategy — concrete shipped products with measurable engagement metrics and explicit acknowledgement of disintermediation risk in earnings calls.
  7. EU regulatory enforcement must not result in structural product remedies or fines exceeding 3% of annual global revenue in a single year.

The Rules

The rules governing this position were set in advance. They translate the current state of the thesis and the share price into one of five actions:

  • If all conditions are tracking and no exit condition has fired, the rule is to hold and continue compounding. No action is required until the share price moves.
  • If conditions are deteriorating but no formal exit condition has triggered yet, the rule is to stop adding. Run a fresh validation after the next two quarterly filings before deciding — one quarter of evidence is not enough to confirm a trend.
  • If a formal exit condition fires, the rule is to exit the full position within thirty days. No averaging down. The exit condition is recorded for future learning.
  • If the share price falls to or below $156 — the cheap edge — and the thesis conditions are still intact, the rule is to add to the position in tranches over one to two quarters.
  • If the share price rises to or above $273 — the trim edge — the rule is to trim 25 to 33% of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding. The remaining position is re-evaluated after the trim.

There is also a full-exit rule: if the share price reaches $437 — the exit edge — the rules call for closing the position entirely. At that price, the market is pricing in roughly seven years of base-case compounding instantly, and the implied forward return falls below the long-term equity premium. Proceeds are redeployed at the next cheap-edge crossing or into a cheaper alternative, with no re-entry for ninety days to remove anchor bias.

The Conditions Today

Room-night growth came in at +5.9% for the three months ending in March 2026, against 319 million nights in the same period of 2025. The company estimates the Middle East conflict shaved approximately two percentage points off that growth rate; excluding the conflict, the implied figure is roughly +8%, consistent with the full-year 2025 rate. The exit condition requires low-single-digit or negative growth "across multiple regions for two or more consecutive quarters absent an isolated shock" — this quarter does not approach that threshold. March specifically, when the conflict began to affect bookings most sharply, showed only +1% growth, which is the number to watch at the next filing.

Gross bookings — the total value of all travel reservations processed, before commissions — reached $53.8 billion in the quarter, up 15.2% on the prior year in reported terms and roughly +8% on a constant-currency basis. This is a healthy reading. The mix is shifting: Booking.com processes an increasing share of bookings as the merchant of record (accepting payment from the traveller and remitting to the property) rather than as a pure intermediary. The merchant mix was 72% in the quarter, up from 67% a year earlier.

The take rate edged up to 10.3% of total bookings from 10.2% in the same quarter of 2025. The filing attributes the improvement to growing payment-facilitation revenues and favourable timing effects. The take rate is not compressing — exit condition (3) is not in view.

Marketing efficiency held flat at 3.8% of total gross bookings, the same as the prior year. The filing notes that, absent the Middle East conflict, modest marketing leverage was actually achieved — conflict-related cancellations ate into the returns on search bids that had already been placed. The structural watch item here is search-engine optimisation (the practice of appearing in unpaid search results): Booking's filing explicitly states that search-engine-driven traffic is expected to decline in the short to medium term, which could push more spend into paid channels. This has not yet moved the marketing-efficiency number, but it is the early-stage version of what disintermediation looks like in the cost line.

Alternative accommodations — apartments, houses, and short-term rentals that compete directly with Airbnb's offering — now make up 38% of total room nights booked on Booking.com, up from 37% in the prior year. The inventory has grown to 4.0 million properties of this type, out of 4.5 million total. The long-term shift away from hotel-only inventory continues.

AI strategy is where the clearest watch clock is running. Booking frames its approach as the "Connected Trip" — an AI-powered traveller experience that offers suggestions and seamless booking across accommodation, flights, attractions, and ground transport. Mobile-app bookings now account for a high-fifties percentage of room nights (up from mid-fifties a year earlier), which is a proxy for how many customers are engaging with Booking's own platform rather than arriving through search engines or metasearch. But the filing discloses no specific product metrics — no data on how many users engage with AI-powered features, no measurable engagement uplift, no direct-traffic figures. Exit condition (6) sets a deadline of the third quarter of 2027 for a coherent, demonstrably executed AI-first strategy. Eighteen months remain on that clock.

Capital return was exceptional. In the three months ending in March 2026, Booking returned $4.0 billion in share buybacks (reducing the share count by approximately 22 million shares) and paid $343 million in dividends — $4.4 billion combined, on operating cash flow of $3.2 billion. The buyback was funded in part by drawing on the balance sheet, which management judged appropriate given prices it considers attractive. The $20 billion buyback authorisation announced in 2025 still had $18.2 billion remaining. Exit condition (4) is clearly not triggered.

Forward demand commentary is cautious. Booking offered no formal guidance for the second quarter of 2026. The filing notes that the revenue impact of Middle East conflict cancellations "will not be fully realised until future quarters" because Booking recognises revenue at the time of travel rather than at the time of booking. No positive forward-booking commentary — on summer demand, for instance — appears in the filing.

Revenue growth was +16.2% for the quarter, including approximately six percentage points of benefit from favourable currency movements. On a constant-currency basis, the quarter grew roughly 10%. Full-year 2025 revenue grew 13.4% on the prior year. Exit condition (5) — the owner-earnings yield falling below 4% — is far from being triggered: the current yield of 6.4% sits well above the 4% floor and comfortably below the 7% buy threshold.

The Share Price

At $170, the share price sits between the $156 cheap edge and the $199 fair-value anchor. In plain terms: the market is not offering a large discount to what the business looks to be worth at a 5.5% owner-earnings yield (the yield Booking has historically averaged), but it is offering a modest one.

The owner-earnings yield at $170 is 6.4% — above the 5.5% fair-value centre and meaningfully above the 4% trim threshold, but short of the 7% buy trigger. The position is inside the band rather than at an edge. Put differently, the price is fair-to-slightly-attractive but not cheap by the rules' definition of cheap.

There is one complication worth noting. A separate valuation model — based on discounted scenario analysis rather than the yield-band approach — puts the base-case fair value near $133, implying a yield of approximately 8.2%. The divergence between that figure and the 5.5% fair-value used here is wide. This does not affect the band framework — the current yield of 6.4% is within the 4%–7% operating band regardless of which fair-value anchor is used — but it does suggest that the fair-value setting warrants a review before the next add or trim decision.

What's Wobbling

The Middle East conflict and near-term room-night growth. March room-night growth was +1% — a sharp deceleration from the quarterly rate of +6%. The filing explicitly warns that the revenue impact of reduced bookings will carry into future quarters because revenue is recognised at travel time, not booking time. Exit condition (1) requires low-single-digit or negative growth across multiple regions for two or more consecutive quarters in the absence of an isolated shock. One conflict-impacted quarter does not trip that condition. But if the second quarter of 2026 also shows low-single-digit room-night growth — whether from conflict spillover, macro softness, or a combination — it will approach the boundary. The next filing is the most important data point in the current cycle.

Structural search-engine decline. Booking's own filing states that search-engine-driven traffic is expected to decline in the short to medium term, potentially pushing more spend into paid channels. The marketing efficiency number has not yet moved — 3.8% of gross bookings, flat year-on-year — but this is the mechanism by which AI-powered search and Google's growing direct-travel-search presence would first show up in Booking's cost line. Exit condition (2) requires a meaningful rise for two or more consecutive years; the trajectory to watch is whether 3.8% holds in future quarters or begins to drift upward.

The AI-strategy clock. Booking talks about the "Connected Trip" as its strategic answer to the AI era, and mobile-app mix is growing, which is a positive proxy indicator. But the filing discloses no concrete shipped-product metrics. Exit condition (6) is clear: by the third quarter of 2027, management must have articulated and visibly executed a coherent AI-first strategy with measurable engagement, direct-traffic, and margin uplift — and acknowledged disintermediation risk explicitly. Eighteen months remain. The risk is not that the deadline fires imminently; it is that the window for demonstrating a real answer is narrowing.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • AI agents disintermediate the booking funnel. Google Gemini, large-language-model assistants, and specialised travel agents could become the default planning surface for travellers, routing bookings directly to hotel chains or to competing platforms that integrate more deeply with AI. Booking currently consumes roughly 30% of its revenue on marketing, much of it in paid search; if that funnel collapses, the economics of the business change materially.
  • A second consecutive quarter of low room-night growth. If conflict spillover or a broader macro softening drives another quarter of low-single-digit room-night growth across multiple regions, exit condition (1) begins to apply. One quarter is a signal; two consecutive quarters meeting the threshold with no isolated explanation would be a formal trigger.
  • Regulatory enforcement biting deeper than compliance cost. The EU's Digital Markets Act and Digital Services Act impose ongoing obligations on large platforms like Booking. A formal structural remedy — mandatory removal of parity clauses, for instance, which prevent hotels from offering cheaper rates elsewhere — or a fine exceeding 3% of annual revenue would cross into economic impairment territory. The regulatory file has been an ongoing cost, not a structural threat, so far.
  • Marketing efficiency deterioration. If search-engine-driven traffic declines as the filing warns, and if Booking is unable to replace it with direct-app traffic fast enough, marketing spend as a share of bookings will rise. Two or more consecutive years of meaningful increase would trigger exit condition (2).
  • A large, value-destructive acquisition. Booking has a track record of disciplined capital allocation, but the $20 billion buyback programme creates an implicit alternative. If management redirects significant capital into a large acquisition — particularly in a new vertical where the moat logic does not apply — exit condition (4) would be worth re-evaluating.

Gaps in the Picture

The main CapEx figure used in the owner-earnings calculation (free cash flow = operating cash flow minus total capital expenditure) does not separate maintenance capital expenditure from growth capital expenditure. The filing's management discussion does not provide this breakdown explicitly. If a material portion of the $322 million in capital expenditure in 2025 is growth-oriented rather than maintenance, the true owner-earnings figure — and therefore the owner-earnings yield — would be somewhat higher than the 6.4% used here. This is a known limitation, not a data error, but it means the yield figure may be modestly conservative.

The share count used in the valuation calculation — 774.8 million diluted shares — is market-implied rather than taken directly from the company's official filings. The most recent filing reports 32.6 million diluted shares, which is approximately 23.7 times lower. The discrepancy is almost certainly explained by a 25-for-1 stock split that took effect on 2026-04-02. Because the owner-earnings yield is the ratio of earnings to market capitalisation (itself derived from price times shares), the yield figure is not affected by which share count is used as long as both the price and the share count reflect the post-split units. This has been cross-checked and the owner-earnings yield of 6.4% is internally consistent.

A separate discounted-scenario valuation model puts the base-case fair value near $133 per share, implying an owner-earnings yield of approximately 8.2% at fair value rather than the 5.5% used in the band framework. The two approaches use different assumptions and are not directly reconcilable without examining both models in detail. This divergence is flagged as a data-quality note; it does not affect the band-framework position (which is determined by the live owner-earnings yield of 6.4% relative to the configured thresholds), but it does suggest reviewing the fair-value setting before the next add or trim decision.
