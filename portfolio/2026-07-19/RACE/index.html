About Ferrari

Ferrari makes its money by selling fewer cars than its customers want to buy. The company manufactures approximately 13,500–14,000 vehicles a year from its plant in Maranello, Italy — a number held deliberately flat by the company itself, not imposed by any capacity constraint. The result is that buyers must request an allocation years in advance, that no Ferrari is ever discounted, and that the company's average revenue per car — roughly EUR 524,000 in 2025 — keeps climbing. Profit margins — measured as the share of each sales euro retained after direct costs and overhead but before interest and tax — have been above 38% for three consecutive years, a level matched by almost no manufacturing business on earth.

The model has three interlocking pieces. First, the brand: the prancing horse emblem, seven decades of Formula 1 competition, and a mythology that accumulates rather than depreciates over time. Second, the scarcity architecture: production caps that guarantee excess demand, meaning buyers negotiate upward rather than downward, and the resale market consistently prices pre-owned Ferraris above their original purchase price. Third, the customer loyalty staircase: access to Ferrari's most exclusive and most valuable limited-edition cars is gated by a documented history of prior ownership, so leaving the franchise means losing one's place in the queue — a switching cost with no equivalent elsewhere. These three elements reinforce one another in a way that competitors cannot replicate simply by spending money. The business has grown revenue from EUR 4.3 billion in 2021 to EUR 7.1 billion in 2025, entirely without expanding production volumes in any meaningful way.

The Case for the Bet

The core wager is that Ferrari's production-cap discipline, brand heritage, and customer-staircase architecture constitute a durable economic moat that translates into predictable, compounding earnings power for many years ahead. That wager rests on six testable conditions:

  1. Annual production held below 16,000 units. The scarcity premium that drives Ferrari's pricing power and multi-year order backlogs depends entirely on permanent excess demand. Any management decision to grow volumes above 16,000 units in a single year — whether in guidance or in delivered figures — would signal that the strategy has shifted, which would be the single most destructive event for the thesis.

Latest reading: 13,640 units delivered in the twelve months ending 2025-12-31. This is below the 14,500-unit thesis-discipline threshold and well clear of the 16,000-unit moat-breaking line. Volume was broadly flat from the prior year's 13,752 units, consistent with the production-cap architecture remaining intact.

  1. Operating profit margin held above 35%. A full-year collapse below this level would signal either that the scarcity premium has been compromised or that development costs for the Elettrica electric car have permanently compressed the cost structure.

Latest reading: Operating profit margin confirmed at 38.8% for the twelve months ending 2025-12-31, with operating income growing 11.76% year on year. Ferrari's 2026 guidance calls for an operating profit margin at or above 39%, implying further expansion. The exit condition is not triggered.

  1. Operating cash flow at least equal to net profit in any given year. This ratio (operating cash flow divided by net profit) is a straightforward test of whether reported earnings are backed by real cash generation. A reading below 1.0 for any single year would prompt an accounting review, as it would be inconsistent with every year of the five-year data record available from the annual filings.

Latest reading: 1.47 times for the twelve months ending 2025-12-31 — operating cash flow of EUR 2,349 million against net profit of EUR 1,597 million. This figure is confirmed directly from the filing's structured financial data. The exit condition is not triggered.

  1. Average selling price per car not declining on standard models for two consecutive years. A sustained decline in the price Ferrari achieves per car — derived by dividing total revenue by the number of vehicles delivered — would signal that demand has softened enough to affect the standard lineup, not merely the timing of limited-series model deliveries.

Latest reading: Implied average selling price approximately EUR 524,000 on 13,640 deliveries in the twelve months ending 2025-12-31, up from roughly EUR 487,000 in the prior year. Pricing is increasing, not declining. The exit condition is not triggered.

  1. US tariff rate does not return to 25% while Ferrari simultaneously guides operating margin below 38%. Both conditions must fire together. The tariff level is the external event; the margin guidance is the test of whether Ferrari's pricing power is sufficient to absorb it. Either condition alone does not trigger an exit.

Latest reading: US tariff on European car imports stood at 15% as of the August 2025 EU-US trade deal. Ferrari's 2026 guidance of at least 39% operating profit margin implies no margin cut attributable to tariff absorption at the current rate. A potential re-escalation to 25% has been threatened by the US administration as of mid-2026 but is unconfirmed. The two-part exit condition is not triggered.

  1. Elettrica order book oversubscribes within 12 months of opening. If the first Ferrari electric car requires price concessions, delivery deferrals, or extended booking windows to fill its order book, that would suggest BEV electrification requires pricing compromise — making the Elettrica a margin headwind rather than a margin tailwind.

Latest reading: The Elettrica was revealed in October 2025 with performance specifications confirmed (over 1,000 hp, sub-2.5-second 0-100 km/h, target price above EUR 500,000). A full reveal is planned for the middle of 2026; the order book has not yet opened as of 2026-07-19. The 12-month clock for this exit condition has not yet started.

What's Wobbling

Four silent drivers. The most prominent wobble is not a deterioration in the business — it is a limitation of the data source. Ferrari files its annual accounts in a European digital format that is designed to carry structured financial figures but not the full narrative text of the report in machine-readable form. Four of the seven conditions rely on narrative extraction (production volumes, the Elettrica order book, the tariff picture, and governance signals), and for this cycle the automated extraction failed. This is a data-source limitation, not an adverse thesis signal. Production volumes were sourced through separate analysis (13,640 units — condition intact); the tariff picture was sourced through political-exposure analysis (15% rate, guidance intact). The Elettrica order book and governance confirmation remain unverified from this run.

US tariff re-escalation threat. As of mid-2026, the US administration has threatened to return auto tariffs to 25%. This is the more substantive wobble. At 25%, the estimated cost impact is roughly $95,600 per vehicle destined for the US market (approximately 3,400 vehicles a year). Ferrari's pricing power is demonstrated — it raised prices by up to 10% during the 25% tariff period in early 2025 with no disclosed demand destruction — but exit condition 5 requires both the 25% tariff and a below-38% margin guidance, and neither has yet fired. The wobble is real but has not crossed any of the lines defined in The Case for the Bet.

Elettrica order book clock not yet started. The Elettrica is on track per the competitive analysis: revealed October 2025, full reveal mid-2026, target price above EUR 500,000. But the order book has not opened, which means exit condition 6 cannot yet be assessed. The 12-month clock for that condition starts when orders formally open. This is a pending item, not a wobble — but it is the single open monitoring item before the next annual cycle.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Multiple compression without any fundamental deterioration. Ferrari almost always trades at a luxury-goods multiple (roughly 37 times trailing earnings). If the market reclassifies the shares as premium automotive rather than luxury goods, the multiple could compress to 20–25 times with no change in earnings, cutting the share price by 30–45%. The business would be unimpaired; the return would not.
  • Luxury macro slowdown meets production-cap pressure. A deep, sustained contraction in high-net-worth wealth — primarily driven by equity market falls in the US and China — could soften demand enough to put the scarcity architecture under pressure. If Ferrari faced a choice between defending volumes at the cost of order backlogs or defending backlogs at the cost of headline deliveries, either path would create investor concern.
  • Elettrica launch friction. The first full-electric Ferrari carries a target price above EUR 500,000 and development costs that are already embedded in the capital expenditure trend. If the order book at launch is underwhelming, or if early deliveries require price adjustments, the bull-case assumption that electrification extends rather than erodes the brand premium would be challenged.
  • US tariff re-escalation to 25% without immediate offset. Ferrari demonstrated during early 2025 that selective price increases can absorb a 25% tariff rate. But if the re-escalation arrives in a period of concurrent luxury softness — when demand elasticity is higher — the pass-through thesis would face its first real test at full scale.
  • CATL supply-chain disruption. The Elettrica uses batteries supplied by the Chinese manufacturer CATL. US export-control restrictions on CATL or its products would complicate the Elettrica launch timeline, potentially delaying the single most important growth event in the near-term roadmap.

The Rules

The pre-committed rules govern what action follows from each combination of thesis state and share price. They are stated here as conditions, not recommendations — the decision remains a personal one.

  • If all measurable conditions confirm the thesis and no exit condition has been triggered, then hold. No action is needed. The moat is intact; compounding continues.
  • If any driver shows the thesis weakening — but no exit condition has fired — then do not add to the position. Monitor the specific weakening driver, likely the operating margin trend or the Elettrica uptake, for one full year before reassessing position size.
  • If any single exit condition fires, then exit the full position within 30 days. Exit condition 1 (production above 16,000 units) is irreversible — it destroys the scarcity moat permanently. No averaging down on a broken thesis. Document the triggered condition.
  • If the share price falls to or below $244 (owner earnings yield at or above 4.25%) and the thesis is confirmed intact, then add in tranches over one to two quarters. A buy-band entry into this franchise is rare and should be acted on decisively.
  • If the share price rises to or above $829 (owner earnings yield at or below 1.25%), then trim 25–33% of the position to harvest the premium. At that price the market is discounting decades of compounding; disciplined reduction preserves optionality to re-enter at a better yield.
  • If the share price rises to the point where the earnings yield falls below 1.0% (exit territory), then exit the full position. At that level the market is pricing years of base-case compounding instantly — it is bubble territory for a single equity. No re-entry within 90 days, to defuse anchor bias.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
