About Elastic

Elastic N.V. is the company behind Elasticsearch — the world's most widely downloaded open-source search database. It sells subscriptions that let enterprises run Elasticsearch as a managed cloud service, and it has extended the platform into three related problem areas: enterprise search (finding information inside a company's own data), observability (watching whether software systems are running correctly and diagnosing failures), and security (detecting threats and managing incidents). The three functions share a single underlying data layer, which means a company that starts with one typically ends up using the others.

The business model is recurring-revenue software: customers pay for subscriptions, expand their usage as their data volumes grow, and pay more over time without Elastic needing to sell to them again. The 112% customer expansion rate in the April 2026 filing — meaning existing customers collectively spent 12% more than they did the previous year — is the most direct evidence that the model is working. Revenue reached $1.74 billion in the year ended April 2026, up 17% from the prior year, with the cloud-delivered portion representing 48% of the total and growing at 22%.

The underlying technology is expensive to replace. Once a company's IT operations team has built its data pipelines, search interfaces, and security dashboards on Elasticsearch, migrating to a competitor requires re-architecting those systems from the ground up — a disruptive, months-long project that most IT departments prefer to avoid. That stickiness is the central economic fact behind the thesis.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that Elastic's three-segment platform, built on a single data layer that customers embed deeply into their IT operations, generates durable and expanding cash flows as cloud adoption grows and as artificial-intelligence workloads create new demand for the kind of fast, flexible data retrieval that Elasticsearch provides:

  1. Customer expansion stays above 105%. The net expansion rate — the ratio of current-year spending by last year's customer base to last year's spending by that same base — is the most direct measure of switching-cost power. If customers were churning or cutting back, this rate would fall below 100%. A rate persistently above 105% for two consecutive quarters is the minimum signal that the moat remains intact; a rate falling below that threshold for two consecutive quarters is exit condition one.

Latest reading: 112% as of April 2026. Unchanged from the prior year. Customer count at the top end — those spending more than one million dollars annually — grew from 200 to over 240. The moat signal is holding.

  1. Cloud revenue grows above 10% annually. The shift from self-managed software to cloud-delivered subscriptions is the revenue compounding engine. If cloud growth decelerates below 10% for a full fiscal year, it would confirm that Amazon's competing open-source fork is successfully capturing the market at the cloud layer — exit condition two.

Latest reading: Total cloud revenue grew 22% in the year ended April 2026, reaching $837 million. The annual cloud subscription tier within that grew 28%. Both are well above the 10% floor and the 15% watch threshold. The forward pipeline, measured as contracted revenue expected within the next twelve months, grew 20%, providing visibility a full year ahead.

  1. Cash generation stays above 14% of revenue. Cash generated from operations minus equipment purchases — the operative cash figure — must stay above 14% of revenue on a full-year basis to confirm the business is genuinely capital-light and that margins are expanding as expected. A drop below 14% without a one-off explanation is exit condition three.

Latest reading: 18.5% cash margin in the year ended April 2026 ($321.8 million on $1,739 million revenue). This is 450 basis points above the 14% floor. Management has guided to approximately 21.5% for the following year, which, if achieved, would mark the third consecutive year of margin expansion.

  1. Staff equity awards stay below 18% of revenue for a second consecutive year. Equity awards to employees are a real economic cost even though they do not appear in the cash flow statement. If those awards exceed 18% of revenue for two consecutive fiscal years, it would signal that compensation discipline has reversed and the business's cash yield is overstated — exit condition four.

Latest reading: Pure equity awards were 17.16% of revenue in the year ended April 2026, down from 17.38% the prior year. Two consecutive years below the threshold means exit condition four cannot be triggered for this filing period regardless of interpretation. The trajectory is improving.

  1. No acquisition above $500 million at more than eight times revenue without a cash-return commitment. Large acquisitions at inflated prices without a clear path to recovering the investment in cash terms are a common way for technology companies to destroy value. The rule requires any such acquisition to come with a disclosed expectation that it will generate net cash returns within three years, with both the price ceiling and the timeline making the test observable from public filings — exit condition five.

Latest reading: Two acquisitions in the year ended April 2026: an artificial-intelligence embedding company for $43 million and a data-workflow tool for $11 million. Both are well below the $500 million threshold.

  1. GAAP operating profit must be positive by the year ending April 2028. The business currently reports a small accounting loss after deducting equity awards as an expense. The trajectory — from a $130 million loss in 2024 to a $55 million loss in 2025 to a $34 million loss in 2026 — puts the 2028 positive-margin target within range. Failure to reach break-even by then would call the profitability convergence story into question — exit condition six.

Latest reading: Operating loss of $33.5 million in the year ended April 2026, a 39% improvement from the prior year. The trajectory remains on track for the 2028 target.

  1. Datadog's revenue growth must not exceed Elastic's by more than 15 percentage points for two consecutive fiscal years while Elastic simultaneously loses its leading position in the Gartner observability ranking. Datadog is the fastest-growing competitor in the observability segment; if both the revenue growth gap and the ranking deterioration materialise together, it would signal structural competitive displacement — exit condition seven.

Latest reading: The annual filing does not disclose Datadog's revenue growth or Elastic's Gartner ranking. Both data points require external sources and are monitored at each quarterly update. No adverse signal has been confirmed as of the most recent review.

The Share Price

At $59, the share price sits in the lower half of the hold band — above the buy threshold but well short of the trim threshold — and deeply below the fair-value central estimate of $101. Put plainly, the market is pricing the business as if its cash generation will grow at roughly 6% a year over the long run; the base-case assumption embedded in the Rules is 15%. That gap is discussed in Why the Market Sees It Differently.

No exit band has been set as a numerical price threshold. The exit rule operates at a yield level — if the annual cash yield falls below 1.50% of the market value (which corresponds to a price of approximately $200), the Rules call for closing the position.

Why the Market Sees It Differently

The market prices Elastic at $59 against a fair-value estimate of $101 — a gap of 41%. The price says the business will earn less over time. The fair-value estimate says the opposite.

Concretely: at $59, the market implies that Elastic's cash generation will compound at roughly 5.8% a year over the long run. The estimate embedded in the Rules assumes 15%. The spread between those two numbers — 9.2 percentage points — is the core of the disagreement.

Three explanations could each, on their own, account for the market's more cautious view. First, cloud consumption growth could stall toward single digits as enterprises route artificial-intelligence spending directly to the cloud platforms — Amazon, Microsoft, Google — that offer native search and data-retrieval tools bundled into their infrastructure charges. The filing data as of April 2026 refutes this: cloud growth ran at 22%, customer expansion held at 112%, and the forward contract pipeline grew at 20%. The market may be pricing a deceleration risk rather than current-period stall.

Second, cash margins could compress rather than expand — if the business needs to sustain heavy spending on sales, product development, and customer support to defend its market position, the operating leverage story would not materialise. The April 2026 data also refutes this: cash margin expanded to 18.5% from 17.7% the prior year, and gross margin — the portion of each sales dollar left after delivering the service — reached 76%.

The third explanation has partial support: the $340 million buyback programme executed in the year ended April 2026 at an average price of $77 per share — above the current market price of $59 and well above the bear-case scenario value of $38 — represents a meaningful capital-allocation misjudgement. That is a real concern; at the same time, it does not bear on the underlying business's earning power and does not affect the exit conditions.

On balance, the current price appears to overshoot the downside case on the two most important hypotheses. The capital-allocation concern warrants keeping any additions staged rather than concentrated.

What's Wobbling

The framework records two warnings from the annual filing, neither of which changes the hold verdict but both of which deserve monitoring.

The first is a technical gap between the fair-value anchor built into the Rules (3.04% yield, corresponding to $101 per share) and the current market yield at today's price (5.17%). The Rules use a scenario-based cash-flow model anchored to a 15% base-case growth assumption, not to trailing-yield history. The divergence is real and deliberate: it reflects the market pricing a materially slower growth trajectory than the base case assumes. Before adding meaningfully to the position, it is worth confirming that the 15% base-case growth assumption is still defensible in light of the most recent competitive data.

The second warning is a similar gap between the rule-based fair value ($101) and the peer-multiple model's output ($241). The peer-multiple model uses a different methodology — it calibrates against what comparable companies trade at — and produces a materially higher number. Both models are intentionally different, and neither alone should anchor a buy or trim decision. The divergence does not change the hold verb but it does mean there is genuine model uncertainty about the fair-value level.

The Datadog competitive watch — exit condition seven — remains unresolvable from the annual filing and requires quarterly tracking of Datadog's quarterly revenue filings and the annual Gartner observability ranking.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • AWS closes the functionality gap. Amazon's open-source Elasticsearch fork now has over 1,400 contributors and is available as a managed cloud service. If it achieves genuine feature parity — not just on the core search functions but on the security and observability extensions — it could arrest new enterprise wins and accelerate existing customer churn. This is the single most important risk: the bear-case scenario has a 25% probability and implies a loss of nearly half the position value from today's price.
  • Enterprise AI spending consolidates on hyperscaler-native tools. The bull case assumes that Elastic becomes the default data-retrieval layer for enterprise artificial-intelligence workloads. If enterprises instead route those workloads to Amazon's, Microsoft's, or Google's native retrieval services as a bundle with their broader cloud contracts, the incremental demand that underpins the bull case does not materialise.
  • Datadog's observability scale advantage widens. Datadog grew revenue at 28% in the most recent period against Elastic's 17%. If this growth-rate gap persists and Elastic's Gartner ranking deteriorates, exit condition seven triggers — requiring full exit within thirty days.
  • The second chief financial officer transition in eighteen months introduces execution risk. The current finance head joined in early 2025, replacing a CFO who departed after less than two years. Financial leadership changes at growth-stage companies can slow guidance discipline and investor communication.
  • The FY2026 $370 million tax benefit does not recur. Net income in the year ended April 2026 was $368 million — essentially all of it a non-cash accounting benefit from recognising deferred tax assets. Cash generation ($322 million) is the operative figure; it is genuine. But investors anchoring to GAAP net income may draw misleading conclusions about the earnings trend.

The discrepancy is deliberate — the two methodologies are calibrated differently — but it creates genuine uncertainty about what fair value actually is. Until the two models are reconciled, any price-target figure should be treated as a range, not a point.

The Rules

  • If thesis confirmed (all conditions intact, price within the hold band): hold and monitor. Require two consecutive quarters of deterioration before acting — one quarter is insufficient to confirm a structural break.
  • If thesis broken (any written exit condition fires): exit the full position within thirty days. No averaging down on thesis breaks.
  • If price falls below the buy band (cash yield above 5.50%, approximately $56 per share): confirm the thesis is intact, then add in tranches. No more than 50% of the intended addition in a single tranche — the bear-case scenario at $38 is destructive enough to require staged entry.
  • If price rises above the trim band (cash yield below 2.20%, approximately $140 per share): trim 25% of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.
  • If the cash yield falls below 1.50% (approximately $200 per share): exit the entire remaining position. At that price the market is pricing in the bull-case trajectory and the asymmetry has inverted. No re-entry within ninety days to defuse the pull back toward a recent entry price.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
