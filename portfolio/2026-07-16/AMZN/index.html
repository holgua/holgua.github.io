Amazon: The Bet Still Works

The Bottom Line

Data as of 2026-07-15 · Latest filing 2026-04-30.

The bet is working. AWS, the cloud-infrastructure business at Amazon's centre of gravity, grew 28% in the three months ending in March 2026, well above the level the thesis requires to stay intact — and no written exit condition has fired. The Share Price section shows the current reading sits in the upper half of the hold band, well above fair value: at $250, the market is pricing in several years of compounding growth that the thesis itself has not yet confirmed. The long-run expected return at today's price — a combination of the current earnings yield plus the assumed pace of business compounding — works out to a healthy equity premium over the risk-free rate, which is the right ballpark for a high-quality business held at a premium. The one condition most worth watching is whether AWS's operating margin, which fell roughly 170 basis points in the latest quarter due to infrastructure investment, stays well above the 28% floor defined in the thesis, and whether the extraordinary capital spending underway — more than $43 billion in a single quarter — begins to convert into revenue on schedule.

Recent Development

The latest quarterly report, covering the three months ending in March 2026, confirmed the two engines at the heart of the bet. Amazon Web Services (AWS, the company's cloud-computing division) grew 28% compared with the same period a year earlier, reaching $37.6 billion in revenue — well above the 18–20% rate the thesis requires and remote from the 15% two-quarter threshold that would trigger a formal exit review. Advertising services reached $17.2 billion, up 24% year on year, and the International segment turned in an operating profit margin of 3.6%, expanding from 3.0% a year earlier. Consolidated revenue came in at $181.5 billion, up 17% year on year, with guidance for the following quarter pointing to 16–19% growth.

The counterpoint to this strength is the scale of investment now underway. Capital spending for the quarter was $43.2 billion — more than double the level of a year earlier — with Amazon's cloud infrastructure alone accounting for $41.5 billion of net additions to property and equipment in a single quarter. When measured against the trailing twelve months, operating cash flow of $148.5 billion was essentially consumed by capital investment, leaving reported free cash flow of just $1.2 billion. The thesis acknowledges this directly: the capital-spending cycle is classified as a monitoring item, not an exit trigger, because the exit condition for capital spending measures annual totals in 2027 and 2028, not 2026. As of the latest filing, no exit condition has been reached.

The full picture — strong AWS and advertising growth alongside extraordinary capital absorption — is described in The Standing Orders and its implications for the share price are addressed in The Share Price.

About Amazon

Amazon is three businesses operating inside one legal entity. The first and largest is a retail and logistics platform serving customers across North America and internationally: a marketplace where third-party merchants sell goods alongside Amazon's own retail operation, all supported by a fulfilment network of roughly 685 million square feet of warehouse and delivery infrastructure. This business generated $588.2 billion in revenue in the year ending December 2025, but its profit margins are comparatively thin — the North America segment operated at 6.9% and the International segment at 2.9%.

The second business is Amazon Web Services, the company's cloud-computing division, which rents computing capacity, storage, database software, and an expanding set of artificial-intelligence services to businesses and governments worldwide. AWS generated $128.7 billion in revenue in 2025 at a 35.4% operating profit margin — making it responsible for 57% of consolidated operating income on 18% of consolidated revenue. The third business is digital advertising: Amazon sells sponsored listings and display placements to sellers and brands within its retail properties and on its video streaming platform. Advertising generated $68.6 billion in 2025 and is growing at around 22% a year. It is the newest of the three compounding engines and the one whose unit economics are still furthest below maturity.

The Case for the Bet

The central argument is that AWS and advertising are compounding profit pools whose unit economics are still maturing — and that the retail and logistics platform, despite thin margins, generates the first-party customer data that makes both pools more valuable. The specific conditions that must keep holding for this argument to work are:

  1. AWS revenue growth stays above 15% on an annualised basis. The thesis posits that the large capital-spending programme underway in 2025 and 2026 will be validated by ongoing revenue acceleration driven by enterprise artificial-intelligence adoption. A sustained deceleration below 15% over two consecutive quarters would signal that the capacity is not converting to demand.

Latest reading: +28% year on year in the three months ending 2026-03-31. AWS reached $37.6 billion in quarterly revenue, with a $364 billion contracted backlog and a newly expanded commitment from OpenAI worth $100 billion over eight years. The exit threshold of 15% is not in view.

  1. AWS segment operating profit margin stays above 28%. A compression below 28% over two consecutive quarters — absent an identifiable one-off charge — would indicate that the reinvestment economics have broken: the company is spending more than the cloud division's incremental returns can justify.

Latest reading: 37.7% in the three months ending 2026-03-31, down from 39.4% in the same quarter a year earlier — a compression of roughly 170 basis points driven by infrastructure investment, not pricing failure. The margin is 970 basis points above the exit floor. One quarter of compression under the thesis rules is not actionable.

  1. Advertising revenue growth stays above single-digit rates on an annualised basis. This condition monitors the third compounding engine; a sustained move into single-digit growth over two consecutive years would signal that the advertising business has saturated or lost ground to competing platforms.

Latest reading: +23.9% year on year to $17.2 billion in the three months ending 2026-03-31. The exit threshold is remote.

The Share Price

At $250, the share price is well above the fair-value anchor. The thesis uses owner-earnings yield as the valuation measure — this is the ratio of the company's free cash flow, after deducting stock-based compensation paid to employees, to the total market value of the business. At the current price, that yield is 4.2%. The fair-value anchor is 6.0%; the buy edge, the level at which a buy order would be triggered, is 7.5% yield (corresponding to a price of $140). The trim edge, the level at which a trim order would fire, is 4.0% yield (corresponding to $263). The exit edge, at 3.0% yield, corresponds to $421.

At $250, the share price sits between the fair-value anchor ($176) and the trim edge ($263) — in the upper half of the hold band. The market is pricing the business at a 42% premium to the fair-value anchor. In plain terms: if the business kept compounding at the base-case pace and the price were today at $176, the long-run return would approach what the framework considers fair value. At $250, those returns are compressed. The thesis is intact, the price is unattractive for a new position, and existing positions are held on thesis grounds rather than valuation grounds. The reasons the market commands this premium are examined in Why the Market Sees It Differently.

Why the Market Sees It Differently

The price says the business will earn more over time than the fair-value anchor assumes. The cautious view, which anchors fair value at a 6% owner-earnings yield (roughly $176 per share), implies the market is paying 42.4% more than that anchor. That is a large premium, and it demands an explanation.

Three hypotheses describe what the market might be pricing. The first is structural demand acceleration: if AWS can sustain above-25% cloud revenue growth for several more years beyond what the base case assumes — driven by the shift from model training to inference and agentic workloads in enterprise computing — then the long-run earnings power is simply larger than the base model captures. The $364 billion contracted backlog and the $100 billion OpenAI expansion, both disclosed in the latest filing, provide concrete evidence that demand commitments already exceed what a modestly-calibrated base case would project. This hypothesis is supported by the current filing.

The second hypothesis is that the margin compression seen in the latest quarter is transitory: the infrastructure depreciation is temporarily running ahead of the revenue it enables, but margins will re-expand as artificial-intelligence capacity fills. The filing's management commentary frames the compression as deliberate investment in AWS business growth — consistent with the thesis, not a refutation of it. This hypothesis is partially supported.

The third hypothesis is that the market is pricing optionality the earnings model cannot fully capture: Amazon's large stakes in Anthropic and OpenAI, both attached to commercial arrangements that lock in cloud-service revenue regardless of which AI model wins the market, represent a structural position in the infrastructure layer of a fast-growing sector. The $12.3 billion upward revaluation of the Anthropic position in the three months ending March 2026 alone illustrates the scale of the optionality. This hypothesis is supported.

For the cautious fair-value view to catch up to the market price, the base-case growth assumption of 8% would need to be revised modestly upward — the market is effectively pricing 8.9%, a gap of only 0.9 percentage points. That is a narrow spread, and two of the three hypotheses have current-filing support.

What's Wobbling

Four of the nine thesis drivers are reading as neither firmly confirming nor challenging — a state that does not trigger any exit condition but warrants monitoring at each subsequent quarterly report.

The most watched is AWS operating margin. It fell from 39.4% in the three months ending March 2025 to 37.7% in the same period this year, as the depreciation from $41.5 billion in new infrastructure additions in a single quarter began flowing through the income statement. The exit condition requires the margin to fall below 28% over two consecutive quarters — the current level is 970 basis points above that floor, so no action is called for. But the direction bears watching.

The second wobble is capital expenditure trajectory. Annualised spending is running above $150 billion and management has guided for further increases in 2026. The exit condition measures 2027 and 2028 specifically, and monitors whether the spending persists without commensurate AWS revenue acceleration. As of the latest filing, AWS is accelerating, so the exit condition's paired requirement is not met. The clock is running nonetheless, and the next two annual reports will be decisive.

The third is capital return discipline. No shares have been repurchased under the $6.1 billion buyback authorisation outstanding since 2022. Meanwhile, $15 billion has been committed to OpenAI's latest funding round and a further $35 billion pledged. The thesis characterises capital allocation as "acceptable but not excellent" — the OpenAI investment carries a commercial cloud-services arrangement attached, making it strategic rather than purely financial — and no exit condition addresses capital allocation directly.

The fourth is tariff and marketplace exposure. The latest filing explicitly identifies China-based sellers as significant contributors to third-party seller revenue and advertising income, and names tariff policy as a risk. North America operating income nonetheless grew 41% year on year in the quarter, indicating resilience so far. The exit condition monitoring advertising revenue growth into single digits over two years is not approached.

Not one of these readings crosses a written threshold. The recommendation is unchanged. See The Case for the Bet for the specific numbers each exit condition requires.

What Could Still Go Wrong

The bear case, weighted at 25% likelihood in the current analysis, involves multiple things going wrong simultaneously:

  • AWS growth decelerates to the mid-teens as Microsoft Azure and Google Cloud close the gap on artificial-intelligence workload performance, producing excess capacity whose multi-year depreciation compresses operating margins toward 9%.
  • A court-imposed marketplace remedy — not a full structural break-up, but mandatory algorithmic neutrality in seller rankings and restrictions on using first-party data to favour Amazon's own retail products — structurally caps operating leverage in the North America retail business.
  • The revaluation gain on the Anthropic stake, which added $12.3 billion of non-cash income in the latest quarter, reverses partially, creating a reported earnings headwind in 2026 even if underlying operations continue normally.
  • Capital spending does not normalise toward the 13–15% of revenue the base case assumes, and the depreciation wave compresses reported earnings before the revenue it was intended to generate has fully accrued.
  • In aggregate, the bear scenario points to a three-year annualised return of approximately −14.7% from the current price — a 38% nominal capital loss if the bear case materialises, as described in What's Wobbling above.

Gaps in the Picture

Two data-quality caveats apply to the figures underlying this evaluation.

First, owner-earnings figures used for valuation are calculated from officially reported, standardised cash flow from operations minus total reported capital expenditure, then deducted for stock-based compensation. This definition produces an owner-earnings total of $113.3 billion for the year ending December 2025. A narrower definition — the one Amazon's management uses in its own communications — would produce a far lower number, because it deducts finance-lease payments for infrastructure that the standard accounting definition treats separately. The two definitions are not interchangeable, and the one in use here is the more conservative of the two on the revenue side but the more favourable on the capital-spending side. The gap between the two measures is material in the current investment cycle and is noted in The Case for the Bet.

Second, there is a divergence between the fair-value anchor used for the valuation bands (6.0% owner-earnings yield, implying a share price of $176) and the midpoint of the base-case scenario produced by the six-method peer-comparison model ($533 per share). The two numbers come from different methodologies and are not in conflict — the 6.0% yield anchor is a conservative cash-flow-yield measure, while the $533 figure is derived from market multiples applied to projected earnings. They represent different views of what the business is worth, and reconciling them would require updating the yield anchor to reflect the peer-multiple approach, or vice versa. Until that reconciliation is done, the valuation bands use the yield anchor exclusively.

The Rules

The following rules govern what action the pre-committed framework calls for, based on where the thesis and the share price sit at any moment of reading:

  • If the thesis is confirmed — all drivers are reading in line with expectations and no exit condition has fired — hold the position without action. Continue compounding the AWS and advertising flywheel.
  • If the thesis is weakening — one or more drivers are deteriorating but no written exit threshold has been crossed — do not add to the position. Monitor the specific deteriorating driver at each of the next two quarterly filings. Decide after one quarter of data, then confirm with a second.
  • If the thesis is broken — a written exit condition has fired — exit the full position within 30 days. No averaging down. Note which exit condition triggered for future reference. AWS revenue and margin exits require confirmation across two consecutive quarters before action is taken; a court-imposed structural remedy or an unexpected auditor change are immediate exits.
  • If the share price falls to or below the buy edge ($140 yield) and the thesis is confirmed intact — add to the position in tranches over one to two quarters.
  • If the share price rises to or above the trim edge ($263) — trim 25–33% of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding. Re-evaluate the remaining position size after the trim.
  • If the share price rises to or above the exit edge ($421) — exit the full position. The market is pricing in roughly 11 years of base-case compounding instantly; redeploy proceeds at the next cheap-edge crossing or into a cheaper alternative. No re-entry within 90 days.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
