The Bottom Line

Data as of 2026-07-11 · Latest filing 2026-05-04.

The bet is working. The compounding thesis — that Greg Abel and the management culture inherited from Warren Buffett will continue to reinvest retained earnings and insurance float at above-average rates — received fresh support in the most recent quarterly filing, with book value growing through the first quarter of 2026 and the freight-rail business posting its best earnings quarter in recent memory. Recent Development

The Oregon Court of Appeals reversed the first-phase PacifiCorp wildfire verdict in 2026-04, eliminating what had been the most acute near-term risk in the story. PacifiCorp — the Pacific Northwest power utility owned by Berkshire's energy subsidiary — had faced the prospect of substantial wildfire-related damages from plaintiffs. The court's reversal significantly reduces that exposure and, separately, PacifiCorp agreed to sell its Washington state electricity operations to Portland General Electric for $1.9 billion in cash, a transaction expected to close in the first half of 2027.

The freight-rail business (BNSF, the western North America railroad) contributed meaningfully to the improvement: revenues in the three months ending in March 2026 rose 4.8% to $5,994 million, while pre-tax profit jumped 13.5% to $1,820 million, the operating ratio improved by 2.3 percentage points, and no regulatory news emerged from the Surface Transportation Board regarding a potential Union Pacific and Norfolk Southern merger that would, if approved, create a new competitor across BNSF's core territory.

The aggregate picture for the Bottom Line is straightforward: book value grew a further $9.8 billion in the first quarter of 2026 to $727.2 billion, operating earnings rose 6.5% compared with the same period a year earlier, and no exit condition came close to triggering. The one mixed note — GEICO's combined ratio rising from 79.8% to 87.3% — is addressed in What's Wobbling.

About Berkshire

Berkshire Hathaway is an insurance-led holding company that makes money in three interlocking ways. First, its insurance businesses — led by Berkshire's car-insurance operation, GEICO, the specialist reinsurer (Berkshire Hathaway Reinsurance Group), and a primary commercial-lines division — collect premiums before they must pay claims. The money sitting in between, roughly $176.9 billion at the end of March 2026, is called the float. Berkshire uses that float to invest at its own discretion and earns a return on capital that costs it little or nothing, because in recent years underwriting has been profitable: the policyholders, in effect, pay Berkshire to hold their money.

Second, Berkshire owns a collection of wholly-owned operating businesses — most prominently BNSF, one of two western North American freight railroads with a territory that spans 32,500 route miles; Berkshire Hathaway Energy (BHE), a regulated utility operating in the United States, Canada, and the United Kingdom; Precision Castparts, an aerospace-component manufacturer; and dozens of smaller industrials and retailers. These businesses generate substantial cash that is collected at the parent level and redeployed by the management team.

Third, Berkshire holds roughly $298 billion in publicly traded equities (primarily Apple, American Express, Bank of America, and Coca-Cola) alongside a $369 billion cash and Treasury-bill reserve. The cash earns interest at current short-term rates while remaining available for opportunistic deployment. The whole enterprise is overseen by Greg Abel, who became chief executive on 2026-01-01, with Warren Buffett continuing as chairman.

The Case for the Bet

The central bet is that the management team will continue to reinvest retained earnings and the insurance float into high-returning businesses and securities at rates exceeding what passive alternatives would deliver. Four written conditions must keep holding for the bet to remain valid:

  1. Book value per share growth must not trail the broad global equity market total return for five or more consecutive years. If it does, that signals the compounding edge has gone — the business is no longer creating value faster than simply indexing. The threshold is specifically five consecutive years, not one bad year or two.

Latest reading: Shareholders' equity grew +10.5% in FY2025 (from $649.4 billion to $717.4 billion) and a further $9.8 billion in the three months ending in March 2026 to $727.2 billion. The per-share book value in Class B terms is approximately $337. The streak of compounding remains intact.

  1. No large acquisition that reveals a lack of capital discipline. Specifically, a transaction exceeding $20 billion at a price above 1.5 times the book value of the target, or one that subsequently requires a goodwill write-down exceeding $5 billion, would trigger this condition.

Latest reading: Abel's first major allocation was the $9.5 billion purchase of OxyChem (the chemical business of Occidental Petroleum), completed in 2026-01. The transaction is below the $20 billion trigger threshold and is described as a cash-flow-generative industrial, not a premium acquisition requiring a moat-based justification. No goodwill impairment has been recorded.

  1. No structural cultural change that abandons the decentralised owner-operator model. This includes initiation of a regular dividend, a break-up of the conglomerate structure, or an activist capitulation that forces a change in capital-allocation philosophy.

Latest reading: Berkshire paid no dividend in the period. No structural changes to the decentralised model were announced. Greg Abel's commentary in the quarterly filing was consistent with continuity.

  1. The ratio of share price to book value must not exceed 2.0 times. Above that level, the valuation fully prices in optimistic compounding and the opportunity cost versus a passive index becomes clearly negative.

Latest reading: The price-to-book ratio stands at approximately 1.52 (using the price of $504 and FY2025 year-end equity; at the updated March 2026 equity of $727.2 billion the ratio is approximately 1.50). Both readings are comfortably below the 2.0 trigger.

The Share Price

The analytical framework values Berkshire by looking at the ratio of share price to book value — how much the market is paying relative to the hard accounting value of the business's assets and retained earnings. Fair value is pegged at 1.45 times book, which reflects the quality of the franchise discounted modestly for the Abel transition. At $504 per share (using FY2025 year-end equity), the ratio stands at approximately 1.52 — placing the price modestly above the 1.45 anchor, which translates to a per-share fair value of $481. In the language of the framework, the share price is at fair value: the gap above the anchor is less than 5%.

The buy edge sits at $415 (a ratio of 1.25 times book), representing the floor at which Buffett historically authorised share repurchases — the market's implicit "intrinsic value" signal. The trim edge sits at $581 (1.75 times book), where the premium begins to price in aggressive compounding that the business may not sustain. The exit edge sits at $647 (approximately 1.95 times book), the level at which the market would be pricing in roughly ten years of base-case compounding in a single step.

What's Wobbling

GEICO's underwriting performance in the three months ending in March 2026 was softer than the same period a year earlier. The combined ratio — the share of premium income consumed by claims and expenses, where a reading below 100% means the business made an underwriting profit — rose to 87.3% from 79.8% in the year-ago period. That deterioration was driven by both higher claims frequency (how often policyholders filed claims, up an estimated 5–7%) and higher claims severity (the cost of each claim, up an estimated 12–14%). Pre-tax underwriting profit fell from $2,173 million to $1,416 million.

Context matters here. The year-ago quarter was unusually clean: catastrophe losses were near zero across the business, making it an exceptionally favourable benchmark. GEICO's 87.3% combined ratio still represents profitable underwriting — well below the 100% line — and the aggregate underwriting result across all of Berkshire's insurance businesses actually improved, rising from $1,722 million to $2,265 million in the three months ending in March 2026, because Berkshire Hathaway Reinsurance Group and the primary commercial-lines business more than offset GEICO's step-down.

The exit condition for GEICO in the written thesis is framed as a market-share and competitive concern — specifically whether Progressive's telematics-based pricing is gaining structural ground against GEICO's cost model — not as a single-quarter combined-ratio print. The quarterly filing does not separately disclose policies in force, so the market-share picture remains opaque for now; that data point needs to be read from the next annual letter.

Buybacks were also modest — approximately $235 million total in the three months ending in March 2026, described by management as "relatively minor amounts." This is consistent with the stated policy of repurchasing only when the price is clearly below intrinsic value. At roughly 1.45–1.50 times book, management evidently judges the current price not sufficiently discounted to warrant aggressive buybacks.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Abel's capital allocation track record is untested at the enterprise level. The OxyChem purchase is defensible but modest; the real stress-test of the investment judgement comes with the next large acquisition. A transaction above $20 billion at a premium valuation, or one that later requires a large goodwill write-down, would break one of the four written conditions.
  • A catastrophic insurance event could temporarily flip float cost positive. A $15 billion or larger net insurance loss in a single year — possible from a combination of hurricane, earthquake, and casualty convergence — would raise the cost of float from near-zero to perhaps 3–5%, reducing the investment advantage that underpins the thesis.
  • BHE's renewable investment economics have narrowed. The energy legislation enacted in the summer of 2025 restricted new eligibility for production tax credits on clean-energy projects started after 2025-12-31. BHE received roughly $5.5 billion in such credits over 2022–2024. Future renewable buildouts face compressed economics, slowing the growth flywheel on Berkshire's regulated utility businesses.
  • GEICO faces a structural competitive threat from telematics pricing. Progressive prices policies in near real-time using driving-behaviour data, allowing faster, more accurate rate adjustments. GEICO's combined ratio recovery since 2022 is impressive, but if Progressive continues to attract the safest drivers with lower prices, GEICO's underwriting quality will gradually erode.
  • The $369 billion cash pile earns less than the broad equity market in most scenarios. At current short-term rates, the cash earns roughly $18 billion pre-tax annually — a comfortable income stream, but one that underperforms in a rising-equity environment. If markets keep rallying without a major dislocation, the cash remains undeployed and the opportunity cost accumulates.

Gaps in the Picture

The share count used to compute book value per share is derived from market capitalisation divided by price rather than directly from Berkshire's EDGAR filings, because the multi-class share structure (Class A and Class B shares with a 1,500-to-1 conversion ratio) makes direct automated extraction from the EDGAR data unreliable. The resulting figure of 2,162.7 million Class B equivalents has been cross-checked against management's own disclosures of per-share book value and is believed to be accurate, but carries a small residual uncertainty.

Policies-in-force data for GEICO — the metric most relevant to the competitive-position thesis driver — are not separately disclosed in quarterly filings and must be read from the annual shareholder letter. The latest policy-count figures are therefore not current.

The score-breakdown and IRR calculations carry an exchange-rate assumption: the portfolio is managed from a euro base, so the dollar-denominated return from Berkshire shares is reduced by an estimated 1.46 percentage points per year to account for expected currency drag. This drag is modelled at the portfolio level and will differ from the actual outcome depending on how the euro–dollar exchange rate evolves over the holding period.

The Rules

If the thesis is confirmed — the conditions are holding and no exit condition has fired — hold the position without action and allow the compounding to continue.

If the thesis is weakened — one or more conditions are deteriorating but the exit threshold has not been crossed — do not add to the position, monitor the specific deteriorating driver through the next quarterly filing, and run a fresh re-validation before making any adjustment.

If the thesis is broken — a written exit condition fires — exit the full position within 30 days. No averaging down on a broken thesis.

If the share price falls below $415 and the thesis is confirmed, add to the position. The confirmation step comes first.

If the share price rises to $581 or above, trim a quarter to a third of the position to harvest the premium while retaining the core holding.

If the share price rises to $647 or above, exit the full position. The market would be pricing in roughly ten years of base-case compounding in a single step; redeploy the proceeds at the next cheap-edge crossing or into a cheaper alternative. No re-entry within 90 days.
