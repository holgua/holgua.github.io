Recent Development

Berkshire's three-month report to March 2026 delivered a clean set of numbers. Book value — the primary metric the thesis tracks — grew a further $9.8 billion in the quarter to reach $727.2 billion, and operating earnings (the company's preferred measure of underlying profitability, which strips out the swings in the equity portfolio's quoted prices) rose 6.5% compared with the same period in 2025 to $12.4 billion before tax. The rail business, Burlington Northern Santa Fe, posted a 13.5% profit gain and improved its operating efficiency ratio to 65.6%. These are straightforward positive readings; the core compounding engines are moving in the right direction.

The development that warrants attention is the car insurance unit. Berkshire's car insurance business — which operates under the brand GEICO — reported a combined ratio (the fraction of every premium dollar consumed by claims and expenses, where anything below 100% means underwriting profit) of 87.3% for the quarter, up from 79.8% in the same period of 2025. A higher combined ratio is worse, and a 7.5 percentage-point deterioration in a single quarter is a meaningful shift. The direct cause was higher claims frequency and severity across bodily injury, property damage, and collision coverages. The context matters: the year-ago quarter was unusually benign for GEICO because Q1 2025 included approximately $860 million in after-tax losses from the Southern California wildfires (per Berkshire's Q1 2025 filing), which pushed the aggregate insurance segment's result down — GEICO's 2025 comparison base was therefore unusually demanding. GEICO remains comfortably profitable at 87.3%, and the aggregate insurance segment's underwriting earnings actually improved year-on-year to $2.3 billion. Still, loss ratios bear watching in the next quarterly filing.

On the regulatory side, an Oregon appeals court reversed the initial wildfire liability verdict against Berkshire's energy business, PacifiCorp, in April 2026 — a material positive development that reduced the tail risk that had been embedded in the stock for two years. PacifiCorp separately agreed to sell its Washington state operations for $1.9 billion in cash, simplifying the utility's footprint. The net effect is that the energy-business liability overhang is substantially smaller today than it was six months ago. The full picture, including how the Share Price reflects these developments, is discussed below.

About Berkshire

Berkshire Hathaway is an unusual company: it is not primarily in the business of making products or delivering services, but in the business of deploying capital across dozens of businesses that do. The mechanism that makes this model distinctive is insurance float — the money that policyholders pay upfront and that the company holds until claims are paid out. Berkshire's insurance operations, which include the car insurance business GEICO, the reinsurance operation that insures other insurers, and a specialty coverage unit, collectively held $176.9 billion of this float at the end of March 2026 and generated underwriting profit in the quarter, meaning the company was effectively paid to hold the money rather than paying for it. That float is then invested in operating businesses and securities, compounding at rates unavailable to competitors who borrow at market rates.

The two large operating businesses are Burlington Northern Santa Fe, one of the two largest freight railways in the United States serving the western half of the country, and Berkshire Hathaway Energy, a regulated utility business covering electricity and gas distribution across multiple US states. Both businesses are capital-intensive — they require substantial ongoing investment to maintain tracks, power lines, and generation capacity — but they earn steady, regulated returns that make them predictable contributors to the whole. Around these anchors sits a constellation of wholly-owned manufacturers, retailers, and service businesses, plus a concentrated portfolio of publicly traded shares in companies such as Apple, Coca-Cola, American Express, and Bank of America. The company holds $369 billion in cash and short-term Treasury bills, a position large enough to fund the entire annual rail budget several times over and to act as a reserve for opportunistic acquisitions.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that the management culture Warren Buffett built — capital allocated to businesses with durable competitive advantages, float deployed at zero or negative cost, earnings retained rather than paid out — will continue compounding retained earnings at rates a passive index cannot match. The conditions that must keep holding:

  1. Book value per share grows faster than the MSCI World index over rolling five-year periods. This is the primary measure of whether the compounding edge is real. If book value consistently trails a broad global index, the rationale for the premium valuation collapses — a holder would be better served owning the index at lower cost.

Latest reading: Shareholders' equity grew 10.5% in the full year to December 2025 to $717.4 billion, and a further $9.8 billion in the three months to March 2026 to $727.2 billion. Book value per Class B equivalent share stands at approximately $337 at March 2026. A five-year trailing comparison against the MSCI World index cannot be assessed from a single quarterly filing, but the current trajectory is positive.

  1. Insurance float cost remains near zero or negative. The entire model depends on Berkshire being paid to hold its investment capital. A sustained positive float cost (where claims exceed premiums) would turn the structural advantage into a liability.

Latest reading: Float reached $176.9 billion at March 2026, up $500 million in the quarter. Management confirmed that the average cost of float was negative in both Q1 2026 and Q1 2025. The free-leverage pillar is fully intact.

  1. No large acquisition reveals a breakdown in capital discipline. Specifically: no transaction exceeding $20 billion at more than 1.5 times the book value of the acquired business, and no subsequent goodwill write-down exceeding $5 billion. A damaging acquisition would signal that the successor CEO, Greg Abel, does not share Buffett's patience for price.

Latest reading: Abel's first significant deployment, the acquisition of the chemicals business OxyChem in January 2026 for approximately $9.5 billion, was executed at terms judged defensible — below the $20 billion threshold and without the hallmarks of price insensitivity. No goodwill impairment has been reported. The test will come with larger allocations.

  1. No structural culture change such as a regular dividend, a break-up, or a capitulation to activist investors. Any of these would signal the abandonment of the decentralised, owner-operator model that has historically driven above-average capital returns.

Latest reading: No dividend has been declared. No break-up discussion has been reported. No activist activity is disclosed in the quarterly filing. The model appears unchanged.

  1. The price-to-book ratio stays below 2.0 times. Above that level, the valuation fully prices in optimistic compounding, and the opportunity cost of holding versus a passive index becomes clearly negative.

Latest reading: The price-to-book ratio stood at 1.47 times at the time of the most recent assessment, based on a share price of approximately $500 and book value per share of approximately $340. This is well below the 2.0-times exit threshold.

The Share Price

At approximately $500 per share, Berkshire trades at 1.47 times its most recently reported book value per share. The fair-value anchor for this analysis is a price-to-book ratio of 1.45 times — meaning the current share price is almost exactly at fair value, with a gap of less than two percentage points. In round numbers, the share is neither cheap nor expensive: the market is paying neither a discount for pessimism nor a premium for optimism.

The hold band runs from 1.25 times book (the buy edge, corresponding to roughly $425 per share) down to 1.75 times book (the trim edge, corresponding to roughly $594 per share). At 1.47 times, the current price sits in the middle of that range — closer to fair value than to either edge. The price has room to move in either direction before any rule-governed action is warranted.

It is worth being explicit about what this valuation method measures and what it does not. A price-to-book ratio compares the share price to the company's net assets as reported on the balance sheet (total assets minus total liabilities, divided by shares outstanding). For most businesses this is a rough proxy; for Berkshire, whose balance sheet is anchored by a large portfolio of publicly traded securities and regulated assets, it captures something real. The limitation is that the $369 billion cash and Treasury bill pile earns roughly 5% annually in the current rate environment, which is a decent return on those assets but below what a capital-light business would earn on incremental deployment. The valuation reflects a business where deployment quality is the key variable; the current price assumes deployment at roughly historical rates.

What's Wobbling

Two things are wobbling, neither of which has crossed a line yet.

The car insurance unit's loss ratio rose 7.5 percentage points in the three months to March 2026. The combined ratio — the measure that determines whether the insurance operation is paying to hold the float rather than being paid — reached 87.3%, up from 79.8% in the year-ago quarter. The underlying causes are higher claims frequency across most vehicle coverage types and rising bodily injury severity in the range of 12 to 14 per cent. GEICO remains profitable at this ratio — any number below 100% represents underwriting income — but the direction is adverse. Policies-in-force were not disclosed in the quarterly filing, leaving the competitive picture (whether GEICO is growing or losing share to rivals including Progressive) partially unobserved. One quarter is not a trend; if the second quarter of 2026 shows further deterioration, the condition in The Case for the Bet on insurance float cost would come under pressure, and a closer assessment of the competitive position would be warranted.

The second wobble is the restraint in share repurchases. Berkshire bought back only approximately $235 million of its own shares in the first quarter of 2026, with no purchases in January or February. The policy that governs buybacks requires both that the price be below intrinsic value and that consolidated cash remain above $30 billion. At 1.47 times book — close to the 1.45 fair-value anchor — management evidently judges the price insufficiently cheap to justify aggressive buybacks. This is consistent with capital discipline, not a change in policy. That said, a prolonged pause in repurchases at prices near or below the buy edge would be an unusual signal and worth noting in future quarterly reviews.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • The capital allocation transition proves imperfect. Greg Abel has completed one significant acquisition — the chemicals business OxyChem, bought for approximately $9.5 billion in January 2026. That deal was defensible; the next large allocation, particularly anything above $20 billion, will be the first genuine test of whether Abel matches the price discipline of the Buffett era. A bad deal cannot be easily unwound.

  • A major catastrophe event hits before the cash fortress is deployed. Berkshire's insurance businesses cover large specialty and catastrophe risks. A single event — an Atlantic hurricane season producing industry losses above $200 billion, or a seismic event in a concentrated exposure zone — could produce net losses exceeding $15 billion. The $369 billion cash reserve absorbs this comfortably; the risk is not solvency but the opportunity cost of that capital being committed to claims rather than acquisitions.

  • GEICO's recovery proves cyclical rather than structural. GEICO's 2025 combined ratio of 84.7% was exceptional; the Q1 2026 reading of 87.3% may be the beginning of a normalisation. Progressive's telematics-based pricing system allows it to reprice faster than GEICO after loss-trend shifts. If GEICO's loss ratio continues rising while Progressive holds its profitability, the competitive position of the car insurance unit — which is the largest single earnings contributor — begins to narrow.

  • PacifiCorp wildfire liabilities re-escalate. The April 2026 appellate reversal was a meaningful positive, but the litigation is not over. If the Oregon Supreme Court reinstates the lower court verdict or if new wildfire events in PacifiCorp's service territory create fresh liabilities, the energy business tail risk — which had been declining — would return.

  • The price-to-book ratio compression happens before deployment. If broader market conditions deteriorate and Berkshire's equity portfolio falls while book value erodes, the price-to-book ratio might compress toward the buy edge not because the thesis is strengthening but because the balance sheet is shrinking. In that scenario, the buy order at $425 would fire for the wrong reasons — a valid concern given the $298 billion equity portfolio's concentration in a handful of names.

Gaps in the Picture

The number of shares outstanding used to compute book value per share is derived rather than directly reported. Berkshire's complex dual-class share structure — Class A shares, each equivalent to 1,500 Class B shares — means the standardised machine-readable data embedded in the filing does not surface a simple diluted share count. The most recent assessment used a count of approximately 2,112 million Class B equivalent shares derived from the market capitalisation divided by the share price, while the filing itself discloses 505,951 Class A shares and 1,397,927,677 Class B shares as of March 2026, which converts to approximately 2,157 million equivalent shares. Both approaches yield a book value per share in the $337–$340 range and are internally consistent. The difference is small and does not affect the valuation conclusion, but readers should note that the precision implied by a specific per-share figure carries a verification footnote.

The dividend yield for Berkshire Hathaway returns no data from the standard market data feed, as expected — Berkshire has never paid a dividend and does not intend to. This is flagged here not as a data gap but as a confirmation that the absence is correct.

Policies-in-force for the car insurance business were not disclosed in the quarterly filing, leaving one competitive dimension of the insurance operation unobserved for this cycle.

The Rules

The following rules govern action. They describe states of the evaluation, not new judgements introduced here.

  • If the thesis is confirmed — operating earnings growing, float cost negative, no disqualifying acquisition, no structural culture change, price-to-book below 2.0 times — then hold without action and let the position compound.

  • If the thesis weakens — meaning one or more conditions above begins to deteriorate but has not yet crossed a defined exit threshold — then do not add to the position. Re-run the thesis validation against the specific deteriorating driver. Make no further decision until the next quarterly filing provides additional evidence.

  • If the thesis breaks — meaning one of the five written exit conditions has fired — then exit the full position within 30 days. No averaging down.

  • If the share price falls to or below the buy edge ($425, corresponding to a price-to-book ratio of 1.25 times) — then confirm the thesis is intact first. If confirmed, add to the position.

  • If the share price rises to or above the trim edge ($594, corresponding to a price-to-book ratio of 1.75 times) — then trim 25 to 33 per cent of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.

  • If the share price rises to or above the exit edge ($662, corresponding to a price-to-book ratio of approximately 2.0 times) — then exit the full position. The market is at that point pricing in roughly ten years of base-case compounding instantly; the implied forward return falls below the long-term equity premium. Redeploy the proceeds at the next cheap-edge crossing or into a cheaper alternative. No re-entry within 90 days to avoid letting the prior price anchor the next decision.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
