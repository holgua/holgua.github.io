PayPal: The Bet Is Wobbling

The Bottom Line

The bet on PayPal — that a structurally cheap global payments network keeps compounding per-share value through aggressive buybacks even if its checkout business loses ground — has run into its first material wobble. The latest quarterly filing shows operating margin compressing for the first time in over a year, even as payment volume keeps growing strongly. The share price near $41 sits at the cheap edge of the acceptable range. The single open question is whether the margin compression is one quarter of noise or the beginning of a structural shift — and the rules say one quarter is not enough to decide.

The Verdict

The latest reading puts PayPal in a "still working but wobbling" state. The rules call for the same patient response across almost every reader situation, with the cheap share price doing some of the work that the wobbling case would otherwise undo.

  • As a fresh investment at today's price (around $41 per share). Wait. The price sits well inside the cheap range — about $50 marks the line above which the shares stop being clearly cheap — but the first signs of margin pressure mean buying in today is buying into a wobbling case. The discipline is to set price alerts at $60 and $50 and wait for the next quarterly filing to clarify whether the margin pressure is a single quarter or a trend.
  • As an existing investment bought below $40 per share — currently sitting on a gain of 3 percent or more. Hold. A small gain against a wobbling case is not a harvest moment. The job now is to watch whether margin compression is a single quarter or a Lores-era trend, not to lock in a thin profit.
  • As an existing investment bought between $40 and $50 per share — currently anywhere between a small loss of about 17 percent and a small gain of about 3 percent. Hold. This band contains today's price. The case is wobbling but the share price is at the cheap edge; the two cancel out into "wait for the next quarter before deciding". Adding here is premature.
  • As an existing investment bought between $50 and $60 per share — currently showing a loss of roughly 17 to 31 percent. Hold. A moderate loss against a wobbling case calls for patience. None of the five lines that would mark the case as clearly broken has been crossed, and the rules explicitly say one quarter of weakness is not enough.
  • As an existing investment bought above $60 per share — currently sitting on a loss of 31 percent or more. Hold. A deep loss on a wobbling but not broken case is exactly the moment when capitulation destroys value. Management is buying back shares at $44.60, retiring 34 million in the latest quarter alone; selling at the bottom of the cycle into the same buyback that is supposed to repair the case is the worst move available.

About PayPal

PayPal runs a two-sided digital payments network connecting roughly 439 million consumer accounts to millions of merchants in about 200 countries. Total payment volume — the gross dollar value of transactions flowing through the system — was $1.79 trillion in 2025. The company takes a small percentage cut on each transaction and holds a portfolio of regulatory licences (all fifty US state money-transmitter licences plus authorisations in Luxembourg and the United Kingdom) that any rival would need to replicate.

The business has three engines. The branded checkout button — "Pay with PayPal" — has historically driven the highest take rate but is now under pressure from Apple Pay and native phone-wallet defaults. Braintree is the back-end payment processor used by large merchants like Uber and Airbnb; volumes are huge but margins thin. Venmo is the consumer-to-consumer app, monetised slowly via debit cards and merchant integrations.

The company generated about $5.6 billion of free cash flow in 2025 and held $14 billion of net cash. The dominant use of that cash is share buybacks — $6 billion retired in 2025, with $12.4 billion of authorisation remaining. The share count has fallen 21 percent in four years.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that PayPal's share price already prices in a structural decline in branded checkout that the underlying numbers do not actually confirm — and that so long as management keeps retiring shares at depressed prices, per-share earnings compound at a healthy rate even if the top line grows only mid-single digits. The bull case is not that PayPal wins the checkout wars; it is that $5 to 7 billion of free cash returned each year via buybacks does the heavy lifting.

For the bet to keep working, the following conditions must keep holding:

  1. Branded checkout does not collapse. Selection-share loss to Apple Pay and native wallets should not exceed two percentage points in a single fiscal year — that size of loss would signal a structural shift, not cyclical softness.
  2. No major regulatory hammer falls. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau or the Department of Justice should not impose a consent order that materially restricts core product features such as dispute resolution, error correction, or PayPal Credit.
  3. Consumer credit losses stay contained. The consumer credit charge-off rate should not rise above 10 percent without a credible recovery path.
  4. Capital allocation stays disciplined. Management should not abandon the buyback programme or take on material incremental debt without a compelling strategic rationale.
  5. Operating profit holds its floor. Annual operating income should not fall below $5.5 billion in any fiscal year, and management should not guide adjusted operating income below $5.0 billion for the next year — either would signal that the cost discipline of the past two years has been abandoned.

The Rules

The rules that govern what action follows from each combination of conditions and share price are fixed:

  • If all conditions are intact. Hold. No action. Let the buyback compounding continue.
  • If any one condition is wobbling but none has clearly broken. Do not add to the position. Re-check the deteriorating condition at the next quarterly filing. Decide only after two consecutive quarters of confirming data — one quarter of weakness is not enough.
  • If any one condition has clearly broken. Sell the full position within thirty days. No averaging down. Document which line was crossed.
  • If the share price has dropped into the bargain range and the conditions are still intact. Add in stages over one or two quarters.
  • If the share price has run into the expensive range. Trim 25 to 33 percent of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.

The Conditions Today

The most recent quarterly filing — covering the three months ending in March 2026 — reads condition by condition as follows.

Branded checkout. The filing does not disclose branded checkout volume as its own line, but the revenue decomposition is telling. Of the roughly $620 million of year-on-year transaction revenue growth in the quarter, Braintree contributed about $410 million, branded PayPal only $140 million, and Venmo $70 million — Braintree drove roughly five times the dollar growth that branded did. A securities class action filed in February 2026 alleges that branded checkout growth has been impaired, but that is an allegation, not a finding. The two-percentage-point share-loss line cannot be measured directly from the filing.

Regulatory. Two Consumer Financial Protection Bureau information requests remain active — one on Regulation E error resolution, one on the PayPal Credit business. A Federal Trade Commission information request was extended in August 2025. In March 2026 the United Kingdom's Financial Conduct Authority opened a competition investigation into provisions in PayPal's contracts with Visa and Mastercard. All four matters are at the investigation stage; none has produced a consent order. The line that would clearly break the case has not been crossed.

Credit losses. The consumer net charge-off rate improved sharply from 4.5 percent to 3.0 percent year on year. The merchant net charge-off rate deteriorated from 4.6 percent to 7.0 percent. The breaking line is written against the consumer rate, which is moving the right way with comfortable headroom against the 10 percent threshold. The merchant deterioration is worth tracking but the merchant book is much smaller.

Capital allocation. The company repurchased about 34 million shares for $1.5 billion at an average price of $44.60 during the quarter, retiring 3 percent of the float in three months. About $12.4 billion of buyback authorisation remains. On April 29, 2026 management announced a strategic reorganisation expected to deliver at least $1.5 billion in annualised cost savings over two to three years. This condition is comfortably met.

Operating profit. Here is the wobble. Quarterly operating margin came in at 18 percent against 20 percent a year earlier. Operating income fell 3 percent year on year despite revenue growing 7 percent. Management attributes the compression to a shift in mix toward Braintree, which runs at a higher expense rate than branded or Venmo. Annualising the quarter's operating income gives about $5.95 billion — still above the $5.5 billion floor; the prior year came in at $6.07 billion. The line is not crossed, but it is the first quarter in which the favourable trend reversed.

The Share Price

At about $41 the share price implies that, for every dollar paid for PayPal stock, the business currently generates roughly 15.3 cents of owner earnings each year. The rules anchor a fair price at 10.5 cents per dollar — roughly $60 per share — and call the shares clearly cheap at 12.5 cents per dollar (a price around $50). The 15.3-cents reading sits well past the cheap line, deep into bargain territory.

In plain share-price terms: $41 is roughly 31 percent below the fair-value anchor of $60 and 18 percent below the cheap-edge line of $50. The shares would have to rise about 46 percent to reach fair value. The market is implicitly pricing PayPal's owner earnings as if they will shrink, not merely fail to grow, for the next decade — the gap the wobbling case must close or fail to close at the next filing.

The Standing Orders

The rules translate the share-price bands into three standing orders and two price alerts. Each is the operationalisation of a pre-committed rule — not a new judgement made here.

Price alerts (informational).
- A first alert at $60, the fair-value anchor. Crossing means the shares have returned to a neutral reading and the buyback engine is running at a much less attractive yield.
- A second alert at $50, the cheap edge. Crossing arms the buy order below; before adding, the latest re-validation should confirm the conditions are still working.

Standing orders (actionable).
- A standing buy order at $50. The rules call for adding in tranches once the share price closes at or below this line and the conditions are intact. Today the price is already below this line, but the wobbling condition on operating profit blocks the add — the rule requires the case to be working, not merely the price to be cheap.
- A standing trim order at $74. The rules call for trimming 25 to 33 percent of the position when the share price closes at or above this line, to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.
- A standing exit order at $97. The rules call for closing the full position when the share price closes at or above this line. At that reading the market is pricing in roughly 11 years of base-case compounding instantly; reallocation beats continuing to hold.

What's Wobbling

The single wobbling condition is operating profit. Quarterly operating margin fell 2 percentage points; operating income fell 3 percent on growing revenue. The mechanism management names — a higher share of payment volume running through Braintree, which has higher expense rates than branded checkout or Venmo — is structural, not cyclical. That is what gives the wobble weight: a mix shift that is a feature of how PayPal now grows, not a one-off cost shock.

Three counterweights soften the reading. First, the absolute level is still safely above the $5.5 billion floor that the rules define as a broken case — annualising the quarter gives just under $6 billion. Second, the late-April reorganisation announcement targets $1.5 billion of annual cost savings, an explicit response to the same mix-shift pressure. Third, the rule already written into the case is that one quarter of weakness is not enough; two consecutive confirming quarters are required.

The other drivers are reading as expected. Total payment volume grew 11 percent. The buyback machinery is accelerating. The active-account count is flat-to-slightly-up at 439 million. Credit losses are mixed at the segment level but well below threshold. The regulatory file is busy but no consent order has emerged.

What Could Still Go Wrong

The case for PayPal could still break in a small number of structurally distinct ways:

  • Margin compression turns out to be a trend, not a quarter. A second consecutive quarter of falling operating profit on growing revenue would re-trigger the rules with stronger force; a fourth or fifth would push annualised operating income below the $5.5 billion floor that defines a clearly broken case.
  • A regulatory consent order lands. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and Federal Trade Commission investigations are real and not all will close quietly. A settlement that restricts dispute-resolution practices or the PayPal Credit product would touch a central line.
  • Apple Pay and native wallets take a measurable bite of branded checkout. A two-percentage-point share loss in a single fiscal year would confirm the structural-decline story. There is no third-party measurement clean enough to test this — that gap is itself a vulnerability.
  • Buyback discipline gives way to a large acquisition. The 2019 to 2021 cycle — Honey and the iZettle integration — caused the 2022 margin crash. A new chief executive with no financial-services background could be tempted to buy growth rather than retire shares. That single decision would change the central return mechanism.

A realistic worst-case drawdown — a second weak margin quarter plus a regulatory shoe drop — could knock the shares 20 to 30 percent below today's price before the buyback and cash balance draw a floor. Permanent capital impairment is the less likely outcome; $14 billion of net cash and $12.4 billion of remaining buyback authorisation provide hard mechanical support even in a bad year.

Gaps in the Picture

A few things in the underlying figures are worth flagging plainly.

The single most important number for the case — branded checkout share of online payments — is not disclosed in the quarterly filing. The company reports total payment volume and growth by product line, but the share-of-checkout figure that the first condition guards against has to be sourced externally. The case is therefore measured against a line that cannot be tested from the company's own numbers alone.

The earnings figure used to anchor cheap and fair treats free cash flow as the working measure but does not separate maintenance investment from growth investment. For an asset-light platform the gap is modest, but it is not zero — a different definition could move the cheap edge by a few dollars either way.

The new chief executive arrived in March 2026 and is yet to deliver a full quarter of his own results — the March 2026 figures still reflect the prior plan. The next two quarters are the first real read on whether buyback discipline, margin defence, and restraint on acquisitions hold under new leadership. That is the most consequential uncertainty in the file, and there is no way to shortcut the waiting.
