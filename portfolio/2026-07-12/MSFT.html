Recent Development

Azure's cloud business grew 40% year-on-year in the three months ending March 2026, matching the rate from the three months ending September 2025. Commercial contracted backlog — the pile of revenue that customers have already committed to but not yet received — surged to $627 billion, up 99% from a year earlier. Operating margins for the nine-month period came in at 47.4%, more than five percentage points above the 42% floor written into the exit rules. The moat is intact by every measurable signal in the latest quarterly report.

The drag on the numbers comes from capital expenditure (the spending on data centres and computing infrastructure that underpins Azure's AI capacity): $80 billion over the nine months ending March 2026, or 33% of revenue. That is up sharply from 23% for the full fiscal year 2025. Free cash flow for the nine months, measured against net income, came to 0.48 — below the 0.65 threshold in exit condition 2. That trigger, however, requires the shortfall to last two consecutive full fiscal years with no improvement in Azure's AI revenue contribution. Neither condition is met: the nine-month window covers three quarters, not two fiscal years, and Azure is growing at 40%. The wobble is real but contained. More detail on the exit conditions is in the Standing Orders.

The IRS transfer-pricing dispute covering fiscal years 2004 to 2013 — for which the tax authority is seeking $28.9 billion plus interest and penalties — showed no adverse development in the latest filing. Microsoft continues to contest through administrative appeals and has not been served with a Tax Court notice. That dispute remains the most important near-term permanent-impairment risk, but it has not moved closer to the threshold that would trigger a mandatory review. How this assessment feeds into the Case for the Bet is set out below.

About Microsoft

Microsoft makes its money by renting software and computing infrastructure to organisations that cannot easily stop renting it. The core product is a bundle of tools — email, document editing, video calls, identity management, and cloud computing — that embeds itself so deeply into an enterprise's daily operations that replacing it requires swapping out every application, every user account, and every security policy at once. That all-or-nothing switching cost is the primary reason the business generates the returns it does.

The cloud computing arm, Azure, is the growth engine. It sells computing power and AI services on a consumption basis — customers pay for what they use — with a mix of long-term contracts that create predictable revenue. Alongside Azure, the Microsoft 365 subscription (which covers the productivity suite plus the AI assistant layer that Microsoft has been layering on top) generates steady, recurring income from roughly one billion commercial and consumer users. A smaller but growing slice comes from LinkedIn, the professional networking platform, and from gaming through the Xbox and Activision franchise.

Fiscal year 2025 revenue was $282 billion, with an operating margin of 45.6% — meaning 45 cents of every dollar of revenue reached operating profit. Return on invested capital has held between 22% and 27% for five consecutive years, well above the 8–10% cost of capital; the business is generating durable excess returns on the money it deploys.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that Microsoft's identity and application lock-in — the all-or-nothing switching cost in the Azure directory layer — is deep enough and wide enough to sustain above-market returns through a decade-long AI infrastructure build, and that the AI spending will ultimately widen the moat rather than permanently compress cash generation.

  1. Azure quarterly revenue growth remains above 25%. The exit rules require growth to fall below 25% for two consecutive quarters while capital expenditure remains above 20% of revenue before the infrastructure bet is considered to have failed. A single quarter below 25% does not fire the trigger.

Latest reading: Azure grew 40% year-on-year in both the three months ending September 2025 and the three months ending March 2026. The 25% floor was not approached.

  1. Free cash flow as a share of net income stays above 0.65 across two full fiscal years. The exit rules require the ratio to fall below 0.65 for two consecutive fiscal years with no improvement in Azure's AI revenue contribution — a compound trigger that demands both persistent cash compression and a concurrent failure of the AI monetisation thesis.

Latest reading: The nine-month ratio (free cash flow pre-share-compensation divided by net income) was 0.48, below the 0.65 threshold. However, only three quarters of data are in view, Azure is growing at 40%, and the full fiscal-year result is not yet available. The compound trigger is not met.

  1. Consolidated operating margin stays above 42%. Two consecutive quarters below 42% would signal that software revenue is no longer scaling faster than operating costs — the core productivity lever of the business.

Latest reading: Operating margin was 46.3% for the three months ending March 2026 and 47.4% for the nine-month period. Both are more than four percentage points above the exit floor.

The Share Price

The valuation method used here measures the ratio of owner earnings (a measure of the cash the business generates on a normalised basis, net of stock-based compensation) to the total market value of the company. The current reading is 2.06% — call it a 2% yield on the price paid for every dollar of ownership. The pre-committed valuation rules set 3.5% as fair value, 5% as cheap enough to add, and 2% as expensive enough to trim.

At a price of $389, the current owner-earnings yield sits 6 basis points above the 2% trim threshold. The shares are priced very close to the upper edge of the hold band. They are not in trim territory yet, but they are within touching distance of it. The share price is well above fair value on the pre-committed valuation anchor.

There is a note of caution in the source data worth surfacing: the pre-committed fair-value yield of 3.5% implies a share price of $229, but the latest quarterly filing reads an actual owner-earnings yield of 2.06% at a price of $389. The two are nearly 70% apart. This divergence reflects the fact that the fair-value anchor was set when the price was lower and owner earnings were higher relative to market capitalisation; the figures are stale. A fresh calibration would set fair value closer to current earnings, not further below. This does not change the trim-edge or exit-edge conclusions — it simply means the "well above fair value" reading should be understood as "well above a stale anchor," not "far from intrinsic worth."

What's Wobbling

Two conditions are mixed rather than confirming.

The first is free cash flow conversion. The nine-month free-cash-flow-to-net-income ratio of 0.48 is below the 0.65 threshold in exit condition 2. This is entirely attributable to capital expenditure: $80 billion over nine months, up from $47 billion in the same period a year earlier. The exit compound trigger requires this to persist for two consecutive full fiscal years while Azure's AI contribution shows no improvement. Azure is at 40% growth and the nine-month window does not constitute two full fiscal years. The wobble is real but the compound trigger is nowhere near firing.

The second is capital expenditure as a share of revenue. At 33% for the nine months and 37% in the single quarter ending March 2026, capital expenditure is running materially above the 22.9% recorded for the full fiscal year 2025. Exit condition 1 requires Azure growth to simultaneously fall below 25% — which at 40% growth is not in range this period.

Neither mixed signal changes the call. That much is settled by the compound structure of both exit rules. The question worth holding in mind for the next quarterly update is whether the capital expenditure rate starts to moderate as guidance has suggested, or whether it continues to climb. The first confirming signal would be two consecutive periods in which capital expenditure as a percentage of revenue declines.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Azure growth decelerates below 25% while capital expenditure stays elevated. Open-weight AI models could commoditise a portion of Azure's inference workloads, reducing the revenue side of the AI infrastructure bet precisely when the cost side is at its highest. This is the only scenario that directly fires the compound exit trigger.
  • IRS dispute escalates to Tax Court. A formal escalation would trigger mandatory 30-day review. The total assessment — $28.9 billion plus interest and penalties — represents roughly 1% of market capitalisation but is meaningful relative to annual free cash flow. An adverse ruling would absorb cash earmarked for buybacks and dividends.
  • FTC files a formal antitrust complaint seeking structural remedies. The Trump-appointed chair of the Federal Trade Commission has explicitly prioritised big-tech investigations. A formal complaint seeking Teams divestiture or cloud interoperability mandates would weaken the portfolio lock-in that is the primary source of the switching-cost moat.
  • Capital expenditure fails to peak. If the guidance that infrastructure spending moderates proves wrong — because AI demand continues to accelerate faster than utilisation improves — the free cash flow compression could persist into a second full fiscal year, bringing the compound trigger within reach.
  • OpenAI equity-method losses scale further. A $4.7 billion charge flowed through the fiscal year 2025 accounts. If OpenAI's losses continue to grow, the drag on earnings per share compounds alongside the capital expenditure headwind.

Gaps in the Picture

  • Owner earnings in this analysis use net income as a proxy, with depreciation and maintenance capital expenditure assumed to cancel. This simplification means the earnings-yield figure is sensitive to how one separates growth capital expenditure from maintenance capital expenditure — a distinction Microsoft's accounts do not make formally.
  • The pre-committed fair-value yield of 3.5% (measured on owner earnings) diverges from the current actual yield (2.06%) by 41%. The pre-committed anchor may not have been updated since the share price rose or since the earnings basis shifted. The trim and exit edges are derived from this anchor and may therefore be set against a stale reference point.
  • The Fair-Value-Bands analysis (a separate model based on a discounted cash flow of forward free cash flow scenarios) puts the central estimate at $561 per share — implying an owner-earnings yield of 1.42% at that price, which diverges from the pre-committed 3.5% fair-value anchor by 59%. The two models are internally consistent within their own assumptions but have not been reconciled with each other.
  • Risk-free rate for this holding is taken from the portfolio's broad market holding rather than a per-holding field; the rate in use reflects a snapshot from 2026-07-12 and may drift.
  • The AI annual recurring revenue figure of $37 billion (referenced in the thesis) does not appear as a standalone disclosed metric in the quarterly report; it is a management-reported non-standard figure. The ten-quarterly filing does not provide a numeric update, so that figure cannot be independently verified from the official account.

The Rules

The evaluation maps to six pre-committed states, each with a defined action.

If the thesis is confirmed — all monitored conditions are working and no exit clause has fired — hold with no new capital at the current price. Azure growth is sustained, the AI contracted backlog is compounding, and the moat is intact. The hold is a compounding bet, not a distressed recovery.

If the thesis is weakening — one or more conditions are deteriorating but no exit clause has fired — do not add to the position. Run a re-validation against the specific deteriorating driver. Monitor for one additional quarter before reassessing position size. Flag whether weakening is cyclical (capital expenditure timing) or structural (competitive displacement). Escalate to a full exit review if a second consecutive quarter confirms the signal.

If the thesis is broken — a written exit condition has fired — exit the full position within 30 days. No averaging down. Log which of the seven exit conditions was triggered and the quarter in which it occurred, for future learning.

If the price falls below the buy edge (a 5% owner-earnings yield, corresponding to roughly $253 per share on current earnings) — confirm the thesis is intact before acting. If intact: add in tranches. The first tranche fires below $352 (40% margin of safety from the base-case fair value of $570). The second fires below $322.

If the price rises above the trim edge ($401, corresponding to a 2% owner-earnings yield) — trim 20–25% of the position to harvest the premium. If Azure growth continues confirming the base case, retain the remaining position.

If the price rises above the exit edge ($1605, corresponding to a 1.5% owner-earnings yield) — exit the full position. At that reading, the position earns less than half the long-term equity premium with zero margin of safety. No re-entry within 90 days.
