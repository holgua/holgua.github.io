Recent Development

The result that mattered most in Berkshire's quarterly report for the three months ending in March 2026 was the reversal of the PacifiCorp wildfire verdict by the Oregon Court of Appeals in April 2026. That single ruling materially reduced the worst-case legal exposure at Berkshire Hathaway Energy, which had been the most visible tail risk on the balance sheet. PacifiCorp also agreed to sell its Washington state operations to Portland General Electric for approximately $1.9 billion in cash, further reducing the regulated utility's geographic concentration in wildfire-prone territory. Taken together, the two developments closed out the most concrete near-term threat to the thesis.

Elsewhere, the quarter was broadly positive. Operating earnings for the three months ending in March 2026 rose 6.5 per cent year-on-year to $12.4 billion pre-tax. BNSF — Berkshire's freight railway — posted pre-tax earnings of $1.8 billion, up 13.4 per cent, on revenues that grew 4.8 per cent. The insurance operation's float — the pool of policyholder premiums held between collection and claims payment — reached approximately $176.9 billion, still at negative cost, meaning Berkshire was effectively paid to hold it.

The one blemish was GEICO, Berkshire's car-insurance business, where the combined ratio (claims and expenses as a share of premiums earned — a lower number is better) rose to 87.3 per cent from 79.8 per cent in the same quarter a year earlier. Higher claims frequency and severity drove the increase. GEICO remained solidly profitable — a combined ratio below 100 per cent means the business earns money on underwriting alone — but the comparison looked worse than it was because the prior-year quarter had near-zero catastrophe losses. The share price reflects a valuation that already accounts for this mixed picture.

About Berkshire

Berkshire Hathaway is an insurance-led holding company that makes money through three reinforcing engines. The first is the insurance float: Berkshire collects premiums before it pays claims, and the gap between collection and payment — approximately $177 billion at present — is invested in equities, bonds, and wholly-owned businesses at no cost, or more precisely at a negative cost, since the underwriting operations have historically earned a profit. The second is the operating subsidiaries: BNSF, one of North America's two largest freight railways, serving western routes that have no economically viable road alternative; Berkshire Hathaway Energy, a regulated electricity and gas utility operating in six states; GEICO, one of the largest US car insurers; Precision Castparts, an aerospace components manufacturer; and dozens of smaller industrial, retail, and services businesses. The third is capital allocation: retained earnings and proceeds from maturing bonds flow to Gregory Abel, who succeeded Warren Buffett as chief executive in January 2026, to redeploy into acquisitions, share repurchases, or additions to the publicly traded equity portfolio.

The publicly traded equity portfolio — worth roughly $298 billion at the latest filing — is concentrated: roughly 65 per cent of it sits in five names, including Apple and Bank of America. Under US accounting rules, every gain or loss on that portfolio passes through the income statement each quarter, which is why Berkshire's reported profit swings wildly — from a $22.8 billion loss in 2022 to a $96.2 billion profit in 2023 — without those swings meaning anything about the underlying business.

The Case for the Bet

The central bet is that Gregory Abel and the capital allocation culture Warren Buffett built will continue to reinvest retained earnings and the insurance float into high-returning businesses and securities at rates a passive investor could not replicate directly. The position is sized as a long-duration core holding rather than a trade, and four numbered conditions must keep holding for it to remain valid.

  1. Book value per share growth must not trail the MSCI World total-return index for five or more consecutive years. That sustained underperformance would signal the compounding edge has gone.

Latest reading: Shareholders' equity grew 10.5 per cent in the full year 2025, rising to $717.4 billion, and added a further $9.8 billion in the three months ending in March 2026 to reach $727.2 billion. The per-share book value is approximately $337 in Class B equivalent terms. Operating earnings grew 6.5 per cent year-on-year in the latest quarter and 16.8 per cent for the full year 2025. No trailing-five-year comparison to the MSCI World index is available in the source data, but the directional reading confirms compounding is ongoing.

  1. No large acquisition that reveals Abel lacks capital discipline — specifically, no single transaction exceeding $20 billion at a reported price above 1.5 times the book value of the target, and no acquisition that subsequently requires a goodwill write-down exceeding $5 billion.

Latest reading: Abel's first notable acquisition was OxyChem at approximately $9.5 billion in January 2026, well below the $20 billion threshold and at a price management described as rational. Share repurchases in the three months ending in March 2026 were described as "relatively minor" at about $235 million, consistent with the stated policy of buying only when the price sits clearly below intrinsic value.

  1. No structural culture change such as a regular dividend, a break-up, or capitulation to an activist investor that would signal the decentralised owner-operator model has been abandoned.

Latest reading: No dividend has been declared. No break-up discussion is disclosed. Buffett remains Chairman; Abel continues as chief executive with no reported changes to the leadership structure.

  1. The price-to-book ratio must not exceed 2.0 times, the level at which the valuation would fully price in optimistic compounding and the opportunity cost versus a passive index becomes clearly negative.

Latest reading: The price-to-book ratio stands at approximately 1.45 times, using year-end 2025 equity of $717.4 billion. The Q1 2026 equity of $727.2 billion would lower the ratio slightly to approximately 1.43 times. The exit level of 2.0 times implies a share price of approximately $651 — about 35 per cent above today's price.

What's Wobbling

The visible wobble is GEICO. The combined ratio rose to 87.3 per cent in the three months ending in March 2026, up from 79.8 per cent in the same period a year earlier, as higher claims frequency and severity drove the figure upward. GEICO's pre-tax underwriting earnings fell to $1.4 billion from $2.2 billion year-on-year as a result.

The wobble is real but contained for three reasons. First, 87.3 per cent still represents solidly profitable underwriting — a combined ratio below 100 per cent means the insurer earns money before investment income is counted. Second, the prior-year quarter was an outlier: near-zero catastrophe losses in early 2025 made the comparison unusually flattering. Third, none of the four numbered exit conditions concerns a single quarter's combined-ratio print; the relevant watchpoint is a combined ratio above 100 per cent sustained for two consecutive quarters, which has not occurred.

A secondary, quieter wobble is the progress of the proposed merger between Union Pacific and Norfolk Southern in the freight-rail market. If the Surface Transportation Board approves that combination in 2027, BNSF's position as one of two dominant western freight railways would face its first serious competitive challenge in a generation. That decision is still pending and the outcome uncertain.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Gregory Abel makes a large acquisition — above $20 billion, at a price well above the target's book value, without a clear and durable competitive advantage — that deploys the cash pile at returns below cost of capital and permanently impairs the compounding engine.
  • A catastrophic insurance event or series of events in a single year exceeds the buffer that the $177 billion float provides, forcing reserve drawdowns and a step-change in underwriting pricing.
  • GEICO loses further ground to Progressive, which surpassed State Farm as the largest US personal auto insurer in early 2026 and built its data and telematics advantages over several years. A sustained GEICO combined ratio above 100 per cent — rather than a single elevated quarter — would signal the competitive position is structurally eroding.
  • The Surface Transportation Board approves the Union Pacific–Norfolk Southern merger, ending the western rail duopoly that has supported BNSF's pricing power and operating margins for decades.
  • The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed into law in July 2025, accelerated the phase-out of production tax credits for new renewable projects beginning construction after 2025. This constraint on Berkshire Hathaway Energy's growth pipeline is enacted law, not a reversible regulatory risk, and will weigh on the energy subsidiary's returns on new capital for the foreseeable future.
