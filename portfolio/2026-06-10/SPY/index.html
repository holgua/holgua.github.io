SP500: The Bet Is Wobbling

The Bottom Line

The fund tracking the largest 500 companies in the United States is doing exactly what it was bought to do — except for one thing. The share price has climbed so far that the basket of underlying businesses now pays out, in earnings per dollar invested, less than a safe ten-year US government bond. The rules treat that as a meaningfully expensive reading and call for trimming. None of the deeper alarms — fund cost, fund size, single-company concentration, business quality — have tripped. The conditions are working on five of six counts; the share price sits past the expensive line; and the one condition to watch is whether the gap between basket earnings and Treasury yields stays inverted for a second quarter in a row.

The Verdict

The overall call is to take some money off the table at today's price, with one exception for those who bought near the previous peak.

  • As a fresh investment at today's price (around $738 per share). Wait. Buying in here means accepting an earnings yield from the basket that is lower than the yield on a US government bond, which fails the central economic bet behind owning the fund at all. Two price alerts make more sense than a purchase: one at $553, where the basket would be reasonably priced, and one at $443, where it would be cheap enough to start adding in earnest.
  • As an existing investment bought below $700 per share — currently sitting on a gain of about 5% or more. Trim a quarter to a third of the position. The rules treat today's price as meaningfully expensive against the alternative of safe bonds; harvesting part of the gain locks in the premium while keeping the core exposure intact for the long run.
  • As an existing investment bought roughly between $700 and $800 — currently within a few percent either side of break-even. Trim a quarter to a third. The same logic applies: the basket is in the expensive zone, and partial harvesting is the pre-committed move at this share price. The cost-basis happens not to matter much here — the rules are reading the market, not the entry price.
  • As an existing investment bought between $800 and $900 — currently showing a loss of up to about 18%. Trim a quarter to a third. This is the awkward case: selling at a mild loss feels wrong, but the rules call for trimming the expensive equity exposure rather than averaging down into it. The discipline is to respect the price signal even when the cost basis disagrees.
  • As an existing investment bought above $900 per share — currently showing a loss of roughly 18% or more. Hold. The rules carve out an exception for deep losses against a thesis that is wobbling but not broken. Capitulating at minus 18 or worse, in order to harvest a premium that the position no longer has, would be the wrong direction. Sit still and let the next quarter clarify whether the wobble becomes a break.

About the Fund

SPY is the original exchange-traded fund — launched in 1993 by State Street Investment Management — and it owns shares in the 500 largest companies listed in the United States, in roughly the same proportions as the well-known S&P 500 index. There are no stock-pickers, no industry bets, no attempt to time the market. The fund simply holds what the index holds, charges a small annual fee, and lets the underlying basket of businesses do the work.

Today the fund manages roughly $784 billion on behalf of its holders, which makes it the most heavily traded equity fund of any kind in the world. Its annual fee is 0.0945% — meaning a holder pays a little under one dollar in costs each year for every thousand dollars invested. The single largest holding is NVIDIA, the maker of chips that power artificial-intelligence systems, at just under 8% of the basket. The technology sector as a whole now accounts for 39% of the fund, more than double the weight of the next-largest sector. In short, the fund is still a broad bet on US business, but its centre of gravity has drifted decisively toward big technology.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is straightforward. Owning the basket is worth it only as long as the businesses inside it produce, in earnings, a meaningful premium over what a safe US government bond pays. If the basket cannot clear that hurdle, holding bonds — or cash — is the better deal.

Six conditions must keep holding for the bet to work:

  1. The basket's earnings yield — the share of every dollar invested that the underlying companies earn in profit each year — should sit above the ten-year US Treasury yield by at least two percentage points. If the earnings yield drops below 3% at the same time as the gap to Treasuries goes negative for two consecutive quarters, the bet is off.
  2. The fund's annual fee must stay at or below 0.15% — that is, 15 cents per year for every hundred dollars invested.
  3. The fund must keep at least $100 billion in assets, the level below which large investors would struggle to enter or exit cleanly.
  4. No single company should account for more than 13% of the basket. Above that line, the fund stops being a diversified bet on the US economy and starts becoming a concentrated bet on one firm.
  5. The rules that govern which companies belong in the index must not be materially restructured in a way that undermines the broad-market mandate.
  6. The underlying quality of the largest holdings must hold up. Specifically, the middle of the top-ten companies should earn a return on the equity they employ of at least 15% — the level below which the basket would have lost its claim to being a collection of quality businesses.

The Rules

The rules behind this evaluation are fixed in advance, so that no single quarter's mood drives the call. There are five of them.

  • If every condition above keeps holding and the share price is in a normal range, hold the fund.
  • If one or more conditions starts to wobble but none of the written exits has been crossed, do not add new money, re-check after the next quarter, and prepare a switch to a cheaper alternative or to cash if the wobble persists.
  • If an exit condition is breached, or if the fund itself fails one of the size, fee, or concentration tests, close the position and move the proceeds either to a safer alternative or to another holding that passes the value test.
  • If the share price falls into the bargain zone — where the basket's earnings yield rises above 6.25% — add to the position. The basket is meaningfully cheap against safe bonds.
  • If the share price climbs into the expensive zone — where the basket's earnings yield falls below 4% — trim a quarter to a third of the position. The basket is meaningfully expensive against safe bonds, and the rules call for harvesting the premium.

The Conditions Today

Five of the six conditions are working; one is wobbling.

The earnings premium over bonds. The basket today earns 3.75 cents per dollar invested per year, while the ten-year US Treasury pays 4.54 cents. The premium is negative — minus 0.79 percentage points — for the first quarter under this evaluation. The written exit requires two consecutive quarters of negative premium combined with an earnings yield below 3%, so this is a warning, not yet a breach.

Underlying business quality. The middle of the top-ten companies earns a return on equity of 37%, far above the 15% line. A weighted-average figure of 68% sits even higher, lifted by Apple and NVIDIA, both of which have shrunk their book equity through years of share buybacks; the 37% middle reading is the safer one. Either way, the basket is full of high-quality businesses.

The fund's fee. The annual fee stands at 0.0945%, comfortably below the 0.15% ceiling. State Street has plenty of room to drift before any concern arises.

Single-company concentration. NVIDIA is the largest holding at 7.89% of the basket, well below the 13% line. Concentration is elevated but not regime-changing.

Fund size. Assets are $784 billion against a $100 billion floor — nearly eight times the line. There is no liquidity concern in any plausible scenario.

Index methodology and sponsor stability. No automatic signal here; the rules of the S&P 500 index and the trust structure that holds the fund are monitored externally, and there is no indication of restructuring.

The Share Price

The fund last closed at $738.40 per share. The rules read this as expensive — meaningfully so. The basket's earnings yield of 3.75% sits below the 4% line that the rules treat as the expensive edge. To put the same idea in everyday language, an investor putting fresh money in today is paying roughly $26.66 for every dollar of underlying annual profits — well above the long-run average of around $16. The fair-value anchor, where the basket would be reasonably priced, sits at $553 per share; today's price is roughly 33% above that anchor and 7% above the expensive edge at $692. That much is settled by arithmetic alone.

The Standing Orders

The rules translate the share-price bands into two standing orders and two price alerts. Each is the pre-committed operationalisation of the bands above — not a new judgement.

Price alerts (informational).
- A first alert at $553, the fair-value anchor. The crossing means the share price has returned to a neutral reading; no action follows, but a fresh read of the conditions is warranted.
- A second alert at $443, the cheap edge. The crossing arms the buy order below; before adding, the latest re-validation should confirm the conditions are still working.

Standing orders (actionable).
- A standing buy order at $443. The rules call for adding to the position once the share price closes at or below this line and the conditions are still working. The order may be staged across one or two quarters rather than filled in a single fill.
- A standing trim order at $692. The rules call for trimming a quarter to a third of the position when the share price closes at or above this line, to harvest the premium while keeping the core exposure.

There is no standing exit order at a higher price. For an index basket of this kind, the sell side is governed by vehicle-integrity breaches — fee, size, or concentration thresholds — rather than by price alone. The standing trim and the verdicts above do not contradict: those verdicts describe the current reading at $738; the standing orders describe what the rules will execute if the share price moves to either line.

What's Wobbling

The one condition that has slipped is the earnings premium over bonds. For the first quarter under this evaluation, the basket pays out less in earnings per dollar than a safe ten-year Treasury — minus 0.79 percentage points where the rules call for plus two. The written exit requires the premium to stay negative for a second consecutive quarter and the basket's earnings yield to drop below 3% on top of that; neither leg of the exit has fully tripped yet, so the rules read this as wobbling, not broken.

A second, smaller wobble: the business-quality reading carries an asterisk. The weighted-average return on equity of the top ten companies is 68%, but that figure is lifted by Apple and NVIDIA, whose book equity has been shrunk by years of share buybacks; the middle reading of 37% is the load-bearing one. The basket has not lost its quality, but the headline number flatters it.

What Could Break the Fund

There are four ways the fund itself, distinct from the bet on the market, could stop working as a vehicle.

  • The fee rises. State Street currently charges 0.0945% per year. If the fee climbed above 0.15% without a competitive response, the cheaper alternatives — Vanguard's VOO or BlackRock's IVV — would become the better way to hold the same basket.
  • The fund shrinks. Assets currently stand at $784 billion. An extraordinary outflow event that took assets below $100 billion would start to make clean entry and exit harder for large holders.
  • Concentration tips into regime change. NVIDIA at 7.89% is already meaningful; if any single name climbed past 13% of the basket, the fund would have stopped being a diversified bet on US business and started being a concentrated bet on one company.
  • The index methodology is restructured. The S&P 500's selection rules are set by an external committee. A material change — for example, narrowing the universe to only profitable firms, or shifting weight from market value to some other measure — could undermine the broad-market mandate that the fund was bought to express.

The deeper macroeconomic risk — that earnings growth disappoints and the basket re-rates from 27 times earnings back toward its long-run average near 16 — is real, but it is not a break in the fund. It is the central reason the rules call for trimming at today's price.

Gaps in the Picture

No material gaps in the underlying figures. The basket valuation, vehicle-integrity numbers, and look-through quality are all drawn from a live snapshot taken on 2026-06-07. One known caveat: a cyclically adjusted earnings figure, which would average earnings over a full economic cycle and is the measure that long-horizon value investors usually prefer, is not produced by the upstream data source for an index basket and is deferred. The current-year earnings yield is the fallback. As wobbles go, this is the slow kind — one quarter of negative premium does not break a thesis built around a multi-year holding period; a second consecutive quarter would.
