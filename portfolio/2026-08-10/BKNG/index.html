How the Score Adds Up

Booking scores 4.65, ranking 11 of 20 active holdings by this measure — it sits just below the portfolio's midpoint, with the score reflecting a high-quality business priced close to, but not below, its fair-value threshold.

Quality weight: 1.20. Booking earns the strongest of three moat tiers, based on two durable sources — the two-sided property network (over 4.4 million properties and 1.24 billion annual room nights creating mutual dependency between supply and demand) and the intangible depth of the Booking.com, Priceline, and Agoda brands across 40 languages. Businesses assigned the strongest moat tier carry a weight of 1.20, which amplifies any return surplus above the hurdle rate.

Expected return: 10.34%. This figure is a probability-weighted average across three scenarios — 20% chance of a −14.9% annual return (the bear), 55% chance of −2.1% (the base), and 25% chance of +57.9% (the bull) — giving 0.20 × (−14.9) + 0.55 × (−2.1) + 0.25 × 57.9 = +10.34%.

Hurdle rate: 4.91%. The minimum acceptable return is built from three components: a 0.91% real yield on inflation-linked government bonds (the no-risk baseline in today's market), a 2.0% inflation assumption, and a 2.0% additional premium for bearing equity risk rather than holding a bond. Together these set the bar that the expected return must clear before this holding earns a positive score.

Booking's expected return clears the hurdle by 3.88 percentage points, multiplied by the quality weight of 1.20, producing the 4.65 score. That places it just inside the top half of the portfolio by a narrow margin — a solid but not exceptional position, consistent with a high-quality business priced near fair value rather than cheaply.

Recent Development

The three months ending in March 2026 confirmed that Booking Holdings' two-sided travel network is growing solidly despite a geopolitical disruption. Total bookings across the platform reached $53,758 million, a 15.2% increase on the same quarter a year earlier, with merchant bookings (paid directly through Booking.com at the point of sale) growing 24.3%. Revenue came in at $5,532 million, up 16.2% year on year including roughly six percentage points of foreign-currency tailwind.

The Middle East conflict introduced an isolated headwind: room-night growth reported at 5.9% year on year, held back by approximately two percentage points by cancellations and reduced demand in the Middle East corridor. Stripping that out, growth was roughly 8%. Exit condition (1) — which requires material room-night declines across multiple regions for two or more consecutive quarters, specifically carving out isolated geopolitical shocks — was not triggered. Marketing costs as a share of total bookings held flat at 3.8%, though return on performance-marketing spend was temporarily depressed by the wave of cancellations: money had already been spent acquiring bookings that were subsequently cancelled. This is the kind of noise the thesis was written to tolerate.

Capital return ran at full pace. The company repurchased $4,022 million of its own shares in the quarter (22 million shares), paid $343 million in dividends, and entered April with $18.2 billion remaining under its $20 billion repurchase authorisation. The Transformation Programme — a cost restructuring begun in 2024 — delivered $550 million in annualised savings. For a fuller picture of how the Share Price translates these figures into valuation terms, see that section below.

About Booking

Booking Holdings operates the world's largest online travel marketplace by room nights booked. Its primary brand, Booking.com, connects travellers directly to hotels, apartments, houses, and other places to stay across more than 220 countries — roughly 4.5 million properties in total, of which 4.0 million are homes, apartments, or other non-hotel accommodation. The company's other brands — Priceline and Agoda, among others — add geographic depth and price-comparison reach.

The economics are straightforward: when a traveller books through Booking.com, the company takes a commission from the property. That commission (revenue divided by total bookings processed) has run at roughly 10.3% in recent quarters. The business requires very little physical capital — no hotels to own, no aircraft to lease. Its main recurring cost is performance marketing (spending on online advertising to bring new travellers to the platform), which ran at $8.2 billion in the full year 2025, or about 30% of revenue. Free cash flow (the cash the business generates after all operating costs and the small amount of technology capital spending required to run the platform) reached $9.1 billion in 2025, a margin of nearly 34% on revenue of $26.9 billion. That cash generation is what funds the aggressive share buyback programme.

The Case for the Bet

The central idea is that Booking.com's two-sided network — properties that need bookings and travellers who need properties — is large enough and interconnected enough to survive the main threats: AI-powered trip-planning tools that bypass the OTA layer, Google's push to keep searches on its own platform, and hotel chains trying to lure guests away from intermediaries. The network's durability, combined with disciplined return of nearly all cash generated to shareholders, is what makes the bet worth holding. Seven conditions must keep holding for that story to stay intact:

  1. Room-night growth must not decline materially across multiple regions for two or more consecutive quarters absent an isolated shock. If demand for stays booked through the platform falls persistently in more than one geography simultaneously, the network effect is leaking: fewer travellers make the platform less attractive to new properties, and vice versa. A single-quarter, single-region dip does not count, particularly when explained by a known geopolitical event.

Latest reading: 338 million room nights in the three months to 2026-03-31, up 5.9% year on year (approximately 8% excluding the Middle East conflict impact). Growth was positive in Europe, Asia, and the United States; negative only in the Middle East corridor. The isolated-shock carve-out in the exit condition applies; no trigger.

  1. Marketing spend as a share of total bookings must not rise meaningfully for two or more consecutive years. If Booking.com must spend more, as a percentage of what it processes, to acquire each booking, it signals that the moat — which previously delivered growth almost automatically through network density and direct traffic — is eroding, and growth is being bought rather than earned.

Latest reading: Marketing costs were 3.8% of total bookings in the three months to 2026-03-31, identical to the same period a year earlier. Performance-marketing return on investment was temporarily impaired by Middle East cancellations (money spent before the conflict hit bookings that were subsequently cancelled), but this is a one-quarter event, not a trend. No trigger.

  1. The revenue take-rate must not compress materially or persistently. The take-rate (revenue as a share of total bookings processed) reflects the economics the platform can extract from both sides of its marketplace. Sustained compression signals that competitors, regulators, or technology intermediaries are squeezing the terms on which Booking.com operates.

Latest reading: Revenue as a share of total bookings was 10.3% in the three months to 2026-03-31, up from 10.2% in the same period a year earlier. Facilitated-payment revenue and favourable timing of bookings versus travel dates contributed. No compression; no trigger.

  1. Capital-return discipline must hold. If buybacks slow or stop while an owner-earnings yield (free cash flow net of share-based compensation, divided by market capitalisation) remains at levels where buying back shares is value-accretive, and especially if cash is instead directed into a large, speculative acquisition, the per-share compounding engine breaks.

Latest reading: $4,022 million in share repurchases and $343 million in dividends in the three months to 2026-03-31; $18.2 billion of the $20 billion authorisation remains. No acquisition announced. No trigger.

  1. Owner earnings yield must not fall below 4%. An owner-earnings yield — annual free cash flow net of share-based compensation, divided by market capitalisation, expressed as a percentage — is the primary valuation gauge. Below 4%, the valuation is pricing in optimistic compounding and the risk-reward of the AI and regulatory threats no longer justifies holding. This is the quantitative exit gate.

Latest reading: Owner earnings yield at 2026-08-10: 6.02%. Above the 4% exit threshold and above the 5.5% fair-value threshold. Current share price of $180 sits within the hold band (buy edge $155, trim edge $271). No trigger.

  1. By the third quarter of 2027, management must have articulated and visibly executed a coherent AI-first strategy. Booking.com faces the risk that AI-powered trip-planning agents — tools from Google, OpenAI, and others — become the dominant first step in booking a trip, bypassing OTA front-doors entirely. The existing walled-garden approach (smart filtering, a conversational agent on Priceline) is not yet a strategy. The deadline for a coherent answer is the third-quarter 2027 earnings cycle.

Latest reading: The filing to 2026-03-31 repeated the Connected Trip vision consistently. Mobile-app room-night mix rose to the high-fifties percentage (up from the mid-fifties a year earlier), and direct booking behaviour is improving through the mobile channel. No shipped artificial-intelligence engagement metrics or feature-level milestones were disclosed. The Q3 2027 deadline has not been reached. Watch condition.

  1. EU regulatory enforcement must not cross from compliance cost into structural economic impairment. The European Union's Digital Markets Act designates Booking.com as a gatekeeper, and six national competition authorities are running simultaneous investigations. The threshold for exit is a formal decision imposing structural product remedies — such as mandatory removal of rate-parity clauses — or fines exceeding 3% of annual global revenue in a single year.

Latest reading: No formal structural remedy or qualifying fine disclosed as of the 2026-04-28 filing. Regulatory proceedings are ongoing but no trigger-level outcome has occurred.

The Share Price

At $180 today, Booking Holdings' shares offer an owner earnings yield (free cash flow net of share-based compensation, divided by market capitalisation) of approximately 6.0%. The hold band runs from a 7.0% yield at the buy edge — corresponding to a share price of around $155 — up to a 4.0% yield at the trim edge, corresponding to around $271. At 6.0%, the share is in the middle of that band: comfortably away from the cheap edge, but also well below the trim threshold. Fair value on this methodology (5.5% yield) implies a price of around $197.

In plain terms: at $180, the shares are priced slightly below what the fair-value framework considers normal — a slight advantage for anyone already holding a position. The market is paying roughly 8.7% less than the fair-value estimate, primarily because the market appears to be pricing in roughly 4% annual cash-flow growth rather than the 13% base case built into the thesis. Whether that gap closes depends on whether the structural drivers in the Case for the Bet keep holding.

The Standing Orders

Two price levels trigger automatic alerts under this evaluation's rules.

Alert at $197 (fair value). If the share price crosses above $197, the position has moved from modestly below fair value to at or above it. No automatic action follows, but it is the signal to review whether the thesis remains intact before adding further.

Alert at $155 (buy edge). If the share price falls to $155, an owner-earnings yield of 7% is reached — the threshold where the gap between price and value is wide enough to justify adding to the position, provided no exit condition has been triggered.

Three standing orders govern what happens at each band edge.

Buy order at $155. If the share price reaches $155 and the thesis is confirmed intact (no exit condition triggered at that moment), add to the position in tranches over one to two quarters. Confirm the thesis first; do not add mechanically.

Trim order at $271. If the share price reaches $271, an owner-earnings yield of 4% is reached — the lower edge where the risk-reward ceases to be favourable. Trim 25–33% of the position to lock in the premium while keeping the core holding. Re-evaluate the remaining position size after trimming.

Exit order at $434. If the share price reaches $434, the market is pricing in approximately 6.5 years of base-case compounding delivered instantly. Exit the full position and redeploy proceeds at the next cheap-edge crossing or into a cheaper alternative. No re-entry within 90 days, to avoid anchor bias to the prior price.

What's Wobbling

Three drivers are mixed rather than outright confirming, and together they form the honest part of this thesis.

Room-night growth with a geopolitical asterisk. Reported growth of 5.9% in the three months to 2026-03-31 includes the impact of Middle East cancellations and reduced demand in that corridor. Adjusting for that, growth was closer to 8%. The exit condition for room nights specifically carves out "isolated geopolitical shocks," so no line was crossed. But the pattern matters: if the Middle East situation persists into a second or third consecutive quarter, the carve-out becomes harder to apply, and exit condition (1) would need re-evaluation. This does not change the current verdict; it is the wobble to watch. See the Case for the Bet for the full condition definition.

AI strategy: vision without metrics. Every earnings cycle, management describes the Connected Trip vision — a seamlessly integrated trip-planning experience powered by artificial intelligence. Every earnings cycle, the filing omits specific engagement numbers, conversion rates, or AI-feature traffic figures. Mobile-app growth and improved direct-booking behaviour are genuine signals. They are not the same as evidence that the company has a strategy for competing with AI agents that originate trip discovery outside the Booking.com environment entirely. Exit condition (6) gives management until the third-quarter 2027 earnings cycle to produce a visible, coherent answer. The clock is running.

Marketing ROI impaired by cancellations. Marketing costs as a share of total bookings held flat at 3.8%, but performance-marketing return on investment was damaged in March 2026: money committed to paid channels before the conflict began produced bookings that were subsequently cancelled. This is a mechanical artefact of the timing mismatch between marketing spend and booking completion, not a signal of moat erosion. No exit condition is triggered. It is, however, a reminder that the direct-traffic mix (mid-fifties percentage of room nights coming from users who type Booking.com directly) is the metric that most cleanly separates structural moat strength from noise. Watch that figure in the next filing.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • AI-native disintermediation (high likelihood, high impairment). If tools from Google, OpenAI, or others become the dominant entry point for trip planning — so that travellers begin and often complete their research inside an AI assistant rather than a travel marketplace — Booking.com's front-of-funnel position erodes. The company's KAYAK unit took a $180 million goodwill writedown in 2025, a direct signal that the meta-search segment of its business has already been partially disintermediated. The full OTA model is the next at risk.

  • EU structural regulatory remedy (medium likelihood, high impairment). If the European Commission or a national court imposes mandatory removal of rate-parity clauses — which prevent hotels from offering lower prices on their own websites than on Booking.com — the economic logic of the platform is impaired. Six simultaneous national investigations are active. This risk has been present for years; it has not resolved.

  • Marketing cost inflation. If Google raises its cost-per-click for travel advertising, and Booking.com's direct-traffic mix does not grow fast enough to offset the higher cost of paid channels, free-cash-flow margins compress. Performance marketing is the single largest operating cost after personnel.

  • Capital misallocation via a large acquisition. Booking Holdings has returned over 100% of its cumulative free cash flow to shareholders since 2022. If management were to redirect that capital discipline into a multi-billion acquisition at a speculative premium — as many platform companies do eventually — the per-share compounding maths would suffer an irreversible setback.

  • Valuation at fair value leaves little margin for error. At an owner earnings yield of 6% against a fair-value threshold of 5.5%, the share is close to fairly priced on the base case. If any of the structural drivers deteriorate before the market has priced them in, there is limited valuation cushion to absorb the shock. A bear-scenario outcome (revenue growth falling to 5% per year, margin pressure, AI headwinds) implies roughly −35% downside from fair value — a steep decline with no cheap-entry buffer at today's price.

Gaps in the Picture

Owner earnings are computed as free cash flow (operating cash flow minus total capital expenditure) net of share-based compensation. Total capital expenditure is used rather than maintenance capital expenditure alone, which may slightly understate true owner earnings if a material portion of spending is growth investment rather than upkeep. The filing's management discussion does not separate these. This is a standard caveat for asset-light technology businesses where total capital expenditure is small (1.2% of revenue), but it is a ceiling on precision.

The risk-free rate used in the hurdle-rate calculation is taken from the SPY market-data snapshot rather than a BKNG-specific field, which is not maintained separately. This has a minor effect on the hurdle rate and score; the rate used (approximately 4.4%) is broadly consistent with ten-year government bond yields at the snapshot date.

The Rules

If the thesis is confirmed intact and valuation is within the hold band: hold — no action. Continue compounding.

If the thesis shows signs of weakening (one or more drivers deteriorating but no exit condition yet triggered): do not add to the position. Re-run the thesis review on the specific deteriorating driver. Wait for the next two quarterly filings before deciding — one quarter of data, then confirm with a second.

If the thesis is broken (an exit condition has triggered): exit the full position within 30 days. No averaging down. Record which exit condition fired, for future learning.

If the share price falls below the buy edge ($155) and the thesis is confirmed intact: add to the position in tranches over one to two quarters. Confirm the thesis first.

If the share price rises above the trim edge ($271): trim 25–33% of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core. Re-evaluate the remaining position size after trimming.

If the share price rises above the exit edge ($434): exit the full position. The market is pricing in approximately 6.5 years of base-case compounding delivered instantly; redeploy proceeds at the next cheap-edge crossing or into a cheaper alternative. No re-entry within 90 days.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
