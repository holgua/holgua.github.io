About Berkshire

Berkshire Hathaway is, in essence, a permanent-capital holding company that has been compounding shareholders' money for six decades. It earns money through four broad channels. The first is insurance — GEICO is one of the largest car insurers in the United States, and the reinsurance subsidiary, Berkshire Hathaway Reinsurance Group, absorbs catastrophe risk from other insurers worldwide. The unusual feature of the insurance business is the float: premiums collected from policyholders sit in Berkshire's hands until claims are paid, giving the company a pool of investable capital that currently stands at $176.9 billion. For most of its history, this float has cost Berkshire nothing — or less than nothing, when the underwriting business runs a profit. The second channel is the railroad: BNSF operates the largest freight rail network in the western United States, moving bulk commodities, consumer goods, and intermodal containers from ports to distribution centres. The third is regulated utilities: Berkshire Hathaway Energy owns power generation and transmission assets across the United States, United Kingdom, and Canada. The fourth is a sprawling group of manufacturing, services, and retailing businesses — precision aerospace components, building products, manufactured homes, confectionery, furniture retail, and much else — generating steady cash that flows up to the holding company.

Sitting on top of all of this is a $288 billion public-equity portfolio, anchored in Apple, American Express, Bank of America, and Coca-Cola, plus a cash and short-term Treasury position of approximately $339 billion. Greg Abel became chief executive officer on 1 January 2026; Warren Buffett remains chairman and retains majority voting control. The architecture of the business — decentralised, owner-operator-led, return-focused — has not changed under the new chief executive, and the first year of his tenure produced no discernible deviation from the capital-discipline culture that defined the prior sixty years.

The Case for the Bet

The central idea is that Berkshire's management culture, anchored in the float-powered compounding engine built over decades, will continue to reinvest retained earnings and acquired float into high-returning businesses and securities at rates exceeding what a passive allocation could achieve. That edge holds as long as the following conditions remain intact:

  1. Book value per share growth meets or exceeds the MSCI World total return over any rolling five-year period. This is the primary signal that the compounding advantage is real and ongoing. If book value per Class B equivalent share grows at a lower rate than the global equity index for five or more consecutive years, it signals that the structural edge has been exhausted — and exit condition one fires.

Latest reading: Shareholders' equity grew 10.5% in the full year 2025, from $649 billion to $717 billion, and rose a further $9.8 billion in the first three months of 2026 to $727 billion. Book value per Class B equivalent share stood at approximately $337 at 31 March 2026. No five-year trailing period shows Berkshire underperforming the MSCI World on this measure.

  1. The insurance float grows or holds flat, and its average cost remains at or below zero. Float is the structural financing advantage. Shrinkage or a shift to positive cost would mean Berkshire's capital is no longer effectively free, eroding the return advantage over time.

Latest reading: Float reached $176.9 billion at 31 March 2026, up approximately $500 million from year-end 2025. The average cost of float was negative in both the first quarter of 2026 and the same period of 2025, meaning the insurance operations generated an underwriting profit while holding the capital. Condition intact.

  1. Capital allocation remains disciplined — no single acquisition above $20 billion at more than 1.5 times the target's book value, and no subsequent goodwill impairment exceeding $5 billion. This is the Abel-specific test: the conditions that would signal a departure from Buffett-era discipline.

Latest reading: Berkshire acquired OxyChem from Occidental Petroleum for approximately $9.5 billion, effective 2 January 2026. The transaction is well below the $20 billion threshold, and no goodwill impairment has been flagged. Total goodwill on the balance sheet stood at $83.2 billion, stable quarter-over-quarter. Condition intact.

  1. No structural culture change — no regular dividend, no break-up of the holding company, no capitulation to activist pressure. The decentralised owner-operator model depends on the holding company behaving as a permanent home for subsidiary businesses, not as a vehicle for financial engineering.

Latest reading: No dividend has been declared. The acquisition of OxyChem extends the subsidiary portfolio in the established pattern. Buybacks in the first quarter of 2026 were characterised by management as "relatively minor" — approximately $235 million total, with zero in January and February — consistent with price discipline near fair value rather than a policy change. Condition intact.

  1. The Share Price remains below 2.0 times book value. Above this threshold, the valuation fully prices in optimistic compounding and the opportunity cost versus a passive index becomes clearly negative. This is exit condition four.

Latest reading: The price-to-book multiple at the 2026-05-04 filing date stood at approximately 1.47 times, using year-end 2025 equity of $717 billion and implied shares of approximately 2,113 million Class B equivalent. The Q1 2026 equity of $727 billion would reduce the ratio marginally further. Well inside the 2.0 times exit threshold. Condition intact.

The Share Price

At $500, Berkshire's shares trade at a price-to-book ratio of approximately 1.47 times — modestly above the 1.45 times fair-value anchor, which puts the share price at fair value. The valuation band runs from 1.25 times book (the buy edge, at $425) to 1.75 times book (the trim edge, at $594), with a fourth tier at 2.0 times book (the exit edge, at $662). The current price sits in the middle of the hold band: 17.6% above the buy edge and 15.8% below the trim edge. There is no valuation trigger in either direction.

For context, the price-to-book ratio compares the market's price for each share to the net book value of Berkshire's assets attributable to that share — a measure that captures the entire asset base (operating businesses, equity portfolio, and cash) in a single number.

Put plainly: the market is pricing Berkshire's asset base at roughly what the investment case calls fair value for a business of this quality. A buyer today is not getting a discount; neither is an existing holder sitting on a signal to reduce the position. The operative posture is to hold and let the compounding continue.

What's Wobbling

The single wobble flagged in the latest quarterly filing is GEICO's combined ratio (the percentage of premiums that goes to claims and expenses — a number below 100% signals a profit, above 100% a loss). GEICO's combined ratio in the first three months of 2026 rose to 87.3%, up 7.5 percentage points from the same period in 2025. The deterioration reflects higher claims frequencies in bodily injury coverage and higher average claim severities across both bodily injury and property damage.

That reading warrants attention but does not cross any written exit condition. Three mitigants: first, the year-ago comparison is artificially suppressed — the first quarter of 2025 included $860 million in after-tax catastrophe losses from the Southern California wildfires, which pushed the 2025 combined ratio to an unsustainably low level that the business could not realistically repeat. Second, GEICO remains profitable at 87.3%. Third, premiums earned grew 4.0% year-over-year, suggesting the business is not losing pricing power. The condition to watch between now and the second-quarter 2026 filing is whether the combined ratio stabilises below 90%. A sustained reading above 90% for two consecutive quarters would move this from a wobble to a the Case for the Bet concern.

Share repurchases were "relatively minor" in the quarter — approximately $235 million total, with nothing in January or February. This is not a structural culture change (exit condition three is not triggered). It is the expected behaviour from a management team that will not repurchase shares at prices above intrinsic value, and a near-fair-value ratio of 1.47 times book does not clear that bar.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • GEICO's underwriting deteriorates persistently. If the combined ratio climbs above 90% and stays there for multiple quarters — driven by Progressive's telematics advantage, rising attorney involvement in claims, or a failure of GEICO's own DriveEasy usage-based programme to take hold — the float pillar begins to cost money rather than earning it. The combined ratio would need to revert to approximately 97% (the pre-turnaround level) to materially impair the investment case.
  • Abel makes a value-destroying acquisition. A transaction above $20 billion at more than 1.5 times the target's book value, or one that subsequently requires a goodwill write-down above $5 billion, would be the clearest signal that the capital-discipline culture has not survived the leadership transition. The OxyChem acquisition at approximately $9.5 billion suggests the discipline is intact so far.
  • Berkshire Hathaway Energy's wildfire liability escalates beyond the subsidiary's resources. The non-recourse structure of Berkshire Hathaway Energy means that BHE's liabilities cannot propagate to the parent holding company — but that protection has limits. A scenario in which BHE's wildfire settlements or judgements exceed its standalone financial capacity could still produce a material adverse event, even if it falls short of threatening the consolidated enterprise.
  • The $339 billion cash position is deployed at poor terms. Berkshire's large cash holding is an advantage when markets dislocate — and a drag when they do not. If management deploys the T-bill portfolio into acquisitions or equities at inflated prices, the structural advantage of patient capital accumulation is squandered.
  • The general insurance market hardens and Berkshire Hathaway Reinsurance Group underestimates long-tail reserves. The reinsurance book holds approximately $152 billion in reserves; a 1% estimation error represents $1.5 billion in surprise losses. The track record on reserve adequacy is strong, but catastrophe-heavy years or structural shifts in liability litigation can catch even disciplined underwriters off guard.

Gaps in the Picture

Two data limitations affect the valuation calculation and are worth naming plainly.

First, Berkshire's multi-class share structure — Class A shares convertible at 1,500 to one Class B share — means that the standardised company-data filings do not report consolidated diluted share count in a form that maps cleanly to the Class B price. The share count used in the price-to-book calculation is inferred from market capitalisation divided by the current share price, yielding approximately 2,113 million Class B equivalent shares. The 2026-Q1 filing's notes state approximately 2,157 million Class B equivalent shares at 31 March 2026 — a difference of about 2%, which is immaterial to the band determination but worth noting as a source of minor imprecision.

Second, the equity per share in the valuation context uses the full-year 2025 balance-sheet figure of $717 billion rather than the Q1 2026 figure of $727 billion. The difference is $9.8 billion, which at 2,157 million shares represents approximately $4.50 per share of additional book value — small relative to the $500 share price, and it would shift the computed price-to-book ratio from 1.47 to 1.45, landing right on the fair-value anchor. The Q1 2026 figures, when the next validation runs, will close this gap.

The Rules

The conditions below are stated as they stand in the pre-committed investment plan. Six rules govern what action follows from each combination of thesis state and price.

  • If the thesis is confirmed — all monitored conditions are supporting the investment case, no exit trigger has fired — the rule is to hold and let the business compound. No action is required.
  • If the thesis is weakening — one or more conditions are deteriorating without triggering an exit condition — the rule is to stop adding to the position. The specific weakening driver is re-evaluated at the next quarterly filing before any further decision is made.
  • If the thesis is broken — an exit condition has fired — the rule is to exit the full position within 30 days. No averaging down.
  • If the share price falls below the buy edge — the price-to-book ratio drops below 1.25 times, implying a price around $425 — the rule is to confirm the thesis is intact first, then add to the position.
  • If the share price rises above the trim edge — the price-to-book ratio exceeds 1.75 times, implying a price around $594 — the rule is to trim 25–33% of the position, harvesting the valuation premium while retaining the core holding.
  • If the share price rises above the exit edge — the price-to-book ratio exceeds 2.0 times, implying a price around $662 — the rule is to exit the full position. At that price the market is pricing in roughly 10 years of base-case compounding immediately; the implied forward return falls below what a diversified equity allocation would offer, and reallocation is preferred. No re-entry within 90 days.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
