Recent Development

The three months ending in March 2026 produced the strongest quarterly confirmation of the Amazon thesis in several years. Amazon's cloud division — the part of the business that now accounts for the majority of the company's operating profits — grew its revenue 28% compared with the same period a year earlier, reaching $37.6 billion for the quarter. Alongside that headline, the company disclosed a $364 billion contracted backlog of future cloud commitments from enterprise customers, with an average remaining life of five and a half years. A separate $100 billion expansion of the existing agreement with OpenAI was also announced. These are unusually concrete signals of demand.

Advertising services reached $17.2 billion for the quarter, up 24% year-on-year, and drove the bulk of the improvement in operating income in both the North America and International retail segments. The international segment itself turned in a 3.6% operating margin — its highest level in the dataset — compared with 3.0% a year earlier. Four of the nine thesis conditions read as confirmed; four others read as mixed rather than broken.

What remains open is the capital spending question. Cash capital expenditures for the quarter were $43.2 billion — more than Amazon spent in the whole of 2021. Management explicitly stated that spending is expected to continue rising in 2026. The trailing-twelve-month cash generated after all investment spending compressed to roughly $1.2 billion, from $25.9 billion a year prior. That compression is the deliberate price of the AI infrastructure build, and exit condition four is structured specifically to catch the case where that price is never recovered: if consolidated capital spending remains above $120 billion in both FY2027 and FY2028 without commensurate cloud revenue acceleration, the condition fires. For now, with cloud revenue accelerating at 28%, the condition is not in view. The share price today sits in the upper half of the hold band and the current reading supports the verdict described in the Bottom Line.

About Amazon

Amazon makes most of its money from three distinct engines that happen to share a corporate roof. The first is the North America retail and logistics platform — a marketplace where hundreds of millions of shoppers buy goods from Amazon directly or from third-party merchants, with Amazon handling fulfilment, delivery, and payment. That platform generated $426 billion in revenue in the twelve months to December 2025 and is, in itself, one of the largest retail operations on the planet. The second engine is Amazon Web Services, the cloud computing division that rents computing power and software services to businesses of all sizes, from start-ups to governments. AWS generated $129 billion in revenue and $46 billion in operating profit in those same twelve months — the source of 57% of the company's total operating income on 18% of its revenue. The third engine is a digital advertising business built on the first-party data generated by the marketplace: $69 billion in revenue in FY2025, growing at 22% per year, largely invisible to consumers but increasingly central to how consumer goods companies reach shoppers online.

The three engines are not independent. AWS subsidises the economics of the Prime subscription bundle (free same-day or next-day delivery, streaming video, music), which holds shoppers inside the Amazon ecosystem, which generates the transaction data that makes the advertising platform valuable, which funds further AWS investment. This flywheel has been operating for more than a decade, and the unit economics of each component are still maturing: Prime has more room to expand internationally, advertising has margins that could rise further, and AWS is only beginning to absorb the computing demand that the proliferation of large language models and AI applications is expected to generate.

The Case for the Bet

The bet on Amazon is that three compounding profit pools are each well below maturity and that the capital being deployed today — heavy as it is — is earning returns that will be visible in the FY2027 and FY2028 results:

  1. Cloud revenue growth stays above 15% for any two consecutive quarters. The thesis requires AWS to sustain 18–20% or higher annual growth while operating margins hold above 30% over time. Growth below 15% for two consecutive quarters would signal that the AI computing demand the business is spending to serve has not materialised at the pace the investment assumes. The threshold for exit is not the same as the thesis target — the thesis requires sustained 18%+; the exit requires only two consecutive quarters below 15%.

Latest reading: Q1 2026 (three months ending March 2026): AWS revenue +28% year-on-year to $37.6 billion. This is well above the 18% thesis target and remote from the 15% exit threshold. The $364 billion contracted backlog extends visibility for multiple years.

  1. Cloud operating margin stays above 28%. An AWS operating margin below 28% for two consecutive quarters, absent a clear one-off charge, would signal that the economics of the AI infrastructure investment are not converting to profit as expected.

Latest reading: Q1 2026: AWS operating margin was 37.7% ($14.2 billion operating income on $37.6 billion revenue), down from 39.4% a year earlier. The compression of roughly 170 basis points (1.7 percentage points) reflects infrastructure depreciation running ahead of revenue — the margin sits 970 basis points above the 28% exit floor. Exit condition two is not approached.

  1. Advertising revenue growth stays in double digits for any given two-year window. Advertising growing at single-digit rates for two consecutive years would signal that the third compounding engine has saturated — that the transaction-intent data advantage is no longer producing pricing power with advertisers.

Latest reading: Q1 2026: advertising services revenue was $17.2 billion, up 23.9% year-on-year. Exit condition five requires two full years of single-digit growth; the current rate is more than twice that threshold.

  1. Capital spending normalises by FY2027 and FY2028. Consolidated capital expenditure remaining above $120 billion in both FY2027 and FY2028 without commensurate cloud revenue acceleration would signal that the AI infrastructure investment has overshot demand.

Latest reading: FY2026 spending is running at an annualised rate above $150 billion. Exit condition four monitors FY2027 and FY2028 — the current FY2026 reading does not trigger it, and AWS revenue (+28%) is accelerating alongside the spending. The first material test of this condition comes at the FY2027 annual filing.

  1. No court-ordered structural remedy in the FTC antitrust case. A ruling requiring Amazon to separate its businesses — for instance, forcing divestiture of AWS from the retail operation — would be an immediate exit regardless of any other metric.

Latest reading: The latest quarterly filing references ongoing antitrust litigation in generic risk-factor language. No structural remedy, consent decree, or new trial timeline has been disclosed. Exit condition three is not triggered.

  1. No material weakness in internal controls, and no unexpected auditor change. A material weakness disclosure or an unexpected replacement of the auditor (Ernst & Young, in place since 1996) would be an immediate exit, as both signal a risk to reported financial integrity that cannot be evaluated from outside.

Latest reading: No material weakness disclosed. EY remains auditor. Beneish M-Score of −2.663 (well below the −1.78 level associated with potential earnings manipulation) confirms no statistical signal of accounting irregularity.

  1. No abnormal insider selling by Jeff Bezos. A single Form 144 filing from Bezos disclosing planned sales above 40 million shares would signal an unusual acceleration beyond the established pre-planned disposal cadence — worth noting but not actionable in isolation.

Latest reading: The latest filing discloses no new trading arrangements under the insider rules. A separate Form 144 filed in May 2026 covers 25 million shares — below the 40 million threshold.

  1. The owner-earnings yield (a measure of how much the business earns in cash relative to its market price) stays above 3.0%. A yield below 3.0% would mean the valuation has fully priced in optimistic long-run growth, leaving no return buffer.

Latest reading: Owner-earnings yield is 4.2% at the current share price of $250. Exit condition eight fires at 3.0% — still 120 basis points away from trigger. The trim edge (4.0% yield, corresponding to a share price of $263) is closer.

The Share Price

The current share price of $250 sits in the upper half of the hold band — between the fair-value anchor of $176 and the trim edge of $263. In plain terms: the price is above what the rules assign as fair value for this business, but not yet at the level that triggers a trim. The market is pricing Amazon's shares 42% above the fair value computed from the owner-earnings yield method. The most direct way to state what that gap means: a buyer at $250 today is paying a price that implies roughly five years of base-case earnings growth delivered instantly, before any new compounding begins. The price is not irrational — the underlying business is doing what it said it would do — but it is not a bargain. Why the Market Sees It Differently

The market's price of $250 sits 42% above the internally computed fair value of $176. That is a large divergence, and the market is not obviously wrong.

The direction of the divergence is that the market is richer than the valuation model — the price implies a long-run owner-earnings growth rate of about 8.9% per year, compared with the 8.0% figure embedded in the fair value calculation. That 0.9 percentage-point spread sounds narrow, but compounded over a decade it translates into a materially larger earnings base and, accordingly, a higher present value.

Three hypotheses could explain why the market is willing to pay a 42% premium. The first is that AWS demand is structurally larger than the base model assumes: cloud computing growth above 25% per year for several more years, driven by enterprise adoption of AI services and the incremental revenue locked in by the $364 billion contracted backlog, would justify a higher long-run growth rate on its own. The most recent quarterly data supports this hypothesis directly — 28% growth alongside a freshly expanded OpenAI commitment worth $100 billion over eight years is not consistent with the demand picture of a decelerating business.

The second hypothesis is that the AWS margin compression visible in Q1 2026 (37.7%, down from 39.4% a year earlier) is temporary rather than structural. If the infrastructure investment currently depressing margins is followed by the same revenue-conversion pattern as the prior capital cycle, margins will re-expand as AI capacity fills — and the permanent earning power of the business is higher than today's compressed figure suggests. The current filing supports this reading: management attributes the compression explicitly to deliberate infrastructure investment, and the absolute margin level remains far above the exit floor.

The third hypothesis involves optionality the owner-earnings yield model does not capture: Amazon holds stakes in Anthropic and OpenAI (combined carrying value approximately $48 billion at March 2026 — the Anthropic preferred stock and the $15 billion OpenAI Series C, against an additional $35 billion equity commitment to OpenAI not yet funded), and has structured both investments so that AWS cloud services are the delivery platform for whatever AI workloads those companies generate. Neither of those positions appears in a cash-flow model until the revenue arrives. The market may be pricing that optionality explicitly.

Two of the three hypotheses are supported by evidence in the most recent filing. The gap between the market price and the internal fair value may reflect a base-growth assumption that is modestly under-calibrated — not a market in error, but a market with a slightly different view of the long-run rate. Whether 8.0% or 8.9% is closer to correct will be answered by the FY2027 AWS revenue line.

What's Wobbling

Four of the nine thesis conditions read as mixed rather than confirmed at the most recent filing. None of the four crosses a written exit condition, but each deserves monitoring.

The first wobble is the AWS operating margin. At 37.7% in the three months ending March 2026, the margin has compressed about 170 basis points from the 39.4% it held a year earlier. The cause is deliberate: infrastructure depreciation is running at $7.3 billion per quarter and rising, ahead of the revenue that infrastructure is intended to enable. The exit condition fires only at two consecutive quarters below 28% — nearly 1,000 basis points below where the margin sits today — so the current compression does not approach the line. It still warrants watching at each quarterly filing.

The second wobble is capital spending. The trailing-twelve-month cash generated after all investment fell to $1.2 billion in the period ending March 2026, against $25.9 billion in the year-earlier period. The annualised spending rate is above $150 billion. Management has confirmed spending will continue rising in 2026. The thesis-specific exit condition monitors FY2027 and FY2028 results — not the current rate — but the current data establishes the baseline from which those tests will be measured.

The third wobble is capital allocation discipline. Amazon has an active buyback authorisation of $6.1 billion, established in 2022, and has not used it in three years of rising profits. Over the same period the company invested $15 billion in OpenAI preferred stock, committed a further $35 billion, and issued $53.8 billion in new debt. The thesis characterises capital allocation as "acceptable but not excellent," and the pattern visible in the most recent filing is consistent with that description. No exit condition applies to capital return directly.

The fourth wobble is marketplace exposure to trade policy. The filing explicitly names China-based sellers as a significant source of third-party and advertising revenue and flags tariff policy as a risk. North America operating income rose 41% in the same quarter, suggesting resilience so far — but the disclosure language on China-seller concentration is more specific than in prior filings, which is a signal worth noting. Exit condition five monitors advertising growth into single digits over two years; the current 24% growth rate is nowhere near that threshold.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Cloud computing loses ground in the AI workload race. Microsoft Azure and Google Cloud are growing at comparable rates and investing at comparable scale. If enterprises route their AI computing requirements to these platforms rather than AWS, cloud growth could decelerate faster than the base case assumes. The exit threshold — two consecutive quarters below 15% growth — exists precisely to catch this scenario before it becomes permanent.
  • Capital spending creates stranded assets. Amazon is spending at a rate that, if AI demand does not materialise at the scale forecast, would leave a very large asset base earning below-market returns. The FY2022–2024 precedent suggests the company can navigate this — it has before — but the current scale of investment is larger than any prior cycle.
  • FTC antitrust proceedings produce a structural remedy. A court-ordered separation of AWS from the retail and advertising business would be a binary event with no partial hedge available. The current filing notes ongoing litigation in generic terms; the trial timeline in fall 2026 brings this risk closer in calendar terms.
  • AI equity stakes reverse. Amazon's combined Anthropic and OpenAI preferred-stock positions carried at approximately $48 billion on the balance sheet at March 2026 are marked to fair value each quarter. A large downward revaluation would depress reported earnings materially in the quarter it occurs, even though it does not affect operating cash flow. The financial-integrity exit conditions are not triggered by mark-to-market moves, but a reversal would cloud the quarterly narrative.
  • Tariff and trade policy disrupts the marketplace. A sustained escalation in tariffs on goods imported from China — or a change to customs policy governing low-value cross-border shipments — could reduce the volume of sales by China-based merchants on the marketplace, cutting both third-party seller services revenue and advertising revenue simultaneously.

Gaps in the Picture

  • The owner-earnings figure used in valuation (the measure that translates the business's cash generation into a share price) deducts all depreciation as a proxy for the cost of maintaining the current asset base. Amazon does not disclose how much of its capital spending is for maintenance versus growth. Using depreciation as the proxy is conservative — it overstates the maintenance cost and understates the true earning power — but the figure used here is the one the valuation methodology requires.
  • The fair-value calculation assigns a 6.0% owner-earnings yield as fair value, implying a share price of about $176. The separately produced fair-value band model, which uses a different methodology (peer earnings-power comparisons and three scenario midpoints), produces a base-case central estimate roughly three times higher, at $533. These two methods have not been reconciled. The share price ($250) sits between the two estimates. The valuation used throughout these sections is the 6.0% yield method, which is the one the re-validation contract specifies — but the divergence is material and has been flagged for review.
  • The risk-free rate used in the score formula is read from a portfolio-level market-data file rather than a holding-specific source, as the per-holding field is not populated for this company. This introduces no material error — the rate used (4.57%) was current as of the most recent refresh on 2026-07-12 — but is noted for transparency.
  • The reconciliation note comparing the probability-weighted scenario return to the owner-earnings-yield return was suppressed: the base-case scenario implies a positive return from the current price, while the current price already sits above the internally computed fair value. This apparent contradiction reflects two different time horizons and two different models in use simultaneously; it does not affect the verdict.

The Rules

If the thesis is confirmed and the price stays inside the hold band: hold the position. No action. Continue compounding.

If the thesis shows signs of weakening — a driver moves from confirming to mixed or challenging, without yet crossing a written exit condition: do not add. Watch the next two quarterly filings before deciding. One quarter of deteriorating data is noise; two consecutive quarters of the same reading is a pattern.

If the thesis breaks — any written exit condition fires: exit the full position within 30 days. No averaging down. AWS revenue or margin exits require confirmation across two consecutive quarters before any action; an FTC structural remedy or an auditor change is an immediate exit with no waiting period.

If the price falls to or below $140 and the thesis is confirmed intact: add to the position in tranches over one to two quarters.

If the price rises to $263 or above: trim 25–33% of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.

If the price rises to $421 or above: exit the full position. At that reading, the market would be pricing in roughly 11 years of base-case compounding instantly; redeploy proceeds at the next cheap-edge crossing or into a cheaper alternative. No re-entry within 90 days.
