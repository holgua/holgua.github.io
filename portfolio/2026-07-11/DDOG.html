About Datadog

Datadog sells software that lets engineering teams watch what is happening inside their computer systems in real time. A company's digital infrastructure — the servers, software containers, cloud services, and applications that run everything from a retail website to a mobile banking app — generates a continuous stream of signals: a login that took too long, a database query that failed, a server filling up. Datadog collects all of that data, correlates it across systems and cloud providers, and presents it to the engineers responsible for keeping things running. The product sits in a category known as observability: the ability to infer the health of a system from the signals it produces.

The business model is usage-based: customers pay in proportion to how much data they send through the platform. A company that starts by monitoring one application tends to add more as it sees the value, then extends to security monitoring, AI workload tracking, and cost management. Each additional product generates more revenue from the same account without requiring a new sales effort. The best single measure of this expansion dynamic is the net revenue retention rate — the percentage of last year's contracted revenue that this year's customer base accounts for, including its additional spending. At 120% or above, the existing customer base alone would compound revenue by 20% a year even if no new contracts were ever signed.

As of the three months ending in March 2026, Datadog had approximately 33,200 customers, of whom around 4,550 spent $100,000 or more a year; those accounts generate 90% of total contracted revenue. The company was founded in 2010 by Olivier Pomel and Alexis Lê-Quôc, who together hold approximately 35% of voting power. There are no dividends and no share buybacks; all cash is held in a $4.5 billion portfolio of short-term securities.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that Datadog's switching-cost franchise — built by deploying its monitoring software on every server, container, and application in a customer's environment — makes the business structurally difficult to dislodge, sustaining the compounding of revenue within the existing customer base. Several specific conditions must stay intact for that logic to hold:

  1. Net revenue retention stays above 112%. The rate at which existing customers expand their annual spending — the engine of the land-and-expand model — must hold above 112%. Below that level for two consecutive quarters, customers would be reducing rather than expanding, signalling that the switching-cost advantage has eroded.

Latest reading: Low-120s per cent trailing twelve months as of March 2026, up from the high-110s a year earlier. The expansion engine is accelerating, not slowing.

  1. Revenue grows at 20% or more year on year. Growth below 20% for two consecutive quarters would suggest competitive displacement or a technology-budget contraction outrunning the base-case assumption.

Latest reading: 32% in the three months ending in March 2026, up from 27% a year earlier — comfortably ahead of the 20% threshold.

  1. The enterprise customer count continues to grow. A sequential decline in the count of accounts spending $100,000 or more annually would signal that the premium-product proposition is losing ground to cheaper alternatives.

Latest reading: 4,550 customers as of March 2026, up 21% from 3,770 a year earlier, now accounting for 90% of total contracted revenue.

  1. Gross margin holds above 77%. If the cost of delivering the service — principally cloud infrastructure hosting — rises faster than the prices Datadog charges, the per-customer economics deteriorate. A sustained drop below 77% for two consecutive quarters signals structural impairment.

Latest reading: 79% in the three months ending in March 2026, unchanged from the same period a year earlier.

  1. Cash generation stays above 25% of revenue. Free cash flow — the cash the business produces after operating costs and capital investment — as a share of revenue must remain above 25%. A sustained drop below that threshold would indicate that the business is consuming cash faster than it earns it from operations.

Latest reading: 28.7% in the three months ending in March 2026, down from 32.1% a year earlier but above the 25% floor. The compression is noted and is discussed further in What's Wobbling.

  1. Equity compensation stays below 25% of revenue. Datadog pays engineers substantially in shares rather than cash. When equity compensation is subtracted from reported free cash flow, the residual owner earnings are much smaller than the headline figure. A ratio above 25% with no credible downward path signals that per-share value creation is becoming illusory.

Latest reading: 19.6% in the three months ending in March 2026, down from 21.6% a year earlier — revenue is outpacing the equity compensation line.

  1. No acquisition above $2 billion at premium multiples. Datadog has grown through small tuck-in deals — $10 to $220 million per transaction — that slot into the product without straining the business. A single large deal at a rich price would absorb the cash cushion and introduce integration risk the management has not previously faced at that scale.

Latest reading: Two small tuck-in deals completed in the three months ending in March 2026, with combined recorded goodwill of approximately $11 million — within historical pattern.

  1. AI observability remains a product differentiator. Tools for monitoring large language model behaviour, graphics processing unit performance, and AI-agent workflows are the forward wedge of the thesis. The risk is that cloud providers bundle equivalent functionality at zero marginal cost before adoption reaches critical mass.

Latest reading: AI-native customers contributed high-single-digit percentage points to revenue growth in the three months ending in March 2026; 11% of customers now use ten or more products (up from 6%), partly reflecting AI-tool adoption. No impairment to retention from this cohort has been disclosed.

  1. No material data-privacy enforcement action. The monitoring platform moves data across borders and is subject to privacy rules in multiple jurisdictions. A material fine or mandatory market-exit order affecting more than 5% of revenue would be an exit trigger.

Latest reading: Extensive regulatory disclosures in the latest official filing — covering European data protection law, the EU Artificial Intelligence Act, and comparable frameworks in other jurisdictions — but no enforcement actions, fines, or market-exit announcements were noted.

The Share Price

The valuation approach treats each dollar of annual owner earnings — the cash the business generates after deducting equity compensation — as a yield on the market price, the same way a fixed-income investor reads a coupon. High yield means the price is low; a low yield means it is expensive.

The fair-value yield is 2.5%. The buy edge — where the yield is high enough to represent genuine value — sits at 4.0%. The trim edge — where the yield has fallen far enough that the shares are no longer worth holding at full size — sits at 1.5%.

Datadog's current owner earnings yield is 0.27%: annual owner earnings of approximately $250 million on a market value of approximately $92 billion. The $250 million is the company's $1 billion of free cash flow in the year to January 2026, after deducting $751 million of equity compensation. At $258, the share price is well past the trim edge of $47 and far above the fair-value anchor of $28. The gap between the current price and the yield-based fair value is 817%.

A note on the two anchors: the $28 figure comes from applying the 2.5% yield threshold to owner earnings post-compensation. A separate scenario model, which uses projected free cash flow before the equity-compensation deduction, produces a central estimate of $103. Both figures are discussed in Gaps in the Picture.

What's Wobbling

Only one driver came in softer than the thesis assumes: cash generation as a share of revenue.

In the three months ending in March 2026, free cash flow was 28.7% of revenue, down from 32.1% in the same period a year earlier. The compression came from a $41.8 million rise in cloud infrastructure hosting costs and higher staffing expenses as the company scaled. The 25% floor at which the concern would become structural was not breached, and the January-to-March quarter is historically the most expensive for infrastructure onboarding — the compression reflects a timing pattern rather than a structural deterioration.

No exit condition is close to triggering. The net revenue retention rate moved in the right direction. Gross margin held flat. Equity compensation as a share of revenue fell. The sole departure from a fully clean reading is the timing mix of costs against revenue in the quarter — a pattern that has reversed in subsequent periods in prior years.

What Could Still Go Wrong

The bear case is that today's share price requires a level of sustained growth and margin expansion that only the most optimistic forward scenario delivers:

  • Net revenue retention falls toward 110%, signalling that existing customers are scaling back rather than expanding. Cloud providers' own monitoring tools are competitively priced for single-cloud deployments; any consolidation of workloads into one provider's ecosystem removes the primary reason to pay for Datadog's multi-cloud correlation.
  • Revenue growth decelerates to around 12% a year, driven by mid-market tool substitution and spending optimisation by large AI-native customers who have reached a natural plateau of telemetry complexity. At that growth rate, the business is worth considerably less than its current market value under any reasonable assumptions.
  • Equity compensation stays sticky above 20% of revenue, keeping the gap between reported cash generation and true owner earnings wide while the share count gradually dilutes existing holders.
  • A large acquisition disrupts the disciplined track record. Datadog has spent $10 to $220 million per deal historically, slotting specialist teams into the product without straining the business. One acquisition above $2 billion at a full-price multiple would absorb the cash reserves and introduce integration complexity the management has not navigated at that scale.
  • Regulatory friction on cross-border data flows increases compliance costs and shrinks the addressable market in Europe or in data-sovereignty-sensitive markets in Asia-Pacific, reducing the revenue ceiling that today's share price effectively assumes.

Gaps in the Picture

Two fair-value anchors coexist: the yield-method figure of $28 (derived from the 2.5% owner-earnings-yield threshold applied to earnings after equity compensation) and the discounted-cash-flow scenario central estimate of $103 (derived from projected free cash flow before the equity-compensation deduction). A future schema update will resolve the divergence. Until then, the $28 figure drives the TL;DR thresholds and the cost-basis table in the re-validation.

That owner earnings calculation deducts equity compensation from reported free cash flow without separating maintenance capital expenditure from growth capital expenditure. If spending classified as growth is in fact required to sustain existing revenues, the resulting figure understates true earning power, making the $28 fair-value anchor more conservative than it needs to be. The latest official filing does not provide a maintenance-versus-growth expenditure breakdown.

The Rules

Six pre-committed conditions govern the decision:

  1. If the thesis remains confirmed — the holding is watched but not added to. The current owner earnings yield of 0.27% is well into avoid territory at $258. Monitoring focuses on net revenue retention, revenue growth, and AI observability traction quarterly; entry is reconsidered only if the yield rises to the trim-threshold level (approximately $200 per share on an FCF basis — TODO(verify): on an owner-earnings basis, the 1.5% trim threshold implies approximately $47 per share at current post-SBC earnings).

  2. If the thesis weakens — no new position is initiated. If net revenue retention declines toward 115% or revenue growth falls below 20%, reassessment follows within one earnings cycle before any sizing decision. A weakening thesis at this valuation carries strictly negative risk and reward.

  3. If the thesis breaks — no entry. If a position is held, it is closed within 30 days. The specific triggers are: net revenue retention at or below 112% for two consecutive quarters, gross margin below 77% for two consecutive quarters, or equity compensation above 25% of revenue with no credible path downward. No averaging down.

  4. If the share price falls to the buy edge — the earnings yield rises to 4.0% or above (approximately $75 per share at current earnings levels), signalling the stock has corrected to value-investor levels. Before acting, the thesis must be confirmed: retention at or above 115%, cash generation above 25% of revenue, no exit condition triggered. Any initial position is built in two tranches over one quarter.

  5. If the share price stays above the trim edge — no position is initiated. If a position is held, the rules call for trimming 25 to 33 per cent of it. No adding.

  6. If the share price rises to the exit band — the full position is closed, or the holding remains out. No re-entry within 90 days to defuse anchor bias.
