Berkshire: The Bet Still Works

The Bottom Line

The bet is working. Greg Abel's team has continued the pattern Warren Buffett established over six decades: reinvesting retained earnings and insurance float into high-returning businesses at a pace that outstrips what most investors can replicate on their own. The share price sits in the middle of the hold band, at fair value — neither a bargain nor overpriced relative to the company's own history. The one condition that bears watching is the slow erosion of GEICO's competitive standing, where a rival with better pricing technology has been taking ground for the past three years.

About Berkshire

Berkshire Hathaway is a holding company unlike any other. Its main business is property and casualty insurance — GEICO (personal auto), General Re (reinsurance), and several specialty lines — but what makes Berkshire unusual is what it does with the insurance premium float (the money collected from policyholders that hasn't yet been paid out in claims). That float, which stood at roughly $176.9 billion at the end of the first quarter of 2026, costs Berkshire essentially nothing to hold — the underwriting operations have run at a net profit, meaning policyholders' money is free capital. Berkshire invests that capital alongside its own retained earnings across a portfolio that spans BNSF Railway, Berkshire Hathaway Energy (a regulated-utility group spanning six states), Precision Castparts (aerospace components), and a $298 billion portfolio of publicly traded shares in companies including Apple, American Express, Coca-Cola, and Chevron.

On top of all that sits a $369 billion pile of cash and short-dated US Treasury securities — itself roughly as large as the gross domestic product of a mid-sized country. That cash is not inert: it earns roughly 5% pre-tax in Treasury bills while Abel waits for the right acquisition target. The first significant deal under Abel's leadership was the purchase of Occidental Petroleum's chemical subsidiary (OxyChem) for roughly $9.5 billion in January 2026, within the price discipline management has always stated publicly. The business earned $20.1 billion in operating profit in the full year 2025 and another $12.4 billion in the three months ending in March 2026 alone — up 6.5% from the same period a year earlier.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that Gregory Abel and the culture Warren Buffett built will continue to reinvest retained earnings and acquired float into high-returning businesses and securities at rates exceeding what most individual investors could achieve on their own. The position is intended as a long-duration core holding rather than a trade on near-term earnings. For the bet to keep working, four conditions must remain intact:

  1. Book value per share keeps growing faster than a passive global equity index. Berkshire's value is best measured by the growth of shareholders' equity per share over rolling five-year periods. If that growth trails the MSCI World total return for five or more consecutive years, the compounding edge that justifies holding Berkshire above a plain index fund is gone.

  2. Abel keeps Buffett's capital discipline on acquisitions. Specifically, no single transaction exceeding $20 billion at a price more than 1.5 times the book value of the target — or one that subsequently requires a goodwill write-down exceeding $5 billion. A deal of that size and price would signal that the new CEO is paying for growth rather than for value.

  3. The decentralised owner-operator model remains intact. A regular dividend, a break-up, or a capitulation to activist investors would signal that the structure enabling subsidiaries to run as independent, owner-operated businesses has been abandoned. That structure is what attracts the kind of subsidiary CEOs who build Berkshire's compounding engine.

  4. The price-to-book ratio stays below 2.0 times. At that multiple, the market is pricing in so much optimistic compounding that the opportunity cost of holding Berkshire rather than a cheaper passive index becomes clearly negative.

The Rules

The action plan that governs this position was set before the latest filing and applies without modification:

  • If all the conditions above are working: hold with no action. Let the compounding run.
  • If one or more conditions are showing cracks but no exit condition has fired: do not add to the position. Revisit after the next quarterly filing.
  • If an exit condition fires: close the full position within 30 days. No averaging down.
  • If the price falls below $417 and the thesis is confirmed: add to the position.
  • If the price rises above $584: trim 25–33% of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.
  • If the price rises above $651: the standing orders call for a full exit, with a 90-day cooling-off period before any re-entry, to defuse the psychological pull of a familiar name.

The Conditions Today

Book value growth. Shareholders' equity grew 10.5% in the full year 2025, rising to $717.4 billion, and then a further $9.8 billion in the three months ending in March 2026 to $727.2 billion — implying a per-Class-B-share book value of approximately $337. Abel's team is compounding retained earnings at a rate consistent with the thesis; no consecutive-year underperformance relative to a passive index is in sight.

Insurance float and underwriting cost. The insurance float reached approximately $176.9 billion in the first quarter of 2026, and the cost of holding it remained negative — Berkshire was paid to hold it rather than charged for it. Total pre-tax underwriting profit across all insurance lines was $2.3 billion in the quarter, up from $1.7 billion a year earlier. The free-leverage pillar is intact.

BNSF railway. BNSF generated revenues of $6.0 billion and pre-tax earnings of $1.8 billion in the three months ending in March 2026 — revenues up 4.8% and earnings up 13.4% from the same period a year earlier. No regulatory action on a potential Union Pacific / Norfolk Southern merger emerged during the quarter.

Berkshire Hathaway Energy and wildfire risk. In April 2026, an Oregon appeals court reversed the first-phase jury verdict that had held PacifiCorp liable for wildfire damages, materially reducing BHE's worst-case legal tail. Separately, PacifiCorp agreed to sell its Washington state operations to Portland General Electric for approximately $1.9 billion in cash. BHE's quarterly earnings were $744 million, up 1.4%.

Abel's capital discipline. The OxyChem acquisition in January 2026 came in at roughly $9.5 billion — well below the $20 billion threshold and at a price management characterised as rational. Share repurchases were approximately $235 million for the quarter, modest at a price near fair value, which is precisely the discipline the thesis depends on. No acquisition above 1.5 times book value of a target has been announced.

Management succession. Greg Abel's name appears frequently in operational context without any leadership disruption or succession-related disclosure in the latest quarterly filing.

The Share Price

The current share price, $484, is essentially at fair value. The valuation method used here compares the current price to book value (shareholders' equity divided by the number of shares outstanding): the ratio today is 1.45 times, against a fair-value anchor of 1.45 times. The cheap-edge price — the level at which the rules call for adding — is $417, which corresponds to a price-to-book ratio of 1.25. The trim-edge is $584 (a ratio of 1.75). The exit edge, where the market is pricing in roughly ten years of base-case compounding instantaneously, is $651 (a ratio of 2.0 times book value).

That 1.25–1.75 range is not arbitrary. Berkshire's own historical trading over the past five years has put the stock at a tenth-percentile multiple of 1.32 and a ninetieth-percentile multiple of 1.73 — so the band tracks reasonably closely to where the stock has actually traded in recent memory. At 1.45 times today, the price is effectively in the middle of that range, neither a bargain nor stretched.

The note in the source data is worth surfacing: because Berkshire's peculiar share structure makes it difficult to extract the share count from standard financial databases, the calculation above uses a market-capitalisation-implied share count rather than a directly reported figure. The best available cross-check — management's own statement in the Q1 2026 filing that per-share book value tracks per-share intrinsic value growth — suggests the approach is sound, but the precise ratio carries a small uncertainty.

What's Wobbling

GEICO's combined ratio. GEICO's combined ratio (the share of premium income consumed by claims and expenses, where anything below 100% means profitable underwriting) rose to 87.3% in the three months ending in March 2026, up from 79.8% in the same quarter a year earlier. That 7.5 percentage-point swing reflects higher claims frequency and severity — more accidents, more expensive repairs — and dragged GEICO's quarterly underwriting profit down to $1.4 billion from $2.2 billion. The context matters: the first quarter of 2025 had near-zero catastrophic weather losses, making it an unusually clean benchmark. GEICO is still solidly profitable at 87.3%, and the aggregate underwriting result across all insurance operations (including General Re and the reinsurance group) improved year on year because the rest of the portfolio had a strong quarter.

The longer wobble is competitive. Progressive Insurance, GEICO's largest rival in personal auto, has spent the past decade building a telematics-based (usage-and-driver-behaviour data) pricing model that can select risks more precisely than GEICO's historical actuarial approach. GEICO management has confirmed it is closing the gap on telematics capability, but closing the gap is not the same as leading. A multi-year drift in market share toward Progressive would eventually show up in policies-in-force data — which is not disclosed in quarterly filings and must be monitored via the annual shareholder letter and industry data.

Share repurchases were modest. Berkshire bought back approximately $235 million of its own shares in the first quarter of 2026 — management described the pace as "relatively minor amounts." At a price-to-book ratio of 1.45, buybacks at any material scale would represent Buffett's and Abel's own rule (buy when the stock is clearly below intrinsic value) being violated. The low repurchase pace is therefore evidence of capital discipline, not a departure from it. But a reader who was hoping for more aggressive buybacks — particularly given the $369 billion cash pile — may take a different view. The company could, in principle, buy back far more stock at today's price; it is choosing not to.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Abel deploys the cash pile badly. The $369 billion in cash and Treasury bills is the most consequential strategic variable Berkshire has held in decades. If Abel uses it for a large acquisition at an elevated price — the internal watch signal is any deal above $20 billion at more than 1.5 times book value of the target — it would confirm that the cultural discipline defining the capital allocation edge has been abandoned. This is the single largest unresolved question overhanging the position.

  • GEICO loses meaningful market share to Progressive. If the telematics gap does not close and GEICO's policies-in-force trend turns decisively negative over the next two to three years, the insurance float would eventually shrink rather than grow, removing the free-leverage pillar that funds Berkshire's entire investment model.

  • A large catastrophe event hits the reinsurance book. An insurance event exceeding approximately $15 billion in insured losses would pressure both underwriting earnings and the float itself. Berkshire has survived multiple large catastrophe years without threatening the enterprise, but the size of the portfolio means a truly catastrophic event is always a tail risk.

  • The $298 billion equity portfolio suffers a severe drawdown. Two-thirds of the publicly traded portfolio is concentrated in five positions. A sustained bear market in large-cap US equities would produce large GAAP losses (purely accounting, per the mark-to-market rule) but would also impair the investment income funding the operating subsidiaries.

  • BHE wildfire liability is reinstated on appeal. The Oregon appeals court victory is not necessarily final; further appeals or new litigation arising from subsequent wildfire seasons could revive the tail liability that the recent reversal reduced.

Gaps in the Picture

The share count used to compute the price-to-book ratio is derived from the market capitalisation divided by the share price — a workaround, because Berkshire's dual-class share structure (Class A and Class B shares with very different nominal prices) is not cleanly extracted by standard financial data tools. Management's own filing language confirms that per-share book value and per-share intrinsic value have been tracking each other closely; but any reader who wants to verify the exact ratio should use the per-share book value figure that Berkshire discloses directly in each quarterly filing rather than the derived number here.

The policies-in-force trend for GEICO — one of the most important leading indicators of whether the competitive wobble is accelerating or stabilising — is not reported in quarterly filings. It appears only in the annual shareholder letter. The GEICO competitive-position condition therefore cannot be fully evaluated until the next annual letter, and the quarterly monitoring relies on the combined ratio as a proxy.

Gregory Abel's compensation structure under the new CEO arrangements is not surfaced in the source data. This does not change the investment case, but it is a known gap in the management-assessment section.
