PayPal: The Bet Still Works

The Bottom Line

The bet is working: PayPal is generating more cash per share than the market gives it credit for, and none of the five written conditions that would force an exit has fired. The share price sits at the buy edge, modestly below fair value — meaning the stock is close to the threshold where the rules call for adding, not trimming. The one condition to watch is branded-checkout share, which PayPal stopped disclosing separately in its most recent quarterly filing while a securities class action alleges the company hid problems with that very metric.

About PayPal

PayPal runs a two-sided digital payments network — a system that connects over 439 million account-holders with millions of merchants across roughly 200 countries. When someone pays with PayPal online, the company earns a small percentage of the transaction value for handling the movement of money, managing fraud risk, and providing the dispute-resolution layer that makes a consumer confident enough to hand over their card details in the first place.

The company's main revenue comes from three products. The first is branded checkout — the "Pay with PayPal" button that appears at the end of an online purchase, which consumers recognise and merchants accept because it typically reduces abandoned carts. The second is Braintree, a back-end payment processor used by large digital companies (such as ride-share and travel platforms) to handle card payments invisibly to the consumer; this segment is growing fast but carries a thinner margin because it is competing on price against other processors. The third is Venmo, a peer-to-peer money-transfer app popular in the United States that PayPal has been gradually monetising through a debit card, business profiles, and buy-now-pay-later integrations.

PayPal also operates a consumer credit business — short-term loans and instalment plans attached to purchases — and a stablecoin called PYUSD (a digital token pegged to the US dollar), though the latter is still a small and speculative part of the picture.

The company entered a deliberate transformation in 2023 and 2024 under then-chief executive Alex Chriss, pivoting from chasing account growth (which had peaked after the pandemic e-commerce surge) toward profitable engagement from the existing base. Chriss was terminated before completing three years. As of March 2026, Enrique Lores — formerly the head of HP Inc. and a PayPal board member since 2021 — is the third chief executive in five years. The financial metrics improved meaningfully during the Chriss period; whether Lores extends the discipline or unsettles it is the central question of 2026.

The Case for the Bet

The central bet is not that PayPal wins the checkout wars. It is that a business generating roughly $5–6 billion a year in cash from operations, with a net cash position of $14 billion and no need for large ongoing capital expenditures, can compound per-share value at a double-digit rate even if revenue growth stays modest — because management keeps buying back shares at a depressed price. At around $42 per share in mid-2026, the company trades as if its earnings will shrink for the next decade. The bet is that they will not.

For the bet to keep working, five conditions must hold:

  1. Branded-checkout share is not in structural decline. Branded-checkout transactions should not lose more than two percentage points of share in a single fiscal year. A loss of that magnitude would signal that Apple Pay and native wallets are permanently displacing PayPal at the consumer selection moment, rather than merely competing with it.

  2. No consent order restricts core products. Neither the US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau nor the Department of Justice should impose a consent order that materially limits PayPal's ability to offer its main services — specifically, the consumer-protection and dispute-resolution programmes that differentiate its checkout from a generic card payment.

  3. Consumer credit losses stay manageable. The annualised net charge-off rate on PayPal's consumer loan portfolio (which covers buy-now-pay-later and PayPal Credit) should not rise above 10%. A sustained rate above that level would suggest the credit business has taken on systemic risk it cannot absorb from operating cash flow.

  4. Buybacks continue without material new debt. Management should not abandon the share-repurchase programme or take on incremental debt that pushes the net-debt-to-operating-profit ratio above 2.0 times without a compelling strategic reason. The buyback at today's prices is the primary value-creation lever; dismantling it without replacement destroys the thesis.

  5. Operating income does not collapse. GAAP operating income should not fall below $5.5 billion in any fiscal year, and management should not guide non-GAAP operating income below $5.0 billion for the following year. Either would signal that the margin-expansion story has reversed rather than paused.

The Rules

If the filing evidence confirms all five conditions are intact, the instruction is to hold and continue compounding — no action.

If one or more conditions shows clear deterioration but no exit threshold has been crossed, the instruction is to stop adding to the position, run a fresh re-validation specifically on the deteriorating driver, and wait for two consecutive quarters of confirming data before concluding it is structural. One bad quarter is not enough.

If any exit condition is confirmed as triggered, the instruction is to close the full position within 30 days. No averaging down. Document which condition fired.

If the share price falls to or below the buy edge of $41 while all five conditions remain intact, the instruction is to add to the position in tranches over one to two quarters.

If the share price rises to or above the trim edge of $60, the instruction is to trim 25–33% of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.

If the share price rises to or above the exit edge of $78, the instruction is to close the full position. At that price, the market is pricing in roughly 11 years of base-case compounding instantly; the implied forward return falls below the long-run equity premium, and redeploying proceeds beats continuing to hold. No re-entry within 90 days.

The Conditions Today

Branded-checkout share (condition 1). This is the one condition that cannot be assessed from the most recent quarterly filing. PayPal stopped publishing branded-checkout transaction volume as a separate line item in the three months to March 2026. Total payment volume grew 11% — healthy — but the split between the higher-margin branded-checkout business and the lower-margin Braintree processing business is not in the public document. A securities class action filed in February 2026 alleges that PayPal concealed problems with its branded-checkout growth strategy; the allegation is at an early legal stage and has not been proven. The condition has not fired, but it cannot be confirmed intact from the current filing either.

Regulatory status (condition 2). The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau has issued information demands relating to PayPal's electronic fund transfer compliance programme and its PayPal Credit product. These are investigative requests, not enforcement actions; no consent order has been issued. A prior shareholder lawsuit unrelated to the new class action was dismissed with prejudice in March 2026. The net reading is mixed — open regulatory investigations are a normal feature of a large consumer-finance platform, not a signal of imminent enforcement — but they warrant monitoring.

Consumer credit quality (condition 3). The net charge-off rate on consumer loans improved to 3.0% annualised in the three months to March 2026, down from 4.5% in the same period a year earlier. The transaction-loss rate held steady at 0.06%. Both are well below the exit threshold of 10%. The consumer loan portfolio of roughly $5.4 billion is 96% current. This condition is confirming.

Capital return discipline (condition 4). PayPal repurchased 34 million shares for $1.5 billion in the three months to March 2026, at an average price of $44.60. The remaining buyback authorisation stands at $12.4 billion — enough at the current price to retire more than a quarter of the outstanding share count. The programme is active and the balance sheet remains conservative: $14 billion net cash, no material increase in debt. This condition is confirming.

Operating income (condition 5). Full-year 2025 GAAP operating income was $6.1 billion, 14% above the prior year and well above the exit threshold of $5.5 billion. In the most recent quarter (the three months to March 2026), operating income was $1.49 billion (18% of revenue), down two percentage points from the prior year's equivalent quarter due to a higher proportion of lower-margin Braintree transactions in the mix. The annualised run-rate of roughly $6 billion still clears the exit threshold. Management has not guided below $5.0 billion on any basis. This condition is mixed — the margin is narrowing in the near term — but it has not crossed any written line.

Active accounts (monitoring, not a named exit condition). Active accounts grew 1% to 439 million in the twelve months to March 2026. This is plateau-level growth rather than expansion, and transactions per account edged slightly lower. The thesis does not require account growth per se — it requires that the existing base keeps generating cash — so this is a monitoring signal rather than a thesis trigger. Worth watching over the next two to three quarters.

The Share Price

At $42 per share, PayPal trades at a yield of roughly 12% on owner earnings (a measure of cash generated after replacing worn-out assets, net of share-based compensation). The rules define a fair-value yield of 10.5%, which corresponds to a share price of about $48. The share price is modestly below that fair-value anchor — roughly 12% below, in fact — putting it close to but not yet below the buy edge of $41.

In simpler terms: the rules treat $48 as the price at which the business is fairly valued, $41 as the price at which it is cheap enough to add, and $60 as the price at which it has become expensive enough to trim. Today's $42 sits between the buy edge and fair value — in the lower end of the hold band, leaning toward cheap.

The market's implicit view is sharply pessimistic. At $42, the share price embeds an assumption that PayPal's earnings will shrink — not merely grow slowly, but actually decline — over the coming decade. The financial record does not support that assumption: operating income has risen four years running and set a new high in 2025. The gap between what the market is pricing and what the business is earning is the source of the potential return.

What's Wobbling

The largest wobble is the one that cannot be measured: branded-checkout share. PayPal's own disclosures in the most recent quarterly filing do not include the metric. The company's total payment volume grew 11%, but a disproportionate share of that growth came from Braintree — the low-margin, invisible-to-the-consumer processing business — rather than from the brand-name checkout flow that underpins the most durable part of the moat. The securities class action filed in February 2026 (still at the pleadings stage, without any finding of wrongdoing) specifically alleges that management concealed problems with this metric during the period it covered. If the next quarterly disclosure shows branded-checkout share loss exceeding two percentage points in 2026, exit condition 1 fires.

The second wobble is the margin trajectory. GAAP operating margin in the three months to March 2026 was 18%, down two percentage points from the equivalent period a year earlier. The cause is clear — Braintree's growing share of total payment volume pulls the blended margin lower because Braintree transactions carry a higher processing-cost rate — but the trend bears watching. The annual figure of 18.3% in 2025 was at record levels; the question is whether the product-mix shift will erode it materially in 2026.

The third wobble is leadership. Enrique Lores, who became chief executive in March 2026, is the third person in the role in five years and has no fintech operating background. The financial transformation under his predecessor was real; how Lores navigates the competitive challenge — specifically, the emerging threat of artificial-intelligence agents that route transactions around the consumer wallet layer entirely — is the central unknown of the next 12 months.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Agentic commerce. If artificial-intelligence agents increasingly handle purchases on behalf of consumers — selecting vendors, completing checkouts, and handling payments through direct bank-to-bank or card-network rails — the "select PayPal at checkout" moment could shrink structurally. This is a multi-year risk with no current evidence of material displacement, but it is the one scenario that could render the network economics obsolete rather than merely under pressure.
  • Branded-checkout structural decline. The bear case is that the share-loss already under way accelerates — that Apple Pay, Shop Pay, and native-wallet integrations are not cyclical competitors but permanent substitutes, and that PayPal's checkout button gradually disappears from the most desirable shopping surfaces.
  • Regulatory enforcement. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's open investigations could produce a consent order that materially restricts how PayPal structures its consumer-credit products or dispute-resolution fees. The current administration has weakened the bureau's enforcement posture, but a future administration change could reactivate those files.
  • Acquisition misstep. The 2019–2021 buying spree (which included Honey and a series of integration costs) compressed margins to 13.9% and destroyed shareholder value for two years. A return to that pattern — particularly given $14 billion of cash on the balance sheet and a new chief executive who may feel pressure to make his mark — would be a material setback.
  • Consumer credit cycle. The charge-off rate on consumer loans improved in the most recent quarter, but it rose sharply between 2023 and 2025 before stabilising. A recession or a sustained deterioration in consumer credit quality could push losses back toward and above the exit threshold.

Gaps in the Picture

The most significant data gap is branded-checkout transaction volume. The metric does not appear in the Q1 2026 quarterly filing and cannot be inferred from any reported line item. The next opportunity to assess condition 1 is the Q2 2026 earnings release and the supplemental data that accompany it.

The owner-earnings calculation used in the valuation bands treats depreciation and amortisation as a proxy for maintenance capital expenditure. For a software-heavy platform, true maintenance spending is probably lower than recorded depreciation, which means the calculated owner earnings may be somewhat understated. The direction of any error is conservative (it understates the yield), but the magnitude is not quantifiable from public disclosures.

The long-run total return estimate of 16% a year at today's price assumes that the market keeps valuing PayPal at roughly the same multiple and that the business keeps growing at today's pace. Neither assumption is guaranteed. The three-year scenario return to the fair-value estimate of $48 implies a 26% annual return in the base case — a much shorter-horizon, price-convergence estimate that assumes the discount narrows rather than the business grows. The two figures are consistent with each other but measure fundamentally different things.

The risk-free rate used to assess the equity premium was sourced from a separate holding's market data rather than directly from PayPal's own filing. The rate as of 2026-06-21 was 4.428%. If the Treasury yield moves materially, the premium calculation would change, but the directional conclusion (a double-digit premium) is robust across a wide range of rate assumptions.
