Netflix: The Bet Still Works

The Bottom Line

The conditions behind the Netflix bet are firing — the latest quarterly figures show a wider profit margin than the year before, a continued tightening of how the company spends its money on deals, and no breach of any of the lines that were drawn in advance to flag trouble. The share price sits almost exactly on the analytical fair-value anchor of $82, which means there is no bargain on offer and no froth to harvest. The one condition to watch is the speed at which the advertising business compounds, because the quarterly disclosures do not yet quantify it precisely — and that is the single number on which the longer-term margin story rests.

The Verdict

The overall call is to do nothing — the conditions are working, but the share price is not in the bargain range that would justify adding, nor in the rich range that would justify trimming. Across the five reader situations:

  • As a fresh investment at today's price (around $82 per share). Wait. The share price sits at the analytical fair-value anchor, with no margin of safety against the conservative discounted-cash-flow figure of $67 per share. The rules call for two price alerts — one at $82, the fair-value line, and one at $53, the price at which the rules switch to a standing buy — and action only once the share price crosses the cheap edge.
  • As an existing investment bought below $80 per share — currently sitting on a gain of about 3% or more. Hold. The bet is intact, the conditions are working, and the share price still does not give the 25% margin of safety the rules demand. Continue compounding; no trim, no top-up.
  • As an existing investment bought roughly between $80 and $90 per share — currently around break-even, give or take. Hold. Today's price sits inside this band. The latest quarterly evidence does not cross any of the lines from the case for the bet; the rules call for sitting still and letting the operating-leverage story continue to play out.
  • As an existing investment bought between $90 and $100 per share — currently showing a loss of up to about 9%. Hold. The mild loss is a price-history matter, not a thesis matter. The latest figures confirm the central conditions — wider profit margins, disciplined spending — so the rules do not call for averaging down and they do not call for capitulation.
  • As an existing investment bought above $100 per share — currently showing a loss of roughly 18% or more. Hold. Even at a deep loss, the bet is intact: the profit margin is expanding, capital discipline has if anything strengthened, and none of the conditions to watch has broken. The rules do not authorise selling on price alone when the operating story is still firing.

The same verb governs all five existing-holder rows; the only different action belongs to the fresh-investment reader, who is told to wait rather than chase fair value.

About Netflix

Netflix runs the largest subscription streaming service in the world — roughly 325 million paying households across more than 190 countries pay a monthly fee to watch its library of original and licensed films and series. The library, accumulating since 2013, is delivered to subscribers over the company's own purpose-built distribution pipes, called Open Connect, which sidesteps the cost of renting third-party delivery infrastructure. Three monetisation layers sit on top of the same content library: the traditional subscription fee, a cheaper advertising-supported tier launched in late 2022, and a paid-sharing feature for households that share a login. Most revenue still comes from monthly subscriptions; the advertising business is growing but is not yet large enough to be broken out separately in the quarterly filings.

What makes the financial story unusual is that the cost of the content library does not grow in lockstep with revenue. The company spends a large fixed sum each year producing and licensing programmes; once that base is set, every extra household added — and every dollar raised in price — falls toward the bottom line at a much higher margin than the underlying cost. In the year just ended the company turned $45.2 billion of revenue into roughly 30 cents of operating profit on every dollar of sales, and returned more than $9 billion to shareholders through share buybacks rather than dividends.

The Case for the Bet

The bet, in a single sentence: Netflix's layered way of charging — subscription fee, advertising tier, paid sharing — will continue to compound against a content cost base that grows more slowly than revenue, so the operating-profit margin keeps widening from today's 30 cents on the dollar toward management's guide of about 31.5 cents on the dollar and beyond.

For that to be true, the following conditions must keep holding:

  1. The operating-profit margin stays above 28 cents on every dollar of sales for any two quarters in a row, ignoring one-off charges. Below that line, the central operating-leverage story is broken.
  2. The advertising business reaches roughly $2.5 billion of annual sales by the end of next year, as a sign that the new revenue layer is compounding rather than stalling.
  3. The return on every dollar the company invests stays above 20 cents on the dollar for two years running. That figure ran at about 26 cents on the dollar in the year just ended and is the cleanest sign that the moat is widening rather than leaking.
  4. The share of sales that converts into free cash holds at 18 cents on every dollar or higher on a full-year view, the line at which cash generation is judged to be still working.
  5. The ratio of cash spent on content to the amount written off through the profit and loss account stays below 1.3 times on a sustained basis. Above that line, the capital-light story is broken.
  6. Capital discipline holds — buybacks continue at a meaningful pace while the share price is reasonable, and management does not divert money into a large, value-destructive acquisition. The April 2026 decision to walk away from the $82.7 billion bid for Warner Bros. Discovery, swallowing a $2.8 billion termination fee rather than overpay, is the cleanest evidence to date that this condition is being honoured.
  7. A coherent generative-artificial-intelligence strategy is articulated by management before the third quarter of 2027. The risk is not that an outside model replaces Netflix in households; the risk is that consumer attention drifts toward open, machine-generated platforms before Netflix has a credible defence.
  8. Brazil's tax exposure does not expand materially beyond the $619 million already booked plus the $700 million in deposits the company has lodged with the authorities, and the long-threatened tariff on foreign-made films is either not enacted or is offset without compressing margins.

The Rules

Five rules govern what action follows from how the conditions read and where the share price sits. They were written down in advance, so that the decision is mechanical rather than emotional when the moment comes:

  • If the conditions are intact — every named condition still reading as it should — the action is to hold. No additions, no trims; continue compounding. The growth-oriented buy reading only kicks in if the share price moves materially closer to the cheap edge.
  • If a single condition is wobbling but no exit line has been crossed, the action is to stop adding to the position and run a focused re-validation on the wobbling driver. The decision waits for the next two quarterly filings — one quarter of confirming data, then a second.
  • If an exit condition is breached, the action is to close the entire position within thirty days. No averaging down. The triggered condition is recorded as a lesson.
  • If the share price falls into the cheap-edge band (around $53 per share or below at current cash-flow levels), and the conditions are still intact, the action is to add to the position over one or two quarters in tranches.
  • If the share price rises into the expensive-edge band (around $183 per share or above), the action is to trim a quarter to a third of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding. A separate, looser rule calls for a full exit only if the share price runs all the way to about $450 — a level at which the entire bull-case scenario would already be in the price.

The Conditions Today

Operating-profit margin. The latest quarterly filing reports an operating-profit margin of 32.3 cents on every dollar of sales for the three months ending in March 2026, up about one cent on the same quarter a year earlier and roughly three cents above the full-year 2025 baseline of 29.5 cents. The filing's own explanation: "revenue growth outpacing the growth in cost of revenues." Revenue grew 16% against a 12% rise in the cost of revenues — the textbook signature of operating leverage at work. Comfortably above the 28-cent line that would flag trouble.

Advertising-business trajectory. The 16% revenue lift was attributed to three drivers — more memberships, higher prices, and increased advertising revenue — but the filing notes only that advertising "was not a material component of revenues" in the quarter, so the exact size is not disclosed. Marketing and personnel costs to support the advertising sales team are visibly growing. The compounding story is consistent with the case for the bet, but the precise number that would settle the $2.5 billion line will not appear until the full-year disclosure. This is the condition most worth watching.

Return on invested capital. Not disclosed in the quarterly filing. The full-year 2025 figure of about 26 cents on every dollar of invested capital — well above any reasonable cost of capital — is the last clean reading. Operating profit grew 18% on 16% revenue growth, which is consistent with the return on capital holding or improving at the margin. The condition will be settled at year end.

Free-cash-flow share of sales. The quarter generated $5.29 billion of cash from operations, but most of that came from receiving the $2.8 billion termination fee from the abandoned Warner Bros. Discovery deal. The underlying run-rate cannot be cleanly read out of one quarter; the 18-cent line is judged on a full-year basis. Full-year 2025 ran at 21 cents on the dollar — comfortably above the line.

Content cash spend versus the amount written off through the profit and loss account. Payments for content rose $841 million from the year-earlier quarter; the amount written off through the profit-and-loss account rose $395 million. The cash payments are rising faster than the write-down, which is normal for a quarter of heavy content delivery. The 1.3-times line is settled on a full-year view and the latest annual reading sits at about 1.1 times — still below the line.

Capital discipline. $1.3 billion of shares were repurchased in the quarter, $6.8 billion remains on the standing authorisation, and the Warner Bros. Discovery financing arrangements were terminated on 27 February 2026 with no money actually drawn. The condition is strengthened, not merely intact.

Generative-artificial-intelligence strategy. The quarterly filing does not discuss generative artificial intelligence. The deadline by which a coherent strategy must be articulated is still well in the future — the third quarter of 2027 — but the absence of any disclosure now is something to note.

Brazil tax exposure. The quarter included $729 million of cash payments for prior-period Brazilian non-income tax assessments. This is the disclosed liability being paid down, not new exposure surfacing. It sits inside the $619 million booked plus $700 million on deposit envelope the case for the bet already prices in. The foreign-film tariff is not mentioned.

The Share Price

At $82.18 per share, Netflix is trading essentially on top of the analytical fair-value anchor of $82. Translated into the rules' yardstick — the share of free cash flow attributable to every dollar of market value — that is 2.7 cents on the dollar. The buy edge sits at 4.2 cents on the dollar (about $53 per share) and the trim edge sits at 1.2 cents on the dollar (about $183 per share).

The plain reading: fair, not cheap and not expensive. A conservative discounted-cash-flow exercise puts the intrinsic worth at $66.68 per share, meaning today's price asks for a 23% premium to that conservative anchor and skips the customary 25% margin of safety entirely. A market-base-case midpoint sits at $86 per share — only 5% above today's price. Today's price is close to fair for what the market expects, with no cushion and no bargain.

For context: the 10-year US Treasury yield sits in the 4 to 4.5% range, comfortably above Netflix's 2.7-cent-on-the-dollar cash yield. Equity holders are being asked to accept a lower current cash yield than the risk-free alternative, and to be paid back through future growth rather than current distributions. That is a coherent bet for a high-quality growing business — but it is a bet on growth, not a bet on present cash generation.

The Standing Orders

The rules translate the share-price bands into two standing orders and two price alerts. Each is the operationalisation of a pre-committed rule — not a fresh judgement.

Price alerts (informational). - A first alert at $82, the fair-value anchor. The crossing means the share price has returned to a neutral reading; no action follows, but a fresh read of the conditions is warranted. - A second alert at $53, the cheap edge. The crossing arms the buy order below; before adding, the latest re-validation should confirm the conditions are still working.

Standing orders (actionable). - A standing buy order at $53. The rules call for adding in tranches once the share price closes at or below this line and the conditions are still intact. The order may be staged across one or two quarters rather than filled in a single fill. - A standing trim order at $183. The rules call for trimming a quarter to a third of the position when the share price closes at or above this line, to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.

A full-exit price tier has not been set as a clean numeric line by the rules' automated machinery; the written instruction calls for a full exit only if the share price runs to roughly $450, at which point the entire bull-case scenario would already be priced in.

The order levels do not contradict the verdicts above: the verdicts describe the current reading; the standing orders describe what the rules will execute if the share price moves to each line.

What's Wobbling

Two of the conditions read as mixed rather than firmly confirming, but neither crosses a line.

The first is the advertising-business trajectory. The quarterly filing does not separately quantify advertising revenue — the relevant disclosure surface is the quarterly shareholder letter and the full-year report, not the three-monthly filing. The 16% revenue lift was attributed in part to advertising, and headcount in advertising sales is visibly growing, but the figure that would settle the $2.5 billion line for next year is not yet on the page. This is not a breakage; it is a deferred answer.

The second is Brazil tax exposure. The $729 million of cash payments in the quarter is real and meaningful in absolute terms, but it represents payment of previously disclosed assessments rather than new exposure. The case for the bet already prices in the $619 million booked plus the $700 million on deposit; the cash going out the door is the cost of that disclosed position becoming real. It would only become a breakage if the exposure expanded 30 to 50% beyond the envelope.

Both deserve tracking; neither shifts the call.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • The foreign-film tariff is enacted and absorbed rather than passed on. A 100% tariff on films produced outside the United States — proposed in 2025 — would, on one external estimate, take 20% off earnings per share at full pass-through. The single largest tail risk in the file and the one most outside Netflix's control.
  • The advertising flywheel stalls below $4 billion of annual sales by 2028 while the amount written off for content grows at roughly 10% per year. That combination breaks the central operating-leverage story; the share price re-rates sharply lower as a result.
  • A wider artificial-intelligence-driven shift in consumer attention pulls viewers toward open, machine-generated content platforms before Netflix has articulated a credible defence. The deadline for management to articulate a strategy is the third quarter of 2027.
  • Content cost spiral. Content amortisation grows roughly 10% next year. If the operating leverage that has driven the past three years of margin expansion is exhausted before the advertising business is large enough to take over, the margin gives back.
  • A return to value-destructive acquisitions. The Warner Bros. Discovery walk-away is the cleanest possible signal of discipline so far. A future deal at the wrong price would reverse that signal — and management has now demonstrated it is willing to look at very large targets.

Gaps in the Picture

A few caveats about the underlying figures. The free-cash-flow figure for the quarter is distorted by the $2.8 billion Warner Bros. Discovery termination fee that landed in the operating-cash-flow line; reading it as a clean run-rate would overstate cash generation. The advertising-revenue figure is not disclosed at quarterly frequency, so the most load-bearing condition in the longer-term story cannot be checked against this filing — it waits for the full-year letter. The return on invested capital is not a disclosed quarterly metric; the last clean reading is the full-year 2025 figure of about 26 cents on every dollar of invested capital. And the assumed maintenance share of capital spending is not separated from growth in the published cash flow, which means the conservative cash measure used in the valuation is slightly more conservative than strictly necessary. None of these are blocking; all should be settled by the next annual disclosure.
