World Equal Weight: The Bet Is Wobbling

The Bottom Line

The fund is doing what it was designed to do — spread money across roughly 1,500 large and mid-sized companies in the developed world, in equal slices — and the wrapper holding the basket together remains sound. What has slipped is the underlying quality of those companies: the ten largest positions, taken at the middle of the pack, earn only about eight cents of profit on every dollar of accounting capital, well short of the level the rules call quality. The share price sits in the middle of the fair-value range at around $5.74, neither cheap enough to invite buying nor expensive enough to invite trimming. The one condition to watch is whether that thin reward for owning shares over a government bond closes back up over the next quarter.

The Verdict

Taken across the rules, the figures, and today's price, the call is to hold what is held and to refrain from adding new money for now. The fund is not broken — its size, its wrapper, and its mechanics are all working — but the case for choosing it over a cap-weighted alternative is, at this snapshot, thinner than the rules require. A first alert is set at $5 and a second at $4, where the share price would become genuinely cheap relative to the same safe yardstick. Until then, fresh money sits and watches.
  • As an existing investment bought below $5 per share — currently sitting on a gain of about 15% or more. Hold. About the Fund

The fund tracks an index called MSCI World Equal Weighted. Where a typical global share fund weights its holdings by company size — so that the largest handful of American technology businesses end up dominating the basket — this one tries to hold every constituent in roughly equal proportions. On each quarterly rebalance day, each of the roughly 1,500 holdings is reset to about seven-hundredths of one percent of the basket. Over the three months that follow, prices drift and the weights drift with them, but never far before the next rebalance pulls them back in.

The practical effect is a different shape of bet. The basket leans away from the very largest American technology firms and toward smaller and mid-sized companies, and toward developed markets outside the United States — Japan, the United Kingdom, continental Europe — that get under-represented in a size-weighted index. The trade-off is real: keeping the weights equal requires turning over roughly 30% of the basket each year, which is more friction than a size-weighted index pays. The fund is run by Invesco, domiciled in Ireland, and is just under two years old at the time of this reading.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is straightforward: that holding the developed world's listed companies in equal slices — rather than in slices proportional to their size — gives a steadier, less concentrated, and on average cheaper basket than the size-weighted alternative, and that this difference is worth the extra running cost. For that bet to keep working, six conditions must keep holding.

  1. The reward for owning the basket over a safe government bond must stay positive and reasonable. Specifically, the basket's profit yield (the share of every dollar invested that comes back as company profits each year) must clear the ten-year government bond yield by at least two percentage points, on a sustained basis. If profit yield ever falls below 3% while also lagging the bond yield for two quarters in a row, the bet is off.
  2. The annual running cost stays at or below 0.30%. It is currently around 0.20%. Equal-weighting is mechanically more expensive than size-weighting because of the rebalance turnover; 0.30% is the ceiling before the cost advantage over a cap-weighted alternative disappears.
  3. The fund stays large enough to survive. Specifically, the pool of money it manages must remain above 500 million dollars. It is currently around 1.43 billion dollars. Small European-domiciled funds are at real risk of closure, and Invesco has shuttered sub-scale products before.
  4. No single holding ever drifts above 0.20% of the basket. By design every holding sits near seven-hundredths of one percent on rebalance day. If any one name reaches three times that level, something has gone wrong with either the index methodology or the way the fund is sampling it.
  5. The index methodology stays intact. If MSCI Inc. were to retire the equal-weighting rule, change the quarterly rebalance, or narrow the universe from developed-world to something smaller, the product on offer would no longer be the product bought.
  6. The underlying quality stays reasonable. Specifically, the middle company among the ten largest weights must continue to earn at least twelve cents of profit on every dollar of accounting capital. Equal-weighting structurally pulls in smaller and less profitable names than size-weighting does — so the bar is set lower than it would be for a size-weighted basket — but below twelve cents the visible top of the basket has lost its quality cushion.

The Rules

The rules attached to this bet specify what follows from each combination of conditions and share price.

  • If all six conditions are holding, hold. The basket is reasonably priced, the wrapper is sound, and the case for equal-weighting over the size-weighted alternative is intact.
  • If one or more conditions are slipping but none is decisively broken, do not add new money. Wait for the next quarterly read. If the reward over a government bond stays below half a percentage point for two quarters running, the case for paying the higher running cost over a size-weighted alternative is gone — and the rules then call for winding down this holding in favour of one.
  • If a condition is decisively broken — the wrapper fails (size, cost, methodology) or the value test fails — exit. Move the proceeds to a size-weighted global fund such as the cap-weighted MSCI World equivalent, or to a safe government bond.
  • If the share price falls below the cheap line, add. A meaningful discount to the safe-asset yardstick should be rare and worth taking.
  • If the share price rises above the expensive line, trim a quarter to a third of the position. Harvest the premium and redeploy it on the next rebalance.

The Conditions Today

The first condition is wobbling. The basket's profit yield is 4.78%, the ten-year government bond yields 4.54%, and the gap between them — the reward for taking equity risk — is therefore 0.25 percentage points. That is positive, which keeps the bet alive, but it is well short of the two-percentage-point cushion the rules call adequate. The reward for owning shares over a bond is real, just thin.

The second condition is silent rather than confirmed. The fund's most recent disclosed running cost was 0.20%, comfortably under the 0.30% ceiling, but the figure was not present in the latest data snapshot — so this reading is unchanged rather than refreshed. No alarm, but no fresh confirmation either.

The third condition is holding comfortably. The pool of money in the fund stands at about 1.43 billion dollars, roughly 2.85 times the 500-million-dollar survival floor. The fund is just under two years old, and the cushion against closure is real but not dominant.

The fourth condition is holding. The largest single holding is Marvell Technology at 0.16% of the basket — above the seven-hundredths-of-a-percent target the equal-weighting design implies, but still beneath the 0.20% ceiling that would mark genuine drift. The cushion against breach is now narrow, however.

The fifth condition is silent by design. No automated signal exists for changes in the MSCI index methodology or for Invesco's product line-up; both are matters for direct watching. No public change to either has been announced.

The sixth condition is slipping, and this is the load-bearing wobble. The middle company among the ten largest weights earns 8 cents of profit on every dollar of accounting capital. That sits below the 12-cent floor the rules call for and well below the 15-cent level the rules call confirmation. The ten largest weights at this snapshot are a slice of cyclical technology and semiconductor names — Marvell, Intel, Bloom Energy, Dell, Ciena, Nokia, Advanced Micro Devices, Kioxia, Seagate, and STMicroelectronics — companies that earn ordinary returns on capital at the middle of their cycles, not high-quality compounders. Seagate's reading of 1,788% is a buyback artefact that distorts averages and is set aside; the middle of the pack is the honest read.

Standing orders (actionable).

  • A standing buy order at $4.39. The rules call for adding once the share price closes at or below this line and the conditions are still working. The structural reward for the equal-weight tilt over the cap-weighted alternative should make this crossing rare and decisive.

There is no standing exit order at a higher price. For an index basket like this one, the sell-side decision is governed by the integrity of the wrapper — size, running cost, single-name drift, methodology — not by price extremes. If the share price keeps climbing while none of those guardrails breaches, the rules call for trimming, not exiting.

What's Wobbling

Two readings are off-strength, and both deserve naming plainly.

The first is the thin reward for owning shares. The basket's profit yield sits just 0.25 percentage points above the safe government-bond yield. The rules call two percentage points the confirmed reading; anything below that is a flag. The current 0.25 is positive, which is why no rule has fired, but it is far from comfortable. The structural argument for paying a slightly higher running cost in exchange for the equal-weight tilt depends on the basket throwing off more profit than a size-weighted alternative; that premium is thin in this reading.

The second is the slip in underlying quality. The middle company among the ten largest weights earns 8 cents of profit per dollar of accounting capital, against the 12-cent floor the rules call the exit condition for this measure. This is the wobble the rules cannot ignore. The mechanical exit triggers attached to this product fire only on a breach of the wrapper — size, running cost, single-name drift, methodology change. None of those has breached. The quality slip is, instead, a written exit condition that calls for deliberate judgement, not an automatic execution.

Neither wobble alone breaks the bet. Together they raise the bar for adding new money and put the next quarter's read squarely in focus.

What Could Break the Fund

  • The reward for equity over bonds stays thin or turns negative for a second consecutive quarter. The structural argument for paying more to own this basket than its size-weighted equivalent then collapses.
  • The fund's pool of money halves to under 500 million dollars. Below that floor, the risk of forced closure climbs sharply for European-domiciled funds, and the wrapper itself is at stake — regardless of how the underlying basket is priced.
  • Running costs rise above 0.30%. The friction of equal-weighting overwhelms its diversification benefit, and the size-weighted alternative becomes the cleaner exposure.
  • The largest single holding drifts above 0.20% of the basket. That is three times the equal-weight target and signals either a methodology change, a rebalance failure, or sampling error large enough to compromise the product.
  • MSCI Inc. restructures the underlying index — for instance, by ending the quarterly rebalance, narrowing the universe, or replacing equal-weighting with a different rule. The product on offer is then no longer the product bought.
