Booking: The Bet Still Works

The Bottom Line

The central bet on Booking Holdings — that its two-sided travel marketplace is durable enough to survive the pressures of artificial-intelligence search tools and direct-booking pushes — remains intact as of the latest quarterly report filed in April 2026. The share price, at $169.62, sits within the fair range but close to the cheap edge, meaning the company is not on offer at a clear discount today. The one condition to watch is the AI-strategy clock: management must demonstrate measurable, shipped-product evidence of how Booking.com competes as artificial-intelligence agents become trip-planning intermediaries — and that deadline runs to late 2027.

The Verdict

The underlying evaluation of the bet is that every load-bearing pillar came through the three months ending in March 2026 intact, with one transient disruption (a geopolitical conflict in the Middle East that knocked March booking volumes) and one still-open question (the AI-strategy deadline). No written exit condition has been crossed.

As a fresh investment at today's price (around $170 per share). The rules call for waiting rather than entering now. The owner-earnings yield — the cash the business produces each year as a fraction of its market price, a measure of how much an owner earns per dollar invested — sits at 6.9%, which is inside the fair-value range but just under the 7.0% line that marks the genuine cheapness threshold. The share price would need to fall roughly $2, to around $168, to cross that line. The discipline here is to set a price alert and wait for the confirmation before committing fresh capital. The thesis remains working, which is necessary (but not sufficient on its own) for entry.

As an existing investment bought below $150 per share — currently sitting on a gain of around 13% or more. The rules call for holding. The thesis is intact, and harvesting early would mean foregoing the compounding from buybacks, a growing dividend, and a business whose cash generation is accelerating. The AI deadline is still more than five quarters away; nothing in the latest quarterly report changes that clock.

As an existing investment bought roughly between $150 and $175 per share — currently near break-even, give or take. The rules call for holding. Today's price is inside this band, which means a position bought in this range is neither far into loss nor far into gain. The valuation is not expensive — it sits near the cheaper end of the fair range — and the business fundamentals reinforced the thesis this quarter.

As an existing investment bought between $175 and $200 per share — currently showing a loss of up to around 15%. The rules call for holding. The loss is real, but no exit condition has been triggered. The capital-return engine — aggressive share repurchases and a growing dividend — continues to compound per-share value even at a flat price. The rules do not call for adding at this valuation; they call for patience.

As an existing investment bought above $200 per share — currently showing a loss of roughly 15% or more. The rules call for holding. A deep-loss cost basis with an intact thesis is not a reason to exit and not a reason to average down. The thesis either holds or breaks; the share price is not what determines that. The relevant action is to watch the conditions carefully over the next two quarterly reports.

About Booking Holdings

Booking Holdings operates the world's largest online accommodation-reservation platform. The core business is Booking.com — a marketplace connecting roughly 4.5 million properties in more than 220 countries (the majority of which are apartments, homes, and other non-hotel accommodation, not just hotel rooms) with hundreds of millions of travellers. The business also includes Priceline, Agoda, KAYAK, and OpenTable, though Booking.com is the dominant earnings engine.

The business model is straightforward. A traveller visits the platform and books a room; the platform charges the property owner a percentage of the booking value — roughly 10 cents on every dollar of gross bookings — as a fee. The company then spends a portion of that fee to acquire the next traveller, primarily through internet-search advertising, and keeps the rest. In the year ending in December 2025, the company processed $26.9 billion in revenue on roughly $260 billion in total booking value, and converted 34 cents of every revenue dollar into free cash flow (the cash the business generates after all its running costs and modest investment spending). That cash is returned almost entirely to shareholders through share repurchases and a dividend.

The share count has been shrinking aggressively for years. More than $54 billion in cumulative buybacks have reduced the number of outstanding shares substantially, meaning each remaining share represents a growing fraction of the total earnings pool. In the three months ending in March 2026 alone, $3.8 billion was spent on buybacks — 73% more than the same period a year earlier.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is this: Booking.com's two-sided network — millions of properties on one side and an enormous pool of repeat travellers on the other — is durable enough to remain the dominant global travel marketplace as the industry's distribution landscape shifts. Neither side can route around the other without meaningful economic cost, and that mutual dependency is what sustains the economics.

The supporting mechanism is capital discipline: even in a world of slower growth, management returning seven to nine billion dollars a year through buybacks and dividends shrinks the share count and compounds per-share value without requiring the underlying business to accelerate.

For the bet to keep working, seven specific conditions must hold:

  1. Room-night growth must not decline into low-single-digit or negative territory across multiple regions for two or more consecutive quarters, absent an isolated shock — that pattern would signal the network effect is leaking.
  2. The marketing-spend ratio (what the company spends on advertising as a fraction of total booking value) must not rise meaningfully for two or more consecutive years — that would signal the company is having to buy growth that its network advantage used to produce for free.
  3. The take-rate (the share of every booking dollar kept as revenue) must not compress materially or persistently — that would signal that competitors, regulators, or AI agents are squeezing the economics.
  4. Capital-return discipline must not break — buybacks should not slow or stop while the business is generating attractive cash yields, and any major acquisition would need to be examined carefully.
  5. The owner-earnings yield must not fall below 4%, which is the level at which the market would be pricing in all the optimistic compounding scenarios and leaving no margin for the risks.
  6. By the third quarter of 2027, management must have articulated and demonstrably executed a coherent strategy for how Booking.com competes when artificial-intelligence agents become the primary trip-planning interface — with concrete shipped product showing measurable engagement or margin effects. The current "Connected Trip" vision is an articulation; it is not yet evidence of execution.
  7. European regulatory enforcement must not produce a formal decision imposing structural product changes — such as mandatory removal of pricing rules — or fines exceeding 3% of global annual revenue in a single year.

The Rules

The pre-committed rules governing what happens under each combination of conditions and share price:

  • If the thesis is confirmed (all conditions working, no exit condition crossed): hold, no action.
  • If the thesis is weakening (one or more conditions deteriorating but no exit condition crossed): do not add to the position; revisit after two quarterly filings before deciding.
  • If the thesis is broken (an exit condition is crossed): exit the full position within 30 days, no averaging down.
  • If the share price falls to or below the cheap edge (owner-earnings yield at or above 7.0%, currently the $168 level): confirm the thesis is still intact, then add in tranches over one to two quarters.
  • If the share price rises to or above the expensive edge (owner-earnings yield at or below 4.0%, currently the $293 level): trim 25–33% of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core.
  • If the share price rises to or above the exit edge ($469): exit the full position. At that level, the market is pricing in roughly six years of base-case compounding instantly; the implied future return falls below the long-run return on equities, and reallocation beats continuing to hold. No re-entry within 90 days.

The Conditions Today

Room nights and gross bookings. Global room nights grew 6% in the three months ending in March 2026, compared to the same period a year earlier. March specifically came in at +1%, pulled down by the outbreak of conflict in the Middle East, which triggered cancellations and slowed new bookings. Management estimated that ex-conflict growth would have been approximately 8%. Total gross bookings reached $53.76 billion, up 15.2% in reported currency terms. This quarter's softness is consistent with the "isolated shock" carve-out that condition one explicitly allows for; the exit-condition clock does not start from one region's disruption. The watch point is whether the weakness spreads or persists across multiple regions in the June and September quarters.

Take-rate. The revenue take-rate expanded slightly to 10.3% of gross bookings, from 10.2% a year earlier. The driver was higher revenues from facilitating payments (a growing part of the model) and a favourable difference between when bookings were made and when the associated travel occurred. Condition three — persistent compression — is decisively not in play.

Marketing efficiency. The marketing-spend ratio held flat at 3.8% of gross bookings for both the March 2026 quarter and the March 2025 quarter. The filing notes that ex-conflict, the quarter showed modest leverage from the growing direct-booking channel. The direct-booking share of room nights (those booked directly through the mobile application, rather than via paid search) advanced from a mid-fifties percentage share to a high-fifties percentage share over the year — a positive directional signal, though the SEO (free search traffic) component of the mix is expected to continue declining. The two-year-rising pattern that would trigger condition two has not started.

Alternative accommodations. Booking.com's property count grew to approximately 4.5 million, with roughly 4.0 million being homes, apartments, and other non-hotel properties — up from about 4.1 million total properties a year earlier. The alternative-accommodations share of room nights widened to approximately 38%, from 37%. Steady expansion of this higher-margin segment is a reinforcing signal for the thesis, not a red flag.

Capital return. Buybacks accelerated to $3.8 billion in the March quarter, up from $2.2 billion in the same quarter a year earlier. The quarterly dividend was raised to $0.42 per share, from $0.38, a 10.5% increase year-on-year. The remaining authorisation for future buybacks stood at $18.2 billion as at March 2026 — roughly four to five quarters of runway at the current pace. No material acquisition was disclosed. Condition four is, by any reading, intact and strengthening.

AI strategy. The filing describes the "Connected Trip" vision — an ambition to create a personalised, AI-powered end-to-end travel experience — and notes rising mobile-app direct-booking share as one measurable indicator. What the filing does not yet disclose is any concrete shipped- product metric for agentic traffic, direct-engagement uplift, or margin effect from AI investments. The strategy is articulated; the evidence of execution is not yet in the public numbers. Condition six's deadline is the third quarter of 2027 — more than five quarters from the date of this filing — which means the clock is real but not yet urgent. The risk is that if the next two or three quarterly filings show the same pattern (strategy language, no measurable metrics), the window to the deadline narrows materially.

EU regulatory exposure. Booking.com's "gatekeeper" status under the European Union's Digital Markets Act and "Very Large Online Platform" status under the Digital Services Act remain in force. Multiple national competition investigations are active (Italy opened a fresh proceeding in April 2026; appeals in Spain, Switzerland, France, Greece, Hungary, and the Netherlands continue). No new formal structural remedy or fine exceeding the 3%-of-revenue threshold was disclosed in the quarter. Condition seven is not crossed, though the regulatory calendar is busy and warrants attention at each quarterly update.

The Share Price

At $169.62, the share price translates to an owner-earnings yield of 6.9% — the ratio of the cash the business generates each year to its total market price. In plain terms: at this price, an owner of the entire business would receive the equivalent of 6.9 cents of annual cash for every dollar invested.

The rules anchor around three reference points. The fair-value point is $213, corresponding to the 5.5% yield that represents the historical midpoint for this type of business. Today's price is below that anchor, meaning the market is applying a discount — pricing the company as if the future is more uncertain than the central case. The cheap edge sits at $168, the level at which the yield reaches 7.0% and the rules call for adding. Today's price is $1.62 above that line. The expensive edge sits at $293.

Put plainly: the share price is in the fair range, but at the cheaper end of it, and within striking distance of the buy threshold. The market is not offering a gift today — but neither is it demanding a premium.

What's Wobbling

Three of the eight conditions read as mixed rather than clean confirmations.

Room nights. The 6% headline figure contains a meaningful conflict-driven distortion. March specifically came in at +1%. Management's estimate of +8% ex-conflict is plausible given the filing's explicit attribution, but it is a management estimate, not a reported figure. The test will be the June and September quarters: if growth returns to mid-single digits across Europe, Asia, and the Americas with the conflict noise removed, condition one remains intact. If softness spreads or extends, the two-quarter clock for condition one begins.

Marketing efficiency. The flat 3.8% ratio is reassuring, but it conceals a directional shift worth watching. SEO (traffic from unpaid internet search) is explicitly declining and expected to keep declining, meaning the direct-channel mix must continue growing to hold the ratio flat. If the direct share plateaus while SEO continues to erode, the flat ratio could tip into a rising one in 2026 or 2027 — which would start the two-year clock for condition two. One quarter of flat-but-fragile stability is not an exit signal; it is a monitoring signal.

AI strategy. The "Connected Trip" vision is coherent and the mobile-app share is moving in the right direction. The gap is between aspiration and measurement: as at the March 2026 filing, no quantitative metric shows how many bookings are arriving via AI-assisted discovery, how the margin profile of AI-direct customers compares to paid-search customers, or whether the walled-garden approach is converting into durable competitive advantage. The deadline is real. Management has five-plus quarters to convert the strategy into evidence — and the filing language suggests awareness of the stakes — but the clock is ticking.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • AI agents disintermediate the platform. If Google Gemini, or a competing large-language- model booking service, becomes the primary starting point for trip planning, the paid-search funnel that currently generates roughly 30 cents of every revenue dollar in marketing efficiency could deteriorate faster than the direct channel can absorb it. The business model does not break immediately in this scenario — the two-sided marketplace still has value — but the marketing efficiency and take-rate conditions would both come under simultaneous pressure.
  • EU structural remedies. The collection of national and EU-level regulatory proceedings is not yet at the level that triggers condition seven, but a single adverse ruling from a major jurisdiction imposing structural pricing changes could shift that quickly. The European regulators have demonstrated they are willing to act; the question is whether they get to structural remedies rather than fines.
  • Macro travel shock. The COVID period demonstrated that a severe enough demand shock can compress revenue by 50% from peak in a single year. The business survived that period with free cash flow still positive, which speaks to its resilience — but any investor holding through a repeat of that cycle would need to accept a multi-year period of impaired earnings.
  • Capital-return discipline breaks. The buyback machine is load-bearing in the per-share compounding story. A large acquisition — particularly one at a premium in the AI-tooling or distribution space — could redirect billions of dollars that would otherwise be returned to shareholders. The KAYAK impairment in 2025 (writing down a 2013 acquisition by $457 million) serves as a reminder that acquisitions in this industry are not automatically value-creating.
  • Google paid-search cost inflation. Marketing spend as a fraction of gross bookings has held flat, but the absolute amount is growing with the business. If Google systematically raises the cost of travel-related paid-search placements — which it has economic incentive to do, since it is developing its own hotel-booking and flight-booking surfaces — the marketing ratio could rise even without any change in Booking's direct-channel mix.

Gaps in the Picture

Two data-quality notes are relevant to the numbers in this evaluation.

The diluted share count in the formal regulatory filings reflects a figure that pre-dates a 25-for-1 share split that took place in April 2026. The validation tools used the market-implied share count rather than the filing-reported figure; yield calculations are unaffected by this (they use market capitalisation directly), but readers should note that any per-share figure drawn from the raw regulatory filings will be approximately 24 times higher than the equivalent post-split figure.

The owner-earnings figure used to calculate the yield ($9.09 billion for the year ending in December 2025) is derived from operating cash flow minus total capital expenditure. This approach does not separate maintenance spending from growth spending. The company's own estimates suggest roughly $200 million of the $322 million in FY2025 capital expenditure was maintenance-related; using the full figure is therefore somewhat conservative (understates the true owner-earnings yield slightly). The direction of any error is favourable to the investment case.
