Microsoft: The Bet Still Works

The Bottom Line

The bet is working. The share price sits at the trim edge, well above fair value — the business is performing exactly as the thesis requires, but the market has priced in enough of the upside that the gap between today's share price and the underlying value anchor has narrowed to near zero. The one condition to watch is the relationship between capital spending and revenue growth: so far, infrastructure investment at 33–37% of revenue is justified by Azure still expanding at 40% a year, but if growth slows while spending stays elevated, the arithmetic changes.

TL;DR

Buy at $160. Trim at $401. Exit at $1605.

At $160, the long-run total return is about 17% a year. At today's $389, about 14% a year. Both figures are steady-state estimates over a horizon of many years — they assume the business keeps compounding at today's pace and the market eventually prices shares to fair value.

Recent Development

The most load-bearing development in Microsoft's latest quarterly results, covering the three months ending in March 2026, is that Azure cloud services grew 40% year on year — matching the same rate from the three months ending in September 2025. That consistency matters: it is the second time the growth rate has landed at exactly 40%, confirming that the acceleration is not a one-quarter artefact. The contracted sales backlog, a figure representing multi-year agreements that customers have signed but not yet had recognised as revenue, reached $627 billion — 99% higher than twelve months earlier. That number is not a forecast; it is a legal obligation by Microsoft's enterprise customers to pay.

Operating profit for the nine months ending in March 2026 was $115 billion on $242 billion of revenue, a margin of 47.4%. That margin is more than five percentage points above the lowest level that would trigger a mandatory review under the terms of the thesis. Gross profit — the share of each dollar of revenue left after paying for the direct costs of delivering the product — held at 68.2% for the nine-month period, also comfortably above its own floor.

The two wobbles are real but bounded. Spending on property, equipment, and infrastructure for the nine months came to $80 billion, equivalent to 33% of revenue for the period — well above the 23% rate for the full prior fiscal year. And the ratio of free cash generated to reported profit has been compressed: for the nine months, approximately 48 cents in free cash was generated for every dollar of reported earnings, against a floor of 65 cents set in the thesis. Neither wobble crosses a written exit line on its own — each requires a second, independent condition that has not been met — but the trend is worth monitoring as the full year concludes. See The Bottom Line for the overall verdict.

About Microsoft

Microsoft makes most of its money from software that large organisations find almost impossible to replace. At the centre of this is an identity and access management system — the software that decides which employee can log in to which application, which device can connect to the corporate network, and which security policies apply to each. That system, which Microsoft markets under the names Azure Active Directory and Entra ID, sits at the root of virtually every enterprise Microsoft customer's technology infrastructure. Migrating away from it does not mean switching a single application; it means replacing email, calendar, file storage, security controls, developer tools, and business software all at once. No technology department accepts that risk lightly, and few have done so in practice.

On top of that foundation sits a second, growing revenue stream: cloud computing through Azure, which competes primarily with Amazon Web Services and Google Cloud. Azure sells raw computing power, database storage, and increasingly the infrastructure needed to run large artificial intelligence models. Revenue from this side of the business grew 40% in the three months ending in March 2026, driven by demand for AI services. Microsoft's largest cloud customers are signing longer and larger contracts than before — that $627 billion contracted backlog is the evidence of it. The company also sells the Microsoft 365 office-software suite — email, word processing, spreadsheets, and the Teams communication platform — to more than a billion monthly active users, predominantly on subscription terms that renew automatically. Gaming, through the Xbox console and the recently acquired Activision library, and advertising on the Bing search engine and LinkedIn professional network round out the picture, though neither is central to the investment case.

The Case for the Bet

The bet rests on a single structural claim — that Microsoft's identity and access management system creates a switching cost so deep it functions as a near-permanent lock-in — and nine specific conditions that must keep holding for the thesis to remain intact:

  1. Azure cloud revenue growing above 25% a year. Azure is the primary vehicle through which the identity lock-in monetises over time: once an organisation's security and access management runs on Microsoft's infrastructure, every new AI workload, database, and cloud application follows the same provider. If Azure growth were to fall below 25% for two consecutive quarters while infrastructure spending remained above 20% of revenue, that would signal the AI investment cycle is failing to generate the revenue coverage that justifies it.

Latest reading: 40% growth as of 2026-03-31. Azure expanded 40% in the three months ending March 2026, matching the September 2025 quarter — the first time the same high growth rate has been confirmed consecutively. The 25% floor was not approached.

  1. Artificial intelligence recurring revenue scaling. The thesis holds that AI workloads are the next layer of revenue that the enterprise lock-in captures. If Copilot — Microsoft's AI assistant embedded into the office-software suite — and Azure AI services fail to attract sustained paying use, the capital spent on AI infrastructure becomes a permanent drag rather than a widening moat.

Latest reading: Silent as of 2026-03-31. The quarterly filing does not disclose a standalone figure for AI recurring revenue. GitHub's AI coding assistant is cited as driving infrastructure cost increases, confirming consumption is growing. The $37 billion annual run rate cited in the thesis was disclosed by management in external communications prior to this filing period and is not updated in the document itself.

  1. Free cash converted from reported earnings above 0.65. Owner earnings — the cash a business generates after maintaining and growing its asset base — is the foundation of the valuation. If free cash generation persistently falls below 65 cents for every dollar of reported earnings over two consecutive full fiscal years with no improvement in Azure AI revenue contribution, that signals the infrastructure spending is permanently impairing rather than temporarily compressing cash returns.

Latest reading: 0.483 for the nine months ending 2026-03-31. The nine-month free cash ratio is below the 0.65 floor, driven by infrastructure spending that reached $80 billion for the period. However, the exit condition requires two full consecutive fiscal years at this level with no improvement in Azure AI — only nine months of data are available, and Azure is growing at 40%. The compound trigger is not met.

  1. Operating profit margin above 42%. A sustained margin below 42% would signal that the business's cost structure is being permanently altered by the AI investment cycle, rather than temporarily compressed during a build phase that should eventually leverage against a larger revenue base.

Latest reading: 47.4% for the nine months ending 2026-03-31. The operating margin for the nine-month period was 47.4%, and 46.3% for the most recent quarter alone — both more than four percentage points above the exit floor. Operating profit grew 22% over the nine-month period.

  1. IRS transfer-pricing dispute not escalating to Tax Court. A dispute from the years 2004 to 2013, in which the US tax authority assessed approximately $29 billion of additional tax plus interest and penalties, remains unresolved. If it escalated to formal court proceedings, or if a single quarter's tax charge exceeded $5 billion, that would trigger a mandatory 30-day review.

Latest reading: Active administrative appeals as of 2026-03-31. The company characterises its tax provisions as adequate and intends to contest through administrative channels before any court action. No material adverse change was disclosed in the quarterly filing. The exit trigger has not fired.

  1. No formal antitrust action seeking structural remedies. Competition regulators in the United States and Europe are scrutinising Microsoft's bundling of software products and its relationships in the artificial intelligence market. If a formal complaint were filed seeking a structural remedy — such as forcing Microsoft to sell its Teams communication platform or to open its cloud infrastructure to competitors — that would directly attack the lock-in mechanism that underlies the thesis.

Latest reading: No formal structural complaint as of 2026-03-31. The quarterly filing restates the standard regulatory risk disclosure without introducing any new enforcement development. The Activision gaming acquisition integration is proceeding without a forced divestiture.

  1. Infrastructure spending declining as a share of revenue. The central debate in the thesis is whether the capital being invested in data centres and computing equipment permanently compresses cash returns or builds a structural moat that widens as AI services scale. If spending as a share of revenue remains elevated without a parallel deceleration in Azure growth, the evidence shifts toward permanent compression.

Latest reading: 33.1% of nine-month revenue as of 2026-03-31. Infrastructure spending ran at 37.3% of revenue in the most recent quarter alone — above the 22.9% rate for the full prior fiscal year. The exit condition requires Azure growth to fall below 25% simultaneously; at 40%, that compound trigger is not met.

  1. Contracted sales backlog growing above 30% a year. The contracted backlog — multi-year agreements signed by enterprise customers — is the most forward-looking indicator of whether customers are deepening or loosening their commitment to Microsoft's ecosystem. A decline in growth below 30%, or an absolute quarterly contraction, would signal that enterprise commitment is weakening.

Latest reading: +99% year on year to $627 billion as of 2026-03-31. The contracted backlog nearly doubled over twelve months. The exit condition was not approached.

  1. Gross profit margin above 65%. If the cost of delivering software and cloud services were to increase faster than revenue — pushing the gross margin below 65% for two consecutive quarters — that would signal either that AI infrastructure costs are being absorbed rather than passed on, or that new competitive pricing dynamics are compressing the core software moat.

Latest reading: 67.6% for the quarter ending 2026-03-31; 68.2% for the nine-month period. Both are comfortably above the exit floor.

The Share Price

At $389, the share price sits just inside the upper boundary of the hold band — so close to the trim threshold of $401 that only six basis points of owner earnings yield (a measure of earnings return relative to market price) separates the two. In other words, the business is priced at almost exactly the level where a quarter to a third of the position is due to be trimmed.

An owner earnings yield — a measure of how much normalised cash the business produces relative to its market price — of 3.5% implies a fair-value share price of $229. The current $389 is 70% above that anchor. The gap reflects the market's expectation that the business will continue compounding at pace and that the AI infrastructure investment will translate into structurally higher earnings power. Under the base scenario — Azure sustaining 25–35% growth, the contracted backlog converting, and the current spending cycle peaking — the three-year fair-value estimate reaches $570 per share, which would imply a meaningful re-rating from today's price. But that scenario needs to materialise; the current share price is already pricing in a substantial portion of the journey.

The buy threshold of $160 — the price at which the earnings yield would indicate genuine cheapness — would require a 59% fall from today's level. That would only arrive in a scenario where the AI investment thesis collapsed and the market re-rated the business sharply downward. For existing holders, the question is not whether to buy more; at $389, the position earns less than the long-run target yield and sits within rounding distance of the trim line.

What's Wobbling

Two of the nine thesis drivers show mixed readings rather than clean confirmations, though neither crosses a written exit line in isolation.

Infrastructure spending as a share of revenue. For the nine months ending March 2026, property and equipment spending reached $80 billion — 33% of revenue for the period, and 37% in the most recent quarter alone. This compares with 23% for the full prior fiscal year. The thesis acknowledges this cycle explicitly: the question is whether the spending is building a structural moat or permanently compressing cash returns. The written exit condition requires this elevated spending to coincide with Azure growth falling below 25%; at 40% growth, the compound trigger is not activated. That distinction matters: spending 37% of revenue on infrastructure while the cloud business grows at 40% is a different signal from spending 37% while growth decelerates. The former is evidence of a capacity race the business is winning; the latter would be evidence of a bet gone wrong. For now, the data supports the former reading — but the ratio will need to improve as the spending cycle matures.

Free cash conversion. For the nine months ending March 2026, approximately 48 cents of free cash was generated for every dollar of reported earnings — below the 65-cent floor set in the thesis. The exit condition requires two consecutive full fiscal years at this level with no improvement in Azure AI revenue contribution. The nine-month period covers only three quarters of one fiscal year, and Azure is growing at 40%. That makes the compound trigger remote at the current trajectory, but the annual figure, when disclosed at the fiscal year end in June, will be the first data point that counts toward the two-year test. The reading is a watch item, not an action signal.

Neither wobble changes the overall verdict. The core moat indicators — operating margin at 47%, gross margin at 68%, contracted backlog up 99% — all confirm the business is compounding as intended. The wobbles are costs being incurred in advance of revenue that the contracted backlog strongly suggests is coming.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • The AI investment fails to monetise. If Azure's revenue growth decelerates to mid-teens while infrastructure spending remains at 30%+ of revenue, the cash compression becomes permanent rather than temporary. Three-year free cash in that scenario reaches roughly $75 billion — flat with the prior fiscal year — and the market re-rates the business down to a depressed valuation multiple, implying a share price around $342 and a three-year capital loss of roughly 4% a year.
  • The IRS dispute escalates. The $29 billion assessment — covering years 2004 to 2013, before the cloud transition — remains unresolved. A move to formal court proceedings would trigger a mandatory review. If it settled at the upper bound, the cash impact would absorb a substantial portion of one year's free cash generation, though the business would survive intact.
  • Antitrust action targets the lock-in architecture. The Federal Trade Commission's active investigation covers cloud bundling, the Teams communication platform, and the relationship with OpenAI. A formal complaint seeking structural remedies — forcing a sale of Teams, or mandating interoperability that reduces switching costs — would directly weaken the moat the thesis rests on. The risk is real; it has not yet crossed the written threshold.
  • Open-weight artificial intelligence models commoditise Azure workloads. If large AI models became freely available and optimised to run on generic infrastructure, some of the premium Azure charges for its AI services would erode. The identity and security platform would remain intact, but the growth narrative that justifies the current valuation multiple would weaken.
  • OpenAI losses scale materially. Microsoft's equity interest in OpenAI produced a $4.7 billion charge in the most recent fiscal year. If OpenAI requires further capital injections at terms unfavourable to Microsoft, the drag on reported earnings would grow. This is a second-order risk — it does not affect the operating business — but it complicates the earnings-quality picture.

Gaps in the Picture

An owner earnings figure — derived from operating cash flow minus total capital expenditure — is used as a proxy for sustainable earnings power. Microsoft does not separately report maintenance capital expenditure and growth capital expenditure in its filings — the full $64.6 billion of spending is combined into a single line. The thesis excludes growth capital expenditure from this earnings figure on the grounds that it is discretionary investment in future capacity rather than the cost of sustaining today's earnings. That simplification overstates near-term earnings to the extent that some maintenance spending is buried inside the growth figure. The fair-value anchor reflects this caveat.

Two warnings are active on the valuation methodology. An earnings yield of approximately 2.1% is currently implied by the market price, against the 3.5% long-run target set when the position was initiated — a gap of 41%. The fair-value midpoint implied by the independently modelled valuation band ($561 per share) implies a yield of 1.4%, which diverges from the long-run target by nearly 60%. Both warnings indicate that the 3.5% target used to compute the $229 fair-value price may be stale relative to how the business has been priced through the AI-investment cycle. A review of the fair-value target is warranted before the next full cycle refresh.

The Rules

Six pre-committed rules govern what action follows from each state of the bet and the share price:

  1. If the thesis is confirmed — all nine drivers holding or mixed, no exit condition triggered — then hold the position without adding. The share price near the trim threshold is not an invitation to add; the compounding logic runs with the existing position until either the price falls to the buy edge or the thesis changes.

  2. If the thesis weakens — one or more drivers deteriorate but no single exit condition fires — then do not add to the position. Run a fresh re-validation against the specific deteriorating driver and monitor for one additional quarter before deciding whether to reduce the position. The question to answer is whether the weakening is cyclical (a temporary cost of the AI investment cycle) or structural (competitive displacement eroding the moat). If a second consecutive quarter confirms the adverse signal, escalate to the thesis-broken review.

  3. If the thesis breaks — any one of the seven written exit conditions fires — then exit the full position within 30 days. No averaging down. The specific condition that triggered the exit should be logged for future learning. The seven conditions are: Azure growth below 25% for two quarters while capital spending exceeds 20% of revenue; free cash conversion below 0.65 for two fiscal years with no improvement in Azure AI revenue; operating margin below 42% for two quarters; the IRS dispute moving to Tax Court or a single-quarter tax charge above $5 billion; a formal antitrust complaint seeking structural remedies; gross margin below 65% for two quarters; contracted backlog growth below 30% or an absolute quarterly contraction.

  4. If the share price falls below the buy edge of $160 — then confirm the thesis is intact first. If intact, initiate an add in tranches: a first tranche at or below $352 (representing a 40% discount to the base-case fair-value estimate), a second tranche at or below $322 (representing a 40% discount to the earnings-based intrinsic value). Each tranche should be 25–33% of the intended add-on allocation. A formal earnings yield threshold of 5.0% corresponds to roughly $253 per share on current normalised earnings — reachable only in a severe bear scenario.

  5. If the share price rises above the trim edge of $401 — then trim 20–25% of the position to harvest the premium. An owner earnings yield below 2.0% implies a market value above roughly $5 trillion, pricing in the base case with limited additional upside optionality. After trimming, re-evaluate the remaining size. If Azure growth continues confirming the base case, retain the remaining position. Do not trim below 50% of the current holding unless a thesis exit condition is also triggered.

  6. If the share price rises above the exit edge of $1,605 — then exit the full position. At that level, the earnings yield falls below 1.5%, implying a market value above roughly $6.8 trillion and pricing in the full bull-case fair value with the three-year annualised return already embedded. Redeploy proceeds at the next cheap-edge crossing or into a less expensive alternative. No re-entry within 90 days, to avoid anchoring to the previous holding price.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
