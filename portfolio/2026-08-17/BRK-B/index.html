Recent Development

The three months ending in June 2026 confirmed five of nine thesis drivers and raised a single structural concern loud enough to shade the overall verdict from intact to wobbling.

The dominant development is GEICO. Berkshire's car-insurance operation reported a combined ratio of 89.3% for the first half of 2026, up from 81.7% in the same period of 2025 — a 7.6 percentage-point deterioration. Loss ratios rose 4.9 percentage points on higher claims frequencies (bodily injury claims up 5–7%, property-damage and collision up 3–5%) and higher severities (bodily injury average cost up 10–12%). Underwriting expenses jumped 28% over the half as advertising spending surged, suggesting that management is paying to recover market share lost to Progressive and its telematics-based pricing platform. Pre-tax underwriting profit fell to $2.4 billion in the first half of 2026, down 40% from $4.0 billion a year earlier. That is still profitable underwriting — no exit condition has crossed — but the direction and magnitude are the concern that pushes the Standing Orders to read-only status for now.

Against that background, the rest of the enterprise delivered broadly. Berkshire shareholders' equity reached $747.9 billion at June 2026, up approximately 12.1% year-on-year on a per-share basis. Insurance float grew to $177.5 billion, with cost of float remaining negative — the structural funding advantage that underpins the entire model remains fully intact. After-tax operating earnings across the non-insurance subsidiaries came in roughly 5.1% ahead of the same period a year earlier, led by manufacturing and services (+15.1%), BNSF — Berkshire's freight-rail business — (+9.5%), and Berkshire Hathaway Energy (+11.5%). Most notably, management repurchased $4.5 billion of stock in the second quarter alone, buying at prices in the low $490s. That is not a trivial signal: the repurchase programme activates only when management believes the price is below intrinsic value, and $4.5 billion in a single quarter is the largest repurchase in recent memory. For a fuller view of the Share Price and what the valuation level implies, see that section below.

A score of 1.40 places Berkshire exactly at the midpoint of the portfolio ranking — a solid position, but one that reflects the current tension between a genuinely durable franchise and a wobbling near-term driver.

About Berkshire

Berkshire Hathaway is a permanently capitalised conglomerate — a holding company that owns, in their entirety or in large part, a collection of diversified operating businesses alongside a vast portfolio of publicly listed shares. The revenue base of roughly $370 billion a year spans four distinct engines: insurance (led by GEICO, the third-largest US car insurer, and Berkshire Hathaway Reinsurance, one of the world's largest reinsurers), freight rail (BNSF, which operates a 32,500-route-mile network across the western United States), regulated energy (Berkshire Hathaway Energy, which operates utilities and pipelines across multiple US states and in the United Kingdom), and a broad collection of manufacturing, service, and retail businesses covering everything from industrial tooling to furniture retail. On top of the operating subsidiaries sits a public equity portfolio valued at over $400 billion, funded in large part by the insurance float.

The insurance float is the mechanism that sets Berkshire apart. Float is the pool of premium money that policyholders pay before their claims are settled — money that sits on Berkshire's balance sheet and can be invested at management's discretion. At $177.5 billion and growing, with the cost of maintaining that float running below zero (because underwriting has been profitable, policyholders are in effect paying Berkshire to hold their money), this is a structural funding advantage that no competitor can economically replicate. The float funds the equity portfolio, which in turn generates investment income of roughly $12–13 billion a year. That is the engine; the operating subsidiaries are the flywheel. Greg Abel, who assumed the chief executive role on January 2026 after Warren Buffett's transition to non-executive chairman, inherited both the architecture and the culture of capital discipline that built it.

The Case for the Bet

The central bet is that Abel and the management culture Buffett built will continue to reinvest retained earnings and acquired float into high-returning businesses and securities at rates exceeding what a passive index could deliver. For the bet to keep working, four conditions must hold:

  1. Book value per share growth must not trail the MSCI World total return for five or more consecutive years. The benchmark comparison is the practical test of whether Berkshire's compounding engine still has an edge over simply buying a global index fund. Five years of underperformance — not one or two — is the exit signal, because Berkshire's concentrated, insurance-heavy model is legitimately expected to lag in strong equity-bull markets.

Latest reading: Shareholders' equity reached $747.9 billion at June 2026, with book value per share growing approximately 12.1% year-on-year on a per-Class-A-equivalent basis as of 2026-06-30. This sits comfortably ahead of any plausible MSCI World benchmark reading and is not close to triggering the five-year consecutive-underperformance condition.

  1. No large acquisition that reveals Abel lacks capital discipline. Specifically, no transaction exceeding $20 billion at a consideration above 1.5 times the book value of the target, nor one that subsequently requires a goodwill impairment exceeding $5 billion. A single deal of that profile would signal that the capital-allocation culture that made Berkshire worth owning has been broken.

Latest reading: The first major Abel-era transaction, OxyChem (closed January 2026), is the only data point. The Q2 2026 filing contains no commentary on additional large acquisitions in progress, and the $4.5 billion in share repurchases during the quarter — at prices management judged to be below intrinsic value — is the capital-allocation signal available as of 2026-06-30.

  1. No structural culture change. A regular dividend, a break-up, or an activist-forced capitulation that dismantles the decentralised owner-operator model would signal that the architecture underpinning the bet has been abandoned.

Latest reading: No dividend, no break-up proposal, no activist involvement visible in the Q2 2026 filing. The management structure and owner-operator pay model (Abel on cash compensation with no stock awards) remain intact as of 2026-08-10.

  1. The price-to-book ratio must not exceed 2.0 times. At 2.0 times book, the valuation fully prices in optimistic compounding and the opportunity cost relative to a passive index becomes clearly negative — the margin of safety is gone and the bet on Abel's allocation skill is no longer compensated.

Latest reading: The price-to-book ratio stands at approximately 1.495 times as of 2026-06-30, using year-end 2025 shareholders' equity of $717.4 billion and the June 2026 share price of $497. This is within the hold band (1.25 times at the cheap edge; 1.75 times at the trim edge; 2.0 times at the exit threshold) and well below the exit condition. A spot calculation using the June 2026 equity figure of $747.9 billion and the current Class-A-equivalent share count of approximately 1.43 million would imply a ratio closer to 1.43 times — modestly below fair value.

The Share Price

At $497 per share, the price-to-book ratio sits at approximately 1.495 times, against a fair-value anchor of 1.45 times book. That places the share price in the upper half of the hold band — above fair value by a slim margin but nowhere near the trim edge at 1.75 times. The band runs from 1.25 times at the cheap edge (roughly $416 per share given current book value) through the fair-value anchor at 1.45 times (approximately $482) to the trim edge at 1.75 times (approximately $582). The book-value calibration uses year-end 2025 equity of $717 billion; the updated June 2026 equity of $748 billion, if carried through, would push the implied fair-value price marginally higher. That is an informational note rather than a conclusion — the validator uses year-end equity as the standard anchor.

The price-to-book measure is the appropriate lens for Berkshire because the equity portfolio's mark-to-market swings make earnings-based multiples unreliable — GAAP net income ranged from negative $22.8 billion in one year to positive $96.2 billion in the next. Book value, which captures the accumulated value of both operating earnings and portfolio appreciation net of losses, is the measure management itself has historically used as the honest account of per-share intrinsic value growth.

What's Wobbling

GEICO is the single active wobble, and it is consequential enough to carry the overall verdict from intact to wobbling on its own. The combined ratio (claims plus expenses as a share of premiums) reached 89.3% for the first half of 2026, up 7.6 percentage points from 81.7% a year earlier. The deterioration is broad-based: loss ratios rose on higher frequency of bodily injury claims, higher severity of bodily injury settlements, and higher property-damage and collision frequencies. Advertising expenses jumped 28%, suggesting that management is paying to defend or recover market share against Progressive's telematics-based pricing platform, without yet demonstrating that the spending is converting into growth in the number of policies in force. Underwriting profit fell 40% year-on-year to $2.4 billion in the first half. This does not cross any written exit condition — underwriting is still profitable, the overall insurance group improved (aggregate pre-tax underwriting profit of $4.4 billion was up 4.4% from the prior year, because Primary and BHRG both recovered strongly), and the thesis-broken condition requires combined ratios above 100% for two or more consecutive years, which has not occurred. What it does cross is the practical threshold for "something that requires watching": GEICO's deterioration has now spanned two consecutive reporting periods, and the exit conditions' logic was written precisely to catch this pattern before it compounds.

The BHE situation is a secondary wobble, and a mixed one. The Oregon Court of Appeals reversed the first-phase PacifiCorp wildfire verdict in April 2026 — a materially positive legal outcome that cut the remaining estimated wildfire liability from $1.2 billion at year-end to $572 million. That is a significant reduction. The complication is that the Oregon Supreme Court then agreed to review the case, with oral argument scheduled for November 2026, which reintroduces uncertainty. BHE earnings grew 11.5% year-on-year in the first half of 2026, so the operating business remains healthy. The wildfire situation is better than it was six months ago but not closed. Neither the GEICO wobble nor the BHE situation is close to triggering the Rules — that assessment is made explicitly in the Standing Orders section above.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • GEICO's combined ratio deteriorates through 100% for two consecutive years. The written exit condition would fire. This is the scenario where Progressive's telematics advantage proves structural — not a transitional rough patch — and GEICO's advertising-driven recovery strategy fails to convert into policies-in-force growth. Pre-tax underwriting earnings would turn negative, eroding the float advantage at the margin.
  • Abel makes a capital-destroying acquisition. A transaction exceeding $20 billion at a consideration above 1.5 times the book value of the target, or one that subsequently requires a goodwill impairment exceeding $5 billion, would signal that Buffett's capital-discipline culture has not survived the transition. The cash hoard — roughly $210 billion in cash and Treasury bills — is the optionality that makes this scenario possible at scale.
  • BHE wildfire liability re-escalates. If the Oregon Supreme Court reverses the Court of Appeals decision in November 2026, the wildfire liability could return to or exceed $1.2 billion. A further escalation into the billions would test the ring-fence separating BHE from the Berkshire parent's credit.
  • The equity portfolio reprices sharply downward. Apple has historically represented 35–40% of the public equity portfolio. A 50% decline in Apple's share price would remove $60–80 billion from book value, compressing the price-to-book ratio's anchor while leaving the share price unchanged — a mechanical deterioration in the valuation reading.
  • BHE operating returns compress from the OBBBA headwind. The law passed in July 2025 phases out wind and solar production tax credits for projects placed in service after December 2027. BHE has invested $38 billion in renewable energy partly on the economics of those credits; the foregone benefit is estimated at $400–600 million a year in trajectory earnings.

The Rules

The action plan has six clauses, each of which maps to a specific state of the evaluation:

  • If the thesis is confirmed (all drivers intact or mixed, with no driver challenging), hold without action and let the business compound.
  • If the thesis is weakened (at least one driver is challenging, as is currently the case with GEICO), hold the existing position. Do not add. Re-run the thesis validation after the next quarterly filing to assess whether the deterioration has progressed, stabilised, or reversed.
  • If the thesis is broken (a written exit condition fires — book value growth trails the index for five consecutive years, a capital-destroying acquisition is completed, a structural culture change occurs, or the price-to-book ratio exceeds 2.0 times), exit the full position within 30 days. No averaging down.
  • If the share price falls below the buy edge ($416, corresponding to a price-to-book of 1.25 times), and the thesis is confirmed — not merely wobbling — add to the position.
  • If the share price rises above the trim edge ($582, corresponding to 1.75 times book), trim 25–33% of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.
  • If the share price rises above the exit edge ($649, corresponding to 2.0 times book), exit the full position. At that price, the market is pricing in roughly ten years of base-case compounding instantly; redeploy proceeds at the next cheap-edge crossing or into a cheaper alternative. No re-entry within 90 days.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
