Recent Development

PepsiCo's latest quarterly filing, covering the six months to the middle of 2026, arrived with two sets of numbers pointing in opposite directions.

On the reassuring side: the gross profit margin held at 54.6% of sales in the first half of 2026 — comfortably above the 52% floor that would signal a genuine erosion of brand pricing power. Cash flow from the operating business turned sharply positive, at over $1.1 billion in the first half against a negative figure a year earlier. The operating profit recovery was partly flattered by the absence of a large write-down that had hit the prior year's numbers; adjusting for that, operating profit still grew by roughly 6% year-on-year, tracking toward the base-case target of a 13.5% margin over the next two years.

On the worrying side: PepsiCo's North American beverage business declined in volume for at least the eighth consecutive quarter. Management attributed the continued softness to a combination of higher commodity costs — aluminium packaging alone added a headwind of five to six percentage points — and consumer trade-down away from branded sodas and sports drinks. The company submitted refund claims under a legal ruling that parts of the aluminium tariff may have been imposed without adequate authority, but confirmed in the filing that the scope and duration of future tariffs remains significantly uncertain.

Taken together, the filing confirms that the moat is intact at the brand-and-distribution level but that volume pressure in North American beverages has not yet reached a turning point. See the Standing Orders for what happens if the volume trend continues.

About PepsiCo

PepsiCo makes and distributes food and drinks. Its best-known products include Pepsi, Mountain Dew, and Gatorade on the drinks side, and Lay's, Doritos, Cheetos, and Quaker oats on the food side. The company has 23 brands that each generate more than a billion dollars in annual sales, spread across snacks, cereals, and refrigerated foods alongside the beverages. It operates in more than 200 countries and territories.

The food business — grouped under Frito-Lay North America — generates the higher profit margins: salty snacks are sold at premium prices with raw-material costs that tend to stay low relative to price. The beverage business operates through a separate North American unit and a set of international operations. Across all of it, PepsiCo's distinguishing feature is that it delivers its products directly to retail shelves through its own logistics network — a system that costs roughly $4.4 billion a year to maintain, gives PepsiCo guaranteed shelf placement in petrol stations and convenience stores, and makes the business structurally difficult to displace. A competitor that wanted to replicate the network from scratch would need years and tens of billions of dollars; most simply choose not to try.

The Case for the Bet

The bet on PepsiCo rests on six conditions, each of which can be checked against future official filings:

  1. Organic revenue keeps growing. The company's revenue, measured before acquisitions and currency swings, must continue to expand — confirming that volume and pricing are working together across both snacks and beverages.

Latest reading: Organic revenue growth was positive in the six months to June 2026, with pricing partially offsetting beverage volume declines (2026-07-09).

  1. North American beverage volumes recover. The beverage arm in North America has declined in volume for at least eight consecutive quarters. The thesis requires this to turn positive, confirming that the loss is cyclical — tied to tariff-driven price increases and post-pandemic consumption normalisation — rather than structural.

Latest reading: Volume declined 3–4% in the three months ending in June 2026 — the eighth consecutive negative quarter. No reversal has appeared yet (2026-07-09).

  1. Operating margin holds above 11.5%. The profit from the company's own operations — the share of every sales dollar left after paying for goods, logistics, and overhead — must not fall below 11.5% for two consecutive quarters. The FY2025 compression to 12.2% was partly caused by a one-time write-down; recovery toward 13.5% is the base case.

Latest reading: Operating margin was 16.6% in the three months ending in June 2026 — well above the 11.5% floor (2026-07-09).

  1. Brand pricing power holds at the gross margin line. The gross profit margin — the share of every sales dollar retained after the direct cost of raw materials and production — must stay above 52%. Below that level, the brand moat would be considered structurally eroding.

Latest reading: Gross margin was 54.6% in the six months to June 2026, materially above the 52% floor (2026-07-09).

  1. Cash conversion stays healthy. The business should convert at least 80% of reported net profit into actual cash flow, confirming that accounting profit is not being obscured by rising receivables or deferred costs.

Latest reading: Cash conversion recovered strongly in the first half of 2026 after a negative prior-year period distorted by acquisition payments; a full-year measure will be available in the next annual filing (2026-07-09).

  1. The dividend growth streak continues. PepsiCo has increased its annual dividend every year since 1972 — 54 consecutive increases. A freeze or cut would signal management's own loss of confidence in the cash outlook.

Latest reading: The most recent dividend announcement maintained the growth streak; the annual dividend stands at approximately $5.92 a share (2026-07-09).

The Share Price

At $139, the shares sit fractionally below the $140 fair-value estimate — the price at which the three-year average of PepsiCo's annual earnings would yield 4.7% a year on the money invested. That yield sits between two thresholds: 6.5% at $101 a share, where the margin of safety becomes meaningful (details in the Standing Orders), and 3.2% at $205 a share, where the price implies the market expects compounding well beyond the base case. The current price is at fair value, which means it offers no discount for the three unresolved uncertainties — beverage volume recovery, tariff trajectory, and acquisition integration. A new buyer would be paying full price for an outcome that is not yet certain.

The Standing Orders

Two price levels are worth watching. First, $140: the fair-value estimate, a dollar above the current share price. That gap is too narrow to matter in practice — the stock is effectively at fair value. Second, $101: the cheap edge, where the earnings yield would reach 6.5%, aligning with the estimated intrinsic value derived from the company's long-run cash flows.

The pre-set responses to each price level are as follows. If the share price falls to $101 or below and no exit condition has been triggered first, the rule is to buy: confirm the thesis is still intact, then build the position in stages over one to two quarters. If the price rises to $205 or above, the rule is to trim: reduce the position by a quarter to a third and re-evaluate the remainder. If the price reaches approximately $469, the rule is to exit the full position; at that level the market is pricing in years of base-case compounding at once, and the rule is to redeploy the proceeds without re-entering within 90 days.

What's Wobbling

One thing is wobbling. PepsiCo's North American beverage business has been losing volume for at least eight consecutive quarters — a stretch that runs from early 2024 through the middle of 2026. In the three months ending in June 2026, volumes fell again by 3 to 4 percent year-on-year. This matters because one of the thesis's exit conditions is a compound trigger: if volume in North American beverages stays negative for three or more consecutive quarters at the same time as the overall operating margin falls below 11.5%, the thesis is considered broken. The volume leg of that condition has been active far longer than three quarters. The operating margin leg, however, is not triggered — the reported margin was 16.6% in the most recent quarter, well above the 11.5% floor — so the compound trigger has not fired and no written exit condition has been breached. The wobble is the volume trend; the safeguard is the operating margin holding firm.

A secondary wobble is the tariff situation. The 50% tariff on aluminium packaging added a headwind of five to six percentage points to beverage costs in the first half of 2026. The legal status of those tariffs remains unresolved — the company submitted refund claims but cannot quantify the outcome. This is a cost headwind, not a threat to the structural moat. It does not cross any written exit condition, but it does keep the three-year scenario returns compressed relative to a tariff-free baseline.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • North American beverage volumes continue declining, evidence emerges that the losses are structural rather than cyclical — consumers permanently shifting toward water, functional drinks, and own-label sodas — and the operating margin drifts toward the 11.5% floor, triggering the compound exit condition.
  • Weight-loss drugs, now used by roughly one in five American adults, continue to suppress snack and soft-drink spending at a faster pace than acquisitions of premium brands can offset.
  • The snack conglomerate formed by the merger of Mars and Kellanova (which owns Pringles, Cheez-It, and Pop-Tarts) gains meaningful shelf space at Frito-Lay's expense, denting the 60% market-share lead that underpins the food business's pricing power.
  • The recent acquisitions of poppi and Siete Foods, which together added roughly $3 billion of debt, fail to generate the revenue growth that justifies their cost — and the balance-sheet leverage climbs above 3.0 times operating earnings for two consecutive years without a credible plan to reduce it.
  • A further round of commodity cost increases squeezes the gross margin toward the 52% floor, signalling that brand pricing power is no longer sufficient to absorb input costs.

The Rules

When no exit condition has been triggered and the share price is between $101 and $205, the rule is to hold and collect the dividend, re-evaluating at the next quarterly filing around October 2026 — the key metric to watch is North American beverage volume at that point.

If one of the exit conditions has begun to wobble but the line has not been crossed, the rule is not to add to the position. Monitor the specific deteriorating measure — operating margin, beverage volume, or gross margin — through the next two quarterly filings before drawing a conclusion. A single quarter is not enough to call a trend.

If an exit condition is triggered — the compound operating-margin-plus-volume signal fires, the dividend streak ends, leverage exceeds 3.0 times earnings for two years, the gross margin falls below 52%, or a major acquisition produces a write-down above $300 million — the rule is to exit the full position within 30 days. No averaging down.

If the share price falls to $101 or below and no exit condition has been triggered, the rule is to confirm the thesis is still intact and then add to the position in stages over one to two quarters. At that price, the earnings yield would align with the estimated intrinsic value of the business.

If the share price rises to $205 or above, the rule is to trim 25 to 33% of the position, harvest the premium, and re-evaluate the remainder.

If the share price reaches approximately $469, the rule is to exit the full position and redeploy the proceeds. At that price, the market is pricing in years of base-case compounding at once; the rule is not to re-enter within 90 days.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
