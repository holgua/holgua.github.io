About Berkshire

Berkshire Hathaway is a holding company that owns dozens of businesses outright and also holds a large portfolio of publicly traded shares. It earns money through four distinct channels. The largest is insurance: the business runs Berkshire's car-insurance unit, GEICO, along with speciality reinsurance businesses that write coverage for unusual or large risks — aviation accidents, nuclear facilities, natural catastrophes — that standard insurers are unwilling or unable to underwrite. Insurance premiums collected before claims are paid create a pool of investable money known as float; Berkshire's float stood at roughly $177 billion at the end of the first quarter of 2026 and carries a negative effective cost, meaning the insurance operations generate underwriting profit while also providing free-of-charge capital to invest elsewhere.

The second channel is a freight railway — Burlington Northern Santa Fe, which operates one of the two major rail networks across the western United States. Third is a group of regulated electric and gas utilities gathered under the Berkshire Hathaway Energy banner. Fourth is a collection of wholly-owned industrial, retail, and services businesses — ranging from an aerospace-components maker to a chain of truck-stop travel centres — alongside a publicly traded equity portfolio concentrated in a handful of large positions, most notably shares in Apple, American Express, and Bank of America. The business does not pay dividends; all earnings are retained and redeployed. The central question since January 2026, when Gregory Abel formally succeeded Warren Buffett as chief executive, is whether the capital-allocation quality that defined Berkshire under Buffett persists under its new stewardship.

The Case for the Bet

The central proposition is that Berkshire can continue to compound the value of shareholders' equity at a rate that exceeds what a passive index fund would return — not through financial engineering, but through disciplined reinvestment of insurance float and retained operating earnings into high-returning opportunities. The specific conditions that must keep holding for that to remain true are:

  1. Book value per share keeps growing faster than the global stock market benchmark. The thesis breaks if shareholders' equity per share consistently grows more slowly than a broadly diversified index — that would signal the compounding advantage is gone. The threshold is five or more consecutive years of underperformance.

Latest reading: Shareholders' equity grew +10.5% in full-year 2025 (to $717 billion) and rose a further $9.8 billion in the quarter ending 2026-03-31, reaching $727 billion. Per-share book value stood at approximately $337 on a Class B equivalent basis. The 2025 growth rate was well ahead of the MSCI World total return for the same period. No underperformance trigger has fired.

  1. The new chief executive does not reveal a significant gap in capital allocation discipline. The exit condition is a single acquisition exceeding $20 billion at a reported consideration above 1.5 times book value of the target, or one that subsequently requires a goodwill write-down (a formal admission that too much was paid) exceeding $5 billion.

Latest reading: The first notable deployment under the new chief executive was the acquisition of an Occidental Petroleum chemicals subsidiary, completed in January 2026 for approximately $9.5 billion — below the $20 billion threshold and at a price that analysts characterised as defensible rather than extravagant. No goodwill impairment has been recorded on this transaction. The test remains live; the sample size is one.

  1. No structural change to the decentralised owner-operator model. The conditions include the introduction of a regular dividend, a break-up of the conglomerate structure, or a capitulation to activist shareholders — each of which would signal that the governance culture Buffett built is being dismantled.

Latest reading: No dividend was declared in the period. No activist position has been reported. Greg Abel's stated management approach reflects the decentralised model. The succession-related disclosure in the filing for the quarter ending 2026-03-31 was silent on any structural changes. Condition intact.

  1. The price-to-book ratio stays below 2.0 times. Once the ratio exceeds 2.0 times, the valuation fully prices in optimistic compounding, and the opportunity cost of holding rather than switching to a cheaper alternative becomes clearly negative.

Latest reading: The ratio stood at approximately 1.52 times using year-end 2025 equity of $717 billion and a share price of $504 at 2026-07-15 (the implied diluted share count was derived from market capitalisation). The updated figure using the more recent March 2026 equity of $727 billion puts the ratio closer to 1.495 times. Both readings are well below the 2.0 times exit threshold. Condition intact.

  1. Insurance underwriting remains profitable. Float is only free-of-charge leverage when underwriting income is positive — if claims and expenses routinely exceed premiums collected, the float carries a positive cost that erodes the investment advantage.

Latest reading: Combined underwriting pre-tax earnings were $2.265 billion in the three months ending 2026-03-31, up from $1.722 billion in the same period of 2025. The aggregate result improved even as GEICO's individual combined ratio worsened (detailed in What's Wobbling). The float cost remained negative: Berkshire was, in effect, paid to hold its float. Condition intact, with the GEICO sub-trend worth continued monitoring.

The Share Price

The share price of $504 on 2026-07-15 places the stock in the upper half of the hold band. The valuation method used for Berkshire is the price-to-book ratio — the share price divided by the per-share accounting value of the equity. The fair-value anchor is set at 1.45 times book, which reflects the quality of the franchise while accounting for the uncertainty around the new management era. At $504, the ratio is approximately 1.52 times, meaning the share price sits modestly above the 1.45 fair-value line. It is not in cheap territory (that would require a ratio below 1.25 times, corresponding to a share price around $415), nor in expensive territory (the trim line sits at 1.75 times, corresponding to a share price around $581). The buy edge and trim edge have a gap of $166 between them; the current price sits roughly in the upper third of that range, nearer to fair value than to the trim line but above the midpoint.

What's Wobbling

The primary wobble is GEICO's underwriting performance. The combined ratio — the percentage of each dollar of premium consumed by claims and expenses — rose to 87.3% in the three months ending 2026-03-31, up from 79.8% in the same period of 2025. Claims frequency (how often accidents happen) rose 5–7% and claims severity (how much each accident costs to settle) rose 12–14%. Pre-tax underwriting earnings from GEICO fell to $1.416 billion in the quarter, from $2.173 billion a year earlier.

Two factors limit the interpretation. First, the prior-year quarter was unusually clean: near-zero catastrophe losses in early 2025 made the comparison period an outlier rather than a representative benchmark. Second, a combined ratio of 87.3% is still solidly profitable underwriting — every dollar of premium generated 12.7 cents of underwriting profit before accounting for investment income on the float. The concern from the Case for the Bet is not a single bad quarter but a sustained competitive erosion driven by Progressive Insurance's faster pricing technology; one quarter of higher claims frequency does not confirm that thesis. The exit condition for GEICO is framed as a market-share and competitive question, not a combined-ratio print.

Share repurchases were described as "relatively minor" in the filing — approximately $235 million in total during the three months ending 2026-03-31, at an average price near fair value. This is consistent with stated policy: Berkshire buys back shares only when the price is clearly below intrinsic value by a meaningful amount. At a price-to-book ratio near 1.45 times, the shares were judged to be at or modestly above fair value, and restraint was the rational response. The small repurchase activity is a discipline signal, not a policy reversal.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • A large insurance catastrophe event — a major hurricane, earthquake, or industrial accident exceeding roughly $15 billion in net losses — would hit earnings sharply in a single year, temporarily flip the cost of float from negative to positive, and test whether the $369 billion cash reserve acts as a genuine shock absorber or simply a delayed deployment problem.
  • The new chief executive makes a large acquisition above $20 billion at a price that proves difficult to justify — either at too high a multiple of the target's book value, or in a business without a durable competitive advantage. The goodwill write-down that would follow is the clearest early signal, and it can take years to appear.
  • Berkshire's equity portfolio remains heavily concentrated — roughly two-thirds of the portfolio's value sits in five or fewer names, with Apple alone accounting for the largest single position. A simultaneous decline in those core positions would reduce the reported value of the portfolio materially even if the operating businesses are unaffected.
  • GEICO loses ground to competitors using telematics-based pricing — technology that lets insurers price each driver's policy based on how they actually drive, rather than demographic proxies. Progressive Insurance has used this approach to build a pricing advantage. If GEICO is structurally slower to adopt equivalent technology, the combined ratio could deteriorate steadily over a multi-year period.
  • Berkshire Hathaway Energy faces a changed regulatory and legislative environment. A law enacted in July 2025 restricts eligibility for renewable-energy production tax credits on new construction after December 2025, compressing the economics of future clean-energy investment by the energy subsidiary. The existing project portfolio is grandfathered, but the growth flywheel for renewable capex is slower.

Gaps in the Picture

Two data gaps are carried forward from the latest filing analysis.

The share count used to calculate the price-to-book ratio is implied rather than directly disclosed in the regulatory filings. Berkshire's multi-class share structure — Class A shares each equivalent to 1,500 Class B shares — means the standard filing disclosures do not surface a clean Class B equivalent diluted share count. The number used here ($1.09 trillion market capitalisation divided by the $504 share price, giving approximately 2,163 million Class B equivalents) is consistent with the per-share book value that management discloses directly, but it involves one layer of inference.

The Class A equivalent conversion ratio is configured at 1,500:1. This is the established market convention, but the Book Value per Share figure above uses the diluted share count derived from market data rather than a number verified against Berkshire's own per-share disclosure in the filing. The two methods should produce near-identical results; any small divergence would not change the valuation band position.

The Rules

The rules governing what follows from each combination of conditions and share price are set out below. They translate the valuation and thesis framework into six specific if-then clauses.

  • If the thesis conditions are confirmed intact: hold without action and let the compounding continue.
  • If the thesis shows signs of weakening — a driver deteriorating but not yet crossing a written exit threshold: do not add to the position; revisit after the next quarterly filing once the specific deteriorating driver has been examined.
  • If one of the four written exit conditions fires: close the full position within 30 days. No averaging down on a broken thesis.
  • If the share price falls to or below the buy edge ($415): confirm first that the thesis conditions are intact, then add to the position.
  • If the share price rises to or above the trim edge ($581): trim between a quarter and a third of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.
  • If the share price rises to or above the exit edge ($647): close the full position. At that level, the market is pricing in roughly ten years of base-case compounding immediately; the implied forward return falls below what a diversified alternative would deliver, and a full exit is warranted. No re-entry within 90 days.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
