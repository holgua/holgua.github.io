Recent Development

Elastic's annual filing (covering the twelve months to 2026-04-30) confirmed every measurable thesis driver without exception. The headline: cash flow from operations reached $326.9 million for the year, and after subtracting $5.1 million of capital expenditure (purchasing computer equipment and leasehold improvements), the business generated $321.8 million in free cash flow — an 18.5% margin on $1,739.3 million of revenue. That cash margin exceeds the 14% floor that would trigger a reassessment of the bet by 4.5 percentage points.

The cloud transition — the SaaS version of the product versus the self-managed install — continued on schedule. Elastic Cloud (the hosted offering) grew 22% in the year, with the annual-contract portion growing 28%; cloud now accounts for 48% of total revenue, up from 46% a year earlier and 43% the year before. The customer expansion rate (a measure of how much existing customers spend on average compared to the prior year) held at 112% as of 2026-04-30, comfortably above the 105% level that would signal a weakening hold on the installed base. The number of customers spending more than $100,000 a year grew to over 1,720 from 1,510 — a 14% increase.

The operating loss continued to narrow: from $54.9 million in the prior year to $33.5 million in the latest year, a 39% improvement. That trajectory keeps the target of reaching accounting breakeven by the 2028 fiscal year on track. Both acquisitions made during the year — a multimodal embeddings company acquired for $43.4 million and an alerting tool acquired for $10.9 million — were well below the $500 million threshold that would require scrutiny of capital-allocation discipline. The full set of conditions and what the latest filing says about each is in the Case for the Bet. Where the price sits today relative to the valuation bands is in the Share Price.

About Elastic

Elastic N.V. makes Elasticsearch — the most widely used open-source tool for searching through large quantities of data quickly. The company sells subscriptions to an enterprise-grade version of this technology and to a cloud-hosted service built on top of it. Customers embed Elasticsearch so deeply into their data infrastructure that it becomes the central index and retrieval layer for their entire operational stack: logs from servers and applications are stored and queried through it, security events are analysed through it, and, increasingly, the context that large-language-model applications need at query time (a technique called retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG) is served through it. Three product lines share the same underlying data platform — an enterprise search and AI layer, an observability layer (monitoring application performance and infrastructure health), and a security analytics layer. The business model is recurring subscriptions: a customer signs up, gets access to the full platform, and over time tends to use it more rather than less, which is reflected in the 112% customer expansion rate.

Revenue for the twelve months ending 2026-04-30 was $1,739.3 million, growing 17% from the prior year. The company's capital expenditure (spending on physical assets) is minimal — $5.1 million, or 0.3% of revenue — which is consistent with a software-as-a-service business that runs on rented cloud infrastructure rather than owned hardware. The company is headquartered in Mountain View, California, but incorporated in the Netherlands; roughly half of its revenue comes from outside North America.

The Case for the Bet

The central bet is that Elastic's three-use-case platform creates durable switching costs — customers who have indexed their data and built their operational workflows through Elasticsearch face a costly and disruptive migration to any alternative. As long as those switching costs hold, customers keep renewing and expanding, and the business compounds its cash generation. A secondary bet is margin expansion: the transition toward a cloud-hosted model, which carries higher operating leverage than self-managed software, is expected to push the cash flow margin from 18.5% toward 21.5% in the next fiscal year, and further beyond that. Seven observable conditions must keep holding for this to remain a valid bet:

  1. Customer expansion rate above 105%. The expansion rate measures how much existing customers spend this year compared to last year, expressed as a percentage. A reading above 100% means the average customer is spending more — the installed base is growing without needing a single new customer. Falling below 105% for two consecutive quarters would signal that the switching-cost moat is eroding.

Latest reading: 112% as of 2026-04-30. The $100,000-a-year customer tier grew 14% over the prior year to more than 1,720 accounts, confirming that expansion is concentrated in the high-value segment of the base.

  1. Cloud subscription growth above 10% for a full fiscal year. The cloud-hosted offering is the higher-margin, higher-growth portion of the business. If its revenue growth fell below 10% for a full year, that would be consistent with the scenario where AWS's open-source Elasticsearch alternative has commoditised enough of the market to slow Elastic's SaaS transition.

Latest reading: 22% growth for the year ending 2026-04-30 (28% for the annual-contract portion). At 48% of total revenue, the cloud share has grown for three consecutive years.

  1. Cash flow margin above 14%. The cash flow margin (free cash flow as a share of revenue) is the operative measure of the business's earning power; accounting profit is distorted by the way stock-based compensation is treated. Falling below 14% on a full-year view, without a specific one-off cause, would signal that operating costs are rising faster than revenue.

Latest reading: 18.5% for the year ending 2026-04-30. Operating cash flow was $326.9 million; capital expenditure was $5.1 million; the resulting $321.8 million of free cash flow on $1,739.3 million of revenue gives a 4.5 percentage-point cushion above the 14% floor.

  1. GAAP operating loss continuing to narrow toward breakeven by 2028. The accounting operating loss — which deducts all stock-based compensation — has been improving for three consecutive years. If the margin trajectory reverses and GAAP operating loss has not reached zero by the 2028 fiscal year, it invalidates the thesis that the business model eventually becomes profitable on a GAAP basis.

Latest reading: Operating loss of $33.5 million (−1.9% of revenue) for the year ending 2026-04-30, down 39% from $54.9 million (−3.7%) in the prior year. Gross margin expanded to 76% from 74%, providing an additional data point on the direction of travel.

  1. Stock-based compensation below 18% of revenue for consecutive years. Stock-based compensation is a real cost to shareholders (it dilutes existing holders by issuing new shares). The threshold is intentionally set at 18% for two consecutive fiscal years, not one, because a single year above that level could reflect a non-recurring grant cycle.

Latest reading: 17.16% of revenue for the year ending 2026-04-30 (stock-based compensation of $298.4 million on $1,739.3 million revenue), down from 17.38% in the prior year. A footnote-reported figure of 18% includes employer-related payroll taxes on those grants; the pure compensation figure is 17.16%, below the threshold for a second consecutive year.

  1. No acquisition above $500 million at more than 8 times revenue without a disclosed timeline for the deal to generate incremental cash flow. This condition guards against the management team destroying the capital efficiency advantage by overpaying for a large business. The specific numeric test — 8 times annual revenue, three-year accretion disclosure — makes it observable from public filings.

Latest reading: The two acquisitions made in the year ending 2026-04-30 were $43.4 million (an AI embeddings specialist, Jina AI) and $10.9 million (an alerting tool, Keep). Both are well below the $500 million threshold. Capital discipline is intact.

  1. No sustained Datadog revenue growth advantage exceeding 15 percentage points for two consecutive fiscal years, combined with a Gartner Magic Quadrant demotion below the Leaders quadrant. This exit condition requires two adverse signals to fire simultaneously: a competitor widening its growth-rate lead, and an independent analyst affirming a structural loss of competitive position. Either alone is insufficient.

Latest reading: Elastic's own annual filing names Datadog as an observability competitor but does not disclose comparative growth rates or Gartner rankings. This condition can only be assessed from Datadog's own filings and Elastic's investor relations announcements. As of the latest filing review, it is not possible to confirm or deny — the condition is neither triggered nor disproved by the filing alone.

The Share Price

The share-price valuation is based on an earnings yield calculation (free cash flow divided by market capitalisation, expressed as a percentage). A higher yield means a cheaper share price relative to the business's cash generation, and vice versa — the polarity is the opposite of a price-earnings ratio.

At the current price of $62, the yield works out to approximately 4.97% on $321.8 million of annual free cash flow. The hold band runs from 5.50% (the buy edge, equivalent to roughly $56 per share) down to 2.20% (the trim edge, equivalent to roughly $140 per share). At 4.97%, the price is in the lower half of that band — closer to the cheap edge than to the fair-value midpoint. The valuation model's central fair value is $101 per share (a 3.04% yield), which implies the current price is sitting at a 39% discount to that anchor.

A separate scenario analysis using peer and historical trading multiples produces a substantially higher central estimate of $215 per share; why those two anchors diverge is described in Gaps in the Picture.

Why the Market Sees It Differently

The price says the business will earn less over time. Our view says the opposite. The current share price of $62 represents a 38.8% discount to the valuation model's fair-value estimate of $101.

At $62, the market is effectively pricing in a long-run growth rate of around 6.4% a year for Elastic's cash generation. The model's base case assumes 15% — a gap of 8.6 percentage points that is meaningful and not easily dismissed.

The market could be pricing in three things: first, that structural demand for Elastic's search and observability platform is eroding — that the AWS open-source fork has been winning workloads at Elastic's expense, or that customers are consolidating onto fewer platforms. Second, that the operating cost base is less controllable than it appears — that stock-based compensation or infrastructure costs will constrain cash margins below the trajectory that the current numbers imply. Third, that capital allocation has been impaired — that the $340 million share buyback programme executed at an average price of $76.91 (above where the stock trades today) signals poor timing judgement that will recur.

The latest filing refutes the first hypothesis: the customer expansion rate held at 112%, cloud growth came in at 22%, and the revenue pipeline (measured by remaining performance obligations) grew 28% to $1.98 billion. The second hypothesis is also refuted by the filing: cash margins are at 18.5%, operating leverage is clearly visible in expanding gross margins, and management's guidance for the next fiscal year puts the cash margin at 21.5%. The third hypothesis does not hold as stated — the buyback was executed at an average price of $76.91, which is below the $101 fair-value anchor used in the model; by that measure the capital was deployed at a 24% discount to intrinsic value, not above it. The blemish is that the shares have since declined further, making the buyback look poorly timed in hindsight.

Not one of the three hypotheses has clear support in the current data. The 38.8% discount most plausibly reflects the market pricing a much more modest long-run growth rate than the thesis assumes — not because the current numbers are weak, but because competitive pressures from AWS and Datadog make a sustained 15% growth rate difficult to underwrite with confidence.

What's Wobbling

One condition is currently unresolvable from the annual filing alone: the Datadog growth gap and Gartner Magic Quadrant standing. Elastic's own disclosures name Datadog as a competitor but provide no comparative growth figures or analyst rankings. The exit condition requires Datadog's revenue growth to exceed Elastic's by more than 15 percentage points for two consecutive fiscal years AND Elastic's Gartner Observability Magic Quadrant ranking to fall below Leader status — both conditions must be met simultaneously. As of the latest filing, neither is confirmed nor disproved. Competition analysis from external sources (Datadog's own quarterly filings, Elastic investor-relations announcements) puts the current growth gap at roughly 28% versus 17% — an 11-percentage-point gap that is below the 15-point threshold but worth watching. The exit condition has not triggered; the monitoring obligation is active.

There are also two valuation calibration notes that are recorded rather than acted upon. The model's yield-based fair value ($101) diverges from the yield implied by the separate scenario-based model ($215); both are intentional outputs of different methodologies, but the two anchors are not reconciled in the valuation setup, which means the precise buy and trim thresholds would shift materially if the anchor were updated. Additionally, the fair-value reference point (3.04%) was calibrated to an earlier price environment and currently diverges meaningfully from the current yield (4.97%). Neither of these represents a thesis problem — they are calibration notes — but both are relevant before acting on any future buy signal.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • The AWS open-source Elasticsearch fork (maintained by a foundation with several hundred contributors) continues to close the functionality gap. Cost-sensitive customers running self-managed workloads could migrate to the free alternative, slowing new workload wins even as the installed base stays put. Revenue growth decelerating to roughly 5% a year would imply a share value in the range of $36–$42, representing a 35–40% permanent loss from current prices.
  • Hyperscaler-native AI tools — AWS Bedrock, Google Vertex, Microsoft Azure AI Search — absorb the retrieval-augmented-generation market that Elastic is positioning to serve. If enterprises use their existing cloud relationships to fulfil AI search requirements rather than adopting a specialist vendor, the growth re-acceleration thesis does not materialise.
  • Stock-based compensation, currently 17% of revenue, represents a persistent dilution of shareholders' ownership interest. The buyback programme has historically offset most of that dilution, but if the programme is suspended (or if the stock trades above fair value for an extended period, making buybacks uneconomic), the net dilution cost rises.
  • The second chief financial officer in 18 months is a governance yellow flag. Finance leadership continuity matters for the quality of capital-allocation decisions at the margin; a third CFO change within two years would elevate this risk.
  • A securities class action filed in early 2025 is disclosed in the annual report without a settlement or estimated liability. The financial exposure is not quantified; it is a contingent liability rather than a confirmed cost, but it warrants tracking.

Gaps in the Picture

The valuation model contains two fair-value anchors that are not reconciled. The first is the yield-derived central estimate of $101 (based on the current free-cash-flow yield and a 3.04% threshold calibrated from a discounted cash flow model). The second is a scenario-based central estimate of $215 derived from a separate analysis of peer and historical trading multiples. Both are intentional outputs of different methodologies; the $101 figure is used for the buy and trim thresholds because it is the more conservative anchor. A future update to the valuation setup is expected to resolve or explicitly document the divergence.

The fair-value reference point (3.04%) also diverges from the current free-cash-flow yield (4.97%) by more than 60%. This divergence does not affect the position of the buy and trim thresholds (which are set in yield terms, not in price terms), but it means the "$101 fair value" is not a live market-price target — it is the price at which the yield would equal the 3.04% calibration point, using the current free-cash-flow base.

The competitive-position condition cannot be monitored from Elastic's own filings. Datadog's comparative revenue growth and the Gartner Observability Magic Quadrant standing require external sources.

The risk-free rate used in the conviction score was read from a US equity index fund's market data snapshot, not from Elastic's own market data file; this is the standard fallback for company holdings.

The Rules

The rules governing what happens under each combination of thesis state and share price are as follows:

  • If the thesis is confirmed — all conditions hold, no exit trigger has fired, and the share price is within the band — hold and monitor. The expansion rate and cloud revenue growth are the two metrics to confirm each quarter; the stock-based compensation trajectory is confirmed at each annual filing. No new position should be added unless the price drops to the buy edge.
  • If the thesis has weakened — one or more conditions are deteriorating but no exit threshold has been crossed — do not add to the position. If the expansion rate drops toward 107% or cloud growth decelerates below 15% for two consecutive quarters, a fresh review is warranted. Two consecutive quarters of deterioration are required before reducing; a single quarter is insufficient to confirm a structural change.
  • If the thesis has broken — any of the seven written exit conditions has fired — close the full position within 30 days. The conditions are: expansion rate below 105% for two consecutive quarters; cloud growth below 10% for a full fiscal year; cash flow margin below 14% on an annual view; stock-based compensation above 18% for two consecutive fiscal years; an acquisition above $500 million at more than 8 times revenue without a three-year cash-flow-accretion disclosure; GAAP operating margin not positive by the 2028 fiscal year; or Datadog's growth gap above 15 percentage points for two fiscal years combined with a Gartner Observability Magic Quadrant ranking below Leader status. No averaging down on a broken thesis.
  • If the price falls below the buy edge — confirm the thesis is intact first. If intact, add in tranches at a yield above 5.50% (approximately $56 per share on the current free-cash-flow base). No more than half the intended position in a single fill; the bear-case scenario implies a price near $42, making staged entry with confirmation between tranches the appropriate posture.
  • If the price rises above the trim edge — trim roughly 25% of the position when the yield falls below 2.20% (approximately $140 per share on the current free-cash-flow base), to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.
  • If the price rises above the exit edge — close the entire remaining position if the free-cash-flow yield falls below 1.50% (approximately $200 per share). At that price the market is paying for the full bull-case scenario; the forward return on continued holding falls below the long-run equity premium, and reallocation becomes the stronger option. No re-entry within 90 days to avoid anchoring to the previous exit price.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
