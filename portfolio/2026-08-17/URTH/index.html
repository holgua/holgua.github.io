About All World

The iShares MSCI World fund tracks the MSCI World Index — a benchmark covering roughly 1,300 large and mid-cap companies across 23 developed-market countries. The index spans the United States (approximately 70% of the basket), Japan, the United Kingdom, France, Germany, Switzerland, Canada, and fifteen other developed economies. It is a float-adjusted, market-capitalisation-weighted index, meaning the biggest companies by market value command the biggest weights. Technology companies account for roughly 30% of the basket; financial services for about 17%; industrials for 11%; healthcare for 9%. The remaining sectors each sit below 9%.

The fund's role in the portfolio is diversification away from a purely US-focused exposure. Because it dilutes the United States' weight to roughly 70% and allocates the remainder to other developed markets, it narrows the regime-risk that comes with a single-country passive allocation. The cost of that diversification is modest: an annual expense ratio of 0.24%, and a currency drag that arises when the reporting currency differs from the dollar.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that a globally diversified basket of developed-market companies, held at a price where its collective earnings yield exceeds the risk-free rate by at least two percentage points, will compound capital acceptably over a long holding period:

  1. Equity risk premium stays positive. The basket's earnings yield (the share of each dollar invested that flows through as collective profits) must exceed the 10-year US Treasury rate by at least zero percentage points, with the ambition of eventually reclaiming a two-percentage-point premium. Two consecutive quarters of negative premium — meaning Treasuries pay more per dollar than the basket's look-through earnings — trigger exit condition (1).

  1. Annual expense ratio stays below 0.35%. The fund charges 0.24% a year; a rise above 0.35% with no compensating fee response triggers exit condition (2). The comparable UCITS structure available to European investors runs at 0.20%, so a further hike would signal fee drift that the cheaper alternative does not require.

Latest reading: 2026-08-17. Expense ratio 0.24%, 11 basis points (hundredths of a percentage point) below the ceiling. Status: within bounds.

The Share Price

The basket's earnings yield — the collective profit of roughly 1,300 companies expressed as a percentage of what the market charges for them — currently stands at 4.19%. The pre-committed framework defines fair value as the yield level of 5.0%, which at today's earnings would imply a share price of approximately $170. The current price of $202 sits above that fair-value anchor: meaningfully above fair value in the terms of the scale, though still within the band that separates fair value from the trim edge at $212.

Separately from the valuation position, the fund is trading at a discount of 4.19% to the underlying net asset value of its holdings (net asset value is the per-share value of all the securities the fund holds, calculated daily). The fund's rules permit a discount of up to 3.0% before the structural guardrail fires; the current 4.19% gap exceeds that ceiling, and the ceiling is a hard exit trigger.

The Standing Orders

The share-price bands translate into three standing orders and two price alerts. Each operationalises a pre-committed instruction.

Price alerts (informational).
- A first alert at $170, the fair-value anchor — the price level that corresponds to a basket earnings yield of 5.0%. Crossing it means the share price has returned to a neutral reading; no action follows automatically, but a fresh read of the conditions is warranted.
- A second alert at $136, the cheap-edge — the price level corresponding to a 6.25% basket earnings yield. Crossing it arms the buy order below; before adding, the latest re-validation should confirm conditions are still working and no integrity breach remains open.

Standing orders (actionable).
- A standing buy order at $136. Adding in tranches is called for once the share price closes at or below this line, provided the integrity conditions are clear and the equity risk premium is positive. The order may be staged across one or two quarters rather than filled in a single trade.
- A standing trim order at $212. Trimming 25–33% of the position is called for when the share price closes at or above this line, to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.
- A standing exit order at $283. Closing the full position is called for when the share price closes at or above this line — the basket would be priced to a 3.0% earnings yield, fully priced relative to the risk-free rate even before any equity premium.

Vehicle-integrity breaches and the named exit-condition triggers (persistent negative equity risk premium, fee drift, fund-size collapse, concentration spike, methodology change, quality collapse) are independent additional paths to exit: any one of them fires the exit instruction regardless of where the share price sits within the band.

What's Wobbling

Two signals are active. The first is the NAV deviation — the gap between what the fund's shares cost on the exchange and what the underlying holdings are worth per share. At 4.19%, this deviation exceeds the 3.0% ceiling, making it the primary active breach. The deviation may reflect illiquidity in some of the fund's developed-market constituents (securities that are slower to trade than US stocks, creating a lag between their true value and what arbitrageurs can efficiently close), creation and redemption friction, or a data-quality issue at the data source. No diagnosis is required before acting; the breach alone is sufficient.

The second signal is the equity risk premium, which has been negative for four consecutive pipeline snapshots. At −0.53 percentage points, the basket's earnings yield is running below the 10-year US Treasury rate, meaning a dollar in Treasuries delivers more look-through income than a dollar in the basket. The formal calendar gate for this condition sits at two consecutive quarters. The first quarter of sustained negativity (from 2026-06-16) is already past; the second quarter will be assessable at the end of September 2026. Neither crossing the The Case for the Bet thresholds for the equity-risk-premium condition in the calendar sense has yet been formally confirmed — but the NAV deviation breach, which has no calendar gate, has already fired.

The remaining four conditions are all within their respective bounds: look-through quality (median return on equity 37.3%), expense ratio (0.24%), fund size ($8.0 billion), and single-name concentration (5.16%). The bet's structural premises about basket quality and vehicle operability remain intact on those four measures.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Bear market with a de-rating. If global developed-market earnings contract and the market simultaneously reduces the multiple it is willing to pay for them — both legs moving adversely at once — a five-year annualised return of roughly −20% is the calibrated outcome. That is the scenario where the bet delivers the worst of both worlds: lower earnings and a cheaper market price per dollar of those earnings.
  • Sustained negative equity risk premium. If the Treasury rate remains above the basket's earnings yield for two consecutive quarters, the calendar gate on exit condition (1) fires independently of the NAV deviation breach.
  • Expense ratio increase without fee response. A rise above 0.35% with no competitive response from the fund manager would trigger exit condition (2). The comparable fund structure available to non-US investors charges 0.20% annually; the gap is already 4 basis points, and a further hike widens it.
  • Fund-size collapse. A roughly 75% fall in total assets — from approximately $8 billion to below $2 billion — would trigger exit condition (3). At that level, trading costs typically widen for a global blend fund. A crash of this magnitude in a developed-market index fund would imply a severe and sustained global equity bear market.
  • Index-methodology change. A material restructuring of the MSCI World Index — reclassifying countries, dropping major developed-market constituents, or shifting away from float-adjusted market capitalisation — would undermine the mandate. No automated signal monitors this; it requires periodic manual review.

The Rules

The pre-committed rules for this holding, stated as conditions:

  • If the thesis is confirmed (basket reasonably priced and the vehicle is sound): hold the position.
  • If the thesis is weakened (basket earnings yield failing the equity risk premium test, or look-through quality deteriorating): do not add. Re-validate after the next quarter. If the equity risk premium stays negative or median look-through quality keeps falling, swap to the lower-cost UCITS equivalent at 0.20% or move to cash per the position-sizing rule.
  • If the thesis is broken (vehicle integrity breached or any exit condition triggered): exit. Move proceeds to risk-free instruments or to a holding that meets the value test.
  • If the price falls below the buy band (basket earnings yield above 6.25%): add to the position. The basket is meaningfully cheap relative to the risk-free rate.
  • If the price rises above the trim band (basket earnings yield below 4.0%): trim 25–33% of the position. The basket is meaningfully expensive relative to the risk-free rate; harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.
  • If the price rises above the exit band (basket earnings yield at or below 3.0%): exit. The basket is fully priced relative to risk-free instruments. Redeploy proceeds to risk-free instruments or to a holding that meets the value test.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
