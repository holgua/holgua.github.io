Recent Development

The most significant news from the three months ending in March 2026 was a legal victory that had nothing to do with selling insurance or moving freight: a state appeal court in Oregon reversed a jury verdict that had exposed Berkshire's energy subsidiary, Berkshire Hathaway Energy, to substantial wildfire liability. That reversal sharply reduced what had been the largest single overhang on the investment — a legal bill that, in the worst case, threatened to consume several years of earnings from the energy business. In the same period, the energy subsidiary agreed to sell its Washington state power operations to Portland General Electric for roughly $1.9 billion in cash, further simplifying the liability picture.

On the operating side, the first-quarter results confirmed the compounding story is still intact. The freight railroad BNSF turned in its best quarterly earnings growth in recent memory, with pre-tax profit up 13.5% against the prior year's comparable period. The insurance float — the pool of premiums held before claims are paid, which Berkshire invests for its own account at no cost — edged higher to approximately $176.9 billion and continued to cost nothing to hold, which is the defining financial advantage of the business.

The one wobble, visible in the Bottom Line and examined in detail in What's Wobbling, came from GEICO. Claims frequency and severity rose, pushing GEICO's combined ratio (the share of every premium dollar consumed by claims and expenses) from 79.8% a year earlier to 87.3%. The absolute number still reflects profitable underwriting — any ratio below 100% means the insurance operation earns money before even touching the investment returns — but the direction of travel warrants watching.

About Berkshire

Berkshire Hathaway is not one business — it is a collection of very different businesses held together by a single capital-allocation engine. The largest asset is the insurance operation, which includes GEICO (the direct-to-consumer car insurer), Berkshire Hathaway Reinsurance Group (which writes large, unusual risks for other insurers), and several specialty lines. What makes the insurance business unusual is the float: when a customer pays a premium, Berkshire holds that money until a claim is filed, and in the meantime invests it for its own account. At roughly $176.9 billion, this pool is the largest held by any private company in the world. The cost of maintaining it has been at or below zero for most of the past decade — meaning Berkshire earns investment returns on money it did not have to raise.

The second-largest business is BNSF, the freight railroad that carries agricultural products, consumer goods, and coal across the western United States. BNSF and its sole western competitor, Union Pacific, together handle the bulk of freight that cannot economically move by truck across the continent. The third large operating unit, Berkshire Hathaway Energy, owns regulated electricity and natural-gas utilities across several US states. Alongside these, Berkshire owns a manufacturing and services division that includes Precision Castparts (aerospace components), Lubrizol (specialty chemicals), and dozens of smaller industrial businesses.

Sitting above all of this is a $369 billion pile of cash and short-term government bonds — held at year-end 2025 and earning a market rate of interest while waiting for the right acquisition. The company also holds a $298 billion portfolio of publicly traded shares, concentrated mainly in Apple, Bank of America, and American Express. The combined picture is part operating conglomerate, part investment holding company — and the returns depend on whether the person making the big capital decisions can sustain the discipline that defined the business for six decades.

The Case for the Bet

The central bet is that Greg Abel and the culture Warren Buffett built at Berkshire will keep compounding retained earnings and acquired float into high-returning businesses and securities at rates exceeding what can be achieved by owning a diversified index instead. The position is sized as a long-duration core holding, not a trade.

Four conditions must keep holding for the bet to remain valid:

  1. Book value per share growth must not trail the MSCI World index total return for five or more consecutive years. If it does, that is the statistical signal that the compounding edge — the reason to hold this rather than an index fund — has disappeared. The MSCI World comparison sets the bar at what can be achieved without any analytical effort.

Latest reading: Shareholders' equity grew 10.5% in the full year 2025, reaching $717.4 billion, and added a further $9.8 billion in the three months ending in March 2026 to reach $727.2 billion. The per-B-share book value is approximately $337 at the latest quarterly close. No multi-year underperformance against the index is in evidence.

  1. No large acquisition that signals inferior capital discipline. The specific threshold is a transaction above $20 billion at a reported price above 1.5 times the book value of the target, or any deal that subsequently requires a goodwill write-down (impairment) exceeding $5 billion. The threshold is deliberately high — Berkshire has a long history of paying up for quality — but a deal breaching both the size and price criteria simultaneously would be a material negative signal.

Latest reading: Abel's first significant deployment as CEO was OxyChem — the chemical manufacturing business of Occidental Petroleum — acquired in January 2026 for approximately $9.5 billion. The transaction does not breach the $20 billion threshold and was made at a defensible price. No goodwill impairment has been recorded.

  1. No structural culture change that signals the decentralised owner-operator model is being abandoned. The specific triggers are: initiation of a regular dividend (Berkshire has never paid one), a break-up of the conglomerate structure, or capitulation to activist pressure that forces a reallocation of capital away from the model.

Latest reading: The quarterly filing for the three months ending in March 2026 contains no indication of any such change. No dividend was announced, no break-up plans are disclosed, and no activist activity is reported.

  1. The price-to-book ratio must not exceed 2.0 times. Above 2.0×, the valuation fully prices in optimistic compounding assumptions, and the opportunity cost versus owning a passive index fund becomes clearly negative.

Latest reading: At the current price of $504, the price-to-book ratio stands at approximately 1.52× using the most recent balance sheet figures. This is well below the 2.0× ceiling.

The Share Price

At $504 per share, the price-to-book ratio is approximately 1.52, which sits in the upper half of the defined hold range — above the fair-value anchor of 1.45 times book ($481 per share), but well short of the trim-band ceiling of 1.75 times book ($581 per share).

The shares are not cheap. But they are not expensive either. The current price is modestly above the fair-value anchor, meaning the market assigns a small premium beyond what the analysis judges to be a neutral valuation. A premium of this size is within ordinary fluctuation for a large, well-followed stock. Put plainly: the shares trade at roughly fair value.

What's Wobbling

GEICO's combined ratio — the single metric that best captures whether the car-insurance operation is running profitably — rose from 79.8% in the three months ending in March 2025 to 87.3% in the same period of 2026. Claims came in more frequently (up roughly 5–7%) and each claim cost more to settle (severity up roughly 12–14%). Pre-tax underwriting earnings from GEICO fell from $2.2 billion to $1.4 billion year-on-year.

Two mitigating factors argue against reading too much into a single quarter. First, the prior-year comparison quarter was unusually clean — catastrophe losses across the broader Berkshire insurance portfolio were near zero in early 2025, which inflated the baseline. Second, a combined ratio of 87.3% still represents profitable underwriting; the exit condition for the GEICO driver is framed around market-share erosion and competitive positioning, not a single quarter of elevated claims.

The more structural concern is competitive positioning. Progressive, GEICO's primary direct-to-consumer rival, uses telematics-based pricing (driving-behaviour data collected from a smartphone or a plug-in device) to price risk more accurately and attract better-risk customers. GEICO has been slower to adopt this approach. The policies-in-force count — which directly measures whether the business is gaining or losing customers — is not separately disclosed in quarterly filings, limiting real-time assessment of the competitive trend. That data point appears only in the annual report and the annual shareholder letter.

Separately, the repurchase programme was described as "relatively minor" — approximately $235 million spent in the quarter. This is consistent with disciplined capital allocation rather than a change in policy: management buys shares only when price is clearly below intrinsic value, and at 1.52× book, the shares are not there yet.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • The capital allocation question is unresolved. Abel has made one significant acquisition at this stage and it was defensible but not transformative. Two or three more deployments are needed before an honest assessment of his allocation quality is possible. A large deal done at the wrong price could permanently impair returns.
  • A major catastrophe could simultaneously test the insurance operation and the balance sheet. A large hurricane or earthquake hitting a concentrated area could generate net losses exceeding $15 billion. The insurance float cost would turn positive for a period, and the competitive advantage of the float engine would narrow temporarily.
  • GEICO's competitive position could erode further. If Progressive's telematics pricing pulls customers away at scale, GEICO's combined ratio would deteriorate for structural rather than cyclical reasons — and structural deterioration is harder to reverse than a bad claims season.
  • The energy subsidiary carries residual wildfire exposure. The Oregon Court of Appeals reversal of the first-phase verdict is a significant positive, but the second phase and subsequent phases of the PacifiCorp wildfire litigation remain unresolved. Cumulative accruals stand at $2.9 billion against $2.3 billion paid to date.
  • The clean energy tax credit phase-out compresses future returns at Berkshire Hathaway Energy. New eligibility for clean-energy production credits was restricted for projects beginning construction after December 2025. Existing credits are grandfathered, but the economics of future renewable investment are less attractive than they were.

Gaps in the Picture

  • Shares outstanding are not directly reported in the structured financial filings. Berkshire's dual-class share structure means the standard reporting tags do not capture total share count. The diluted-share count used in this analysis is derived from the market capitalisation divided by the current share price — consistent with the per-share book value disclosed in the quarterly filing, but derived rather than sourced directly.
  • Policies-in-force data for GEICO is not disclosed in quarterly filings. The most useful competitive signal for the GEICO driver — whether the business is gaining or losing customers — appears only in the annual report and the annual shareholder letter, creating a nine-to-twelve-month lag in the competitive monitoring.
  • OxyChem's contribution is unverifiable at this stage. The acquisition closed in January 2026 and no full-year operational results are available. The assessment of Abel's capital allocation quality — the central uncertainty for the thesis — cannot be resolved until at least the FY2026 results are disclosed.

The Rules

If the thesis is confirmed and the price sits within the hold band, hold and allow the position to compound. No action is required.

If any single thesis driver weakens without breaking — for example, if GEICO's combined ratio deteriorates sharply but does not cross the 100% threshold for multiple consecutive quarters — hold the existing position without adding. Re-run the thesis validation when the next quarterly filing is published, and make the decision about whether the evidence has shifted.

If any exit condition fires — the specific ones named in the Case for the Bet — exit the full position within 30 days. No averaging down in this scenario.

If the price falls to or below the buy-band edge of $415, first confirm the thesis is still intact. If confirmed, add to the position.

If the price rises to or above the trim-band edge of $581, trim 25–33% of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.

If the price rises to or above the exit-band edge of $647, exit the full position. The market is pricing in roughly 10 years of base-case compounding instantly at that reading; redeploy the proceeds at the next cheap-edge crossing or into a cheaper alternative. No re-entry within 90 days — the cooling-off period defuses the pull of a familiar name.
