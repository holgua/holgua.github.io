Amazon — A Quality Compounder at a Fair Price

Report date: 24 June 2026 | Ticker: AMZN | Price at last re-validation: $250.02

TL;DR

Buy at $176. Trim at $263. Exit at $421.

At $250, Amazon sits comfortably within the hold band — above fair value ($176) but well short of the trim edge ($263). The thesis is intact. All the indicators that matter are pointing in the right direction. The one thing this stock is not, right now, is cheap.

What Amazon actually is

Most people think of Amazon as a shop that delivers things very quickly. That framing is twenty years out of date. Today Amazon is three businesses glued together by the same infrastructure, the same customer relationships, and the same brand.

The first business is the retail and logistics platform. North America alone generated $426 billion in sales in FY2025 at a 6.9 per cent operating margin, and the International segment — which covers everything outside the United States — added another $162 billion and finally crossed into profitability. The stores are the cash-generative engine. They are not where the investment story lives.

The second business is Amazon Web Services, hereafter AWS. AWS is a cloud computing infrastructure franchise that generated $129 billion in revenue in FY2025 at a 35.4 per cent operating margin. That margin figure bears repeating: AWS earned $45.6 billion in operating profit on $129 billion of revenue. The segment represents 18 per cent of consolidated sales and 57 per cent of consolidated operating income. There is no other division in mega-cap technology that produces this kind of profit concentration on this kind of revenue base. The moat in cloud is real and specific: enterprise customers sign multi-year reserved-capacity contracts, lock proprietary workloads into AWS-native databases and machine-learning tooling, and face genuine switching friction that keeps them renewing rather than migrating.

The third business is advertising. Amazon's advertising services segment — sponsored product listings, display ads, Prime Video spots — generated $68.6 billion in FY2025, up 22 per cent on the prior year. For context, that is a larger advertising business than the entirety of Netflix, Spotify, and most legacy media companies combined. It runs at near-zero marginal cost on top of the retail infrastructure, which is what makes it so valuable: Amazon already knows what people intend to buy, because they are in the middle of buying it.

The investment thesis is that these three engines are still in their compounding phases. AWS is not a mature utility — its revenue growth re-accelerated to 28 per cent year-on-year in Q1 2026 as AI workload adoption picked up. Advertising is still growing at high-twenties rates. International is just turning the corner on profitability. If all three keep compounding simultaneously for another three to five years, the operating income trajectory is extraordinary.

The number that matters most

For a company of Amazon's complexity, cutting through to a single valuation metric is necessary. The one used here is owner earnings yield: what the business generates in free cash flow, after capital spending, as a percentage of total market value.

Using FY2025 financials: - Operating cash flow: $139.5 billion - Capital expenditure (on an XBRL-reported basis): $6.7 billion - Free cash flow: $132.8 billion - Less stock-based compensation: $19.5 billion - Owner earnings (post-compensation): $113.3 billion - Market capitalisation at $250: $2.69 trillion - Owner earnings yield: 4.2 per cent

The fair value anchor is a 6.0 per cent yield, which implies a share price of roughly $176. A yield above 7.5 per cent — implying a price below $140 — is the buy zone. A yield below 4.0 per cent — price above $263 — is the trim zone.

At $250, the yield is 4.2 per cent: within the band, but sitting near the upper edge and moving toward it as the price has risen from 2026 lows around $184. There is no margin of safety here. There is not supposed to be one for a position being held rather than purchased fresh.

The big bet: AI infrastructure converts to revenue

Everything interesting about Amazon in 2026 comes back to one question: does the $128 billion of capital expenditure Amazon deployed in FY2025 — and the still-larger figure guided for FY2026 — actually convert into AWS revenue growth, the way the prior buildout cycle (2017–2022) converted into the current $45.6 billion operating income franchise?

The early evidence is encouraging. AWS grew 28 per cent year-on-year in Q1 2026. The contracted backlog stands at $364 billion across a 5.5-year weighted average — real, signed enterprise commitments to future AWS spending. Anthropic, which received a $15 billion investment from Amazon and committed to spending $100 billion on AWS compute over the next decade, is the anchor tenant that no competitor can replicate.

Against this: capital expenditure is running at approximately $147 billion on a trailing-twelve-month basis, nearly double the prior year. The AWS operating margin compressed modestly — from 39.4 per cent in Q1 2025 to 37.7 per cent in Q1 2026 — as the new infrastructure investment ran ahead of the revenue it will eventually serve. That compression is exactly what the investment thesis expected and accepted. The exit condition is not modest margin compression; it is sustained margin compression below 28 per cent across two consecutive quarters. That threshold is nearly 1,000 basis points below where the margin currently sits.

The tariff environment adds a second layer of complexity. China-based sellers account for meaningful portions of Amazon's third-party seller services and advertising revenues, and the company has explicitly flagged tariff policy changes as a risk. North American operating income still grew 41 per cent year-on-year in Q1 2026 despite this headwind, which is the most important piece of counter-evidence — so far, the exposure is being managed.

Three futures, and what they are worth

Because Amazon's outcome genuinely depends on an investment cycle that has not yet concluded, thinking in scenarios is more honest than a single point estimate.

Bear (25% probability): AWS growth decelerates to mid-teens as Microsoft's Azure and Google's cloud platform close the model-quality gap. The $147 billion capital expenditure wave produces excess capacity, and depreciation on that stranded infrastructure compresses operating margins toward 9 per cent. Simultaneously, the Federal Trade Commission's ongoing antitrust case produces a structural remedy — not a full break-up, but forced algorithmic restrictions on the Prime/marketplace flywheel. Under this scenario, three-year free cash flow reaches $30 billion, and the implied share value is approximately $74. The annualised return from $250 is −13.4 per cent per year.

Base (50% probability): AWS sustains 18–22 per cent growth. Enterprise AI adoption broadens from model training to inference workloads, validating the infrastructure investment. North America stores deliver 50–80 basis points of annual margin improvement. Advertising continues compounding above 18 per cent. Capital expenditure normalises toward 13–15 per cent of revenue by FY2027, and free cash flow expands toward $75 billion. The FTC case settles with behavioural constraints, not separation. The implied share value after three years is approximately $275 at current multiples. The annualised return from $250 is +3.2 per cent per year.

Bull (25% probability): AWS accelerates to 25 per cent sustained growth as Anthropic's Bedrock ecosystem creates a self-reinforcing model-to-deployment flywheel. Project Kuiper — Amazon's low-earth-orbit satellite broadband initiative — reaches commercial scale and opens a new revenue vector. The International segment reaches 5–6 per cent operating margin. Free cash flow reaches $110 billion by year three. The implied share value is approximately $511. The annualised return from $250 is +26.9 per cent per year.

Probability-weighted outcome: approximately +5.0 per cent per year over three years. That is a healthy equity premium above the current risk-free rate of 4.0 per cent — but only modestly so, and the distribution is not comfortable: the bear case implies permanent capital impairment of roughly 70 per cent from today's price.

What makes this worth owning anyway

A +5 per cent probability-weighted return is not the reason to own Amazon. The reason is what the business is compounding into.

The marketplace network effect has been running for 25 years. Third-party sellers generated $172 billion in services revenue on the platform in FY2025. The buyer-seller density creates a flywheel that nobody has replicated at scale. The FBA (Fulfillment by Amazon) logistics system, which ships third-party goods as efficiently as first-party goods, is the mechanism that makes Prime worth renewing — and $49.6 billion in annual Prime subscription revenue demonstrates how sticky that renewal is.

AWS switching costs are structural. Long-term reserved-instance contracts, purpose-built Trainium silicon for AI workloads, and security clearance requirements for government cloud work create friction that keeps customers on platform. The NSA and CIA cloud contracts represent a category of infrastructure where switching costs are measured in years and classified clearances, not pricing spreadsheets.

Advertising is the quietest compounder of the three. Its unit economics are exceptional because the marginal cost of an additional advertising impression on a high-intent commerce platform approaches zero. The $68.6 billion it generated in FY2025 carries margins that are far superior to the consolidated average.

The balance sheet is genuinely strong. Amazon holds approximately $21 billion more in cash than it owes in long-term debt. Interest coverage is 25 times earnings before interest and tax. The business can afford the investment cycle it is in.

The risks that matter

The FTC antitrust case is the one risk that could permanently impair the business model rather than just compress earnings temporarily. If the outcome includes mandatory API access to marketplace infrastructure or forced algorithmic restrictions on the Prime/FBA cross-subsidy, the flywheel that makes Prime economics work is mechanically damaged. This is not a probability-of-loss risk; it is a severity-of-loss risk. Even at moderate probability, it demands monitoring.

The regulatory environment is currently more favourable than at any point since 2015, partly because of the Bezos-Trump relationship and partly because of the AWS federal cloud footprint. That favourability is personal and transactional — it reversed once before, between 2017 and 2020, at material cost to shareholders. The classified AWS contracts are genuinely bipartisan-durable. The retail regulatory environment is not.

Capital allocation remains a source of moderate frustration. The $6.1 billion share repurchase authorisation issued in March 2022 has not been used, despite the stock trading at $80–103 during 2022–2023. That was demonstrably cheap given what Amazon has earned since. No buybacks have occurred in Q1 2025 or Q1 2026, while the company issued over $50 billion in new debt to fund AI infrastructure. This pattern is consistent with the thesis assessment that Amazon's capital allocation is "acceptable but not excellent."

Insider selling deserves a note without alarm. Jeff Bezos disposed of approximately $5.65 billion of shares in the last twelve months via pre-planned trading arrangements. A May 2026 Form 144 filing covers a further 25 million shares. These are liquidity transactions from an 8.8 per cent beneficial owner with $237 billion of personal exposure to the stock. They are not exit signals. The written threshold that would change the assessment is a single filing covering more than 40 million shares — nearly double the recent rate.

The verdict in plain language

Amazon at $250 is working. The business is in good health. The three compounding engines are all operating as the thesis predicted. The most important driver — AWS revenue growth — re-accelerated to 28 per cent in Q1 2026, well above the 18 per cent threshold the thesis requires and far from the 15 per cent floor at which the exit conditions begin.

The valuation is not cheap. At a 4.2 per cent owner earnings yield against a fair-value anchor of 6.0 per cent, the price is above intrinsic value. A strict value investor's margin of safety — buying at a 25 per cent discount to the $182 base-case intrinsic value — would require a price near $136. That level has not been available in 2025 or 2026.

For someone who already owns Amazon at a lower cost basis, the current position merits holding. The thesis is intact, no exit condition has been triggered, and continuing to compound the AWS and advertising flywheel is the rational action. For someone who does not own the stock, this is not an entry point — there is no price margin of safety for a fresh purchase, and a 5 per cent probability-weighted annual return does not adequately compensate for the 70 per cent downside in the bear scenario.

The price to watch for a trim is $263 — the point at which the owner earnings yield falls to 4.0 per cent and the thesis would say harvest a quarter to a third of the position. At $421, the yield falls to 3.0 per cent and the full position exits. Both numbers require material price appreciation from today's level and are not near-term concerns.

What to watch next

Three numbers will tell the story before any other indicator:

  1. AWS quarterly revenue growth. The threshold is 18 per cent. Below 15 per cent for two consecutive quarters, and the AI conversion thesis is not working.
  2. AWS operating margin. Currently 37.7 per cent. The exit floor is 28 per cent. Compression toward 30 per cent is a watch signal, not an action signal.
  3. FTC antitrust case. A settlement with behavioural conditions is within the band. Structural separation language in any ruling or preliminary injunction is the exit trigger, regardless of price.

The next quarterly filing covers Q2 2026. Prime Day falls within Q2, which typically inflates the North America retail numbers and provides a read on marketplace health. The more important number will be the AWS figure — whether the 28 per cent Q1 acceleration holds, accelerates further, or shows early signs of mean-reversion toward the mid-twenties.
