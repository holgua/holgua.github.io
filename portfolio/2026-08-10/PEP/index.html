The Bottom Line

Data as of 2026-08-10 · Latest filing 2026-07-09.

The bet is wobbling. PepsiCo's underlying business — its brands, its gross profit, its cash generation — is doing broadly what the thesis said it would, but one persistent drag keeps the verdict in the amber zone rather than the green. At $139, the share price sits in the middle of the hold band: not cheap enough to add, not dear enough to trim. At fair value, the long-run return is a modest equity premium above the risk-free rate — adequate compensation, but not the kind that rewards adding more. The one condition to watch is PepsiCo's beverage volume in North America (the PepsiCo Beverages North America segment, which covers Pepsi, Gatorade, Mountain Dew, and similar drinks in the United States and Canada): it has now declined for at least nine consecutive quarters with no sign of turning positive, and until it does — or until the operating profit margin drops below the exit floor and the volume decline becomes the second leg of the exit trigger — the verdict stays hold, collect the dividend, and wait.

Recent Development

The latest quarterly report, for the 24 weeks ending 2026-06-13 (filed 2026-07-09), delivered a mixed picture that left the overall verdict unchanged: the business is holding its ground at the gross profit level while one segment continues to lose ground on volume.

The headline number that matters for the thesis is PepsiCo Beverages North America volume: unit sales declined 4% in the twelve-week period and 3% across the full twenty-four weeks, with both carbonated soft drinks and non-carbonated beverages falling. That extends an unbroken run of quarterly volume declines to at least nine reporting periods. The exit trigger requires this volume decline to coexist with a sharp drop in the operating profit margin — and that second condition is nowhere close to firing: the consolidated profit margin for the period was 16.6%, far above the 11.5% floor written into the exit conditions. One leg of the trigger is active; the other is not. The verdict stays amber.

The positives are genuine. Gross profit as a share of revenue was 54.7% for the first half and 54.2% for the twelve-week period — inside the 53–55% band the thesis requires — confirming that brand pricing power continues to absorb commodity cost pressures at the gross level. Free cash flow recovered sharply: the first half generated $1.17 billion of cash compared with a $342 million outflow in the same period a year earlier. The annual dividend was raised 4% to $5.92 per share, extending a consecutive-growth streak now in its 55th year. Both of those readings confirm the moat is structurally intact even as the North American beverage business loses ground on unit volumes. For the full valuation picture, see the Share Price.

About PepsiCo

PepsiCo is two businesses that happen to share a balance sheet. The first and larger is a global snack empire: through its Frito-Lay division it makes the crisps, tortilla chips, and corn snacks — Lay's, Doritos, Cheetos, Fritos — that sit on roughly 60% of American supermarket shelves in that category. The second is a beverage operation: Pepsi, Mountain Dew, Gatorade, Lipton iced tea, Aquafina water, and, since 2025, the wellness drinks brand poppi. Both halves sell through what the company calls a Direct-Store-Delivery network — a fleet of trucks and drivers that delivers product directly to retailers rather than through an intermediary warehouse, giving PepsiCo unusually strong relationships with shop-floor managers and unusually good visibility into what is selling. Replicating that network would cost any competitor several billion dollars and several years of capital spending; that cost is a meaningful barrier to entry.

Money enters from two directions: consumers buy the products, and the gross profit margin — the share of every sales dollar that remains after paying for ingredients, packaging, and manufacturing — has held in a narrow band of 53–55% for five consecutive years, even through a period of significant cost inflation and tariff increases. The business then needs to cover its distribution, marketing, and administrative costs, plus roughly $4.4 billion a year in capital investment to maintain the manufacturing and distribution infrastructure. What remains after all of that — normalised across three fiscal years to smooth out single-year distortions — is approximately $8.96 billion a year. That figure is the foundation of the valuation.

The Case for the Bet

PepsiCo is held on the premise that its combination of dominant brands and a distribution network that cannot be replicated quickly gives the business a durable right to earn high gross profit margins through most economic conditions. Five observable conditions must keep holding for that premise to remain valid:

  1. Operating profit margin must not fall below 11.5% for two consecutive quarters while beverage volume in North America is simultaneously negative. The two conditions together — not either alone — constitute the exit trigger for thesis breakdown. The 11.5% floor was set at the level that signals the FY2025 margin compression (caused by a one-off asset write-down and tariff-driven cost increases) has become structural rather than temporary. A prolonged operating margin decline combined with sustained beverage volume losses would signal that neither the snack business nor the beverage business is generating sufficient profit to sustain the dividend and deleveraging simultaneously.

Latest reading: Operating profit margin 16.6% for the first half of 2026 (2026-06-13 period end), well above the 11.5% floor. North American beverage unit volume declined 4% in the twelve-week period and 3% in the twenty-four-week period. The operating margin leg of the exit trigger is not threatened; the volume leg remains active. Exit condition (1) is not triggered.

  1. The annual dividend per share must increase year-over-year in every formal dividend announcement. PepsiCo has raised its dividend every year since 1972 — 54 consecutive increases as of the most recent annual report. Management treats this streak as a statement of confidence in the business's ability to generate cash through all economic conditions. Any freeze or reduction would signal that management itself no longer believes the cash generation thesis.

Latest reading: Dividend raised 4% to $5.92 annualised per share, effective 2026-06 (announced 2026-02-03). Half-year dividend payments of $2.9025 per share compare with $2.7775 per share in the same period a year earlier. Exit condition (2) is not triggered.

  1. Net debt relative to earnings before interest, tax, depreciation, and amortisation must not exceed 3.0 times for two consecutive fiscal years without a credible management-disclosed plan to reduce it. PepsiCo borrowed heavily to acquire two wellness-food brands — poppi ($1.95 billion) and Siete Foods ($1.2 billion) — in 2025, bringing the leverage ratio to 2.48 times at fiscal year-end 2025. At 3.0 times and rising, the dividend programme itself would come under pressure and the ability to make further acquisitions would be constrained.

Latest reading: Long-term debt was essentially flat at $42.6 billion as of 2026-06-13 versus $42.3 billion at year-end 2025. The mid-year ratio cannot be precisely recalculated without a disclosed half-year earnings figure, but the directional read is stable below the 3.0 times exit threshold. Exit condition (3) is not triggered.

  1. Gross profit as a share of revenue must not fall below 52% for two consecutive quarters. Gross margin (gross profit as a share of revenue) is the earliest place where brand pricing power either holds or cracks under cost pressure. The 52% floor sits two percentage points below the bottom of the historical range (53–55%) — it is a level that would indicate commodity inflation or competitive pressure has begun to overwhelm the pricing power that the brand portfolio provides.

Latest reading: Gross margin 54.7% for the first half of 2026 and 54.2% for the twelve-week period ending 2026-06-13 — within the historical band and 2.7 percentage points above the exit floor. Exit condition (4) is not triggered.

  1. No single goodwill write-down on any of the three recent acquisitions — poppi, Siete Foods, or SodaStream — must exceed $300 million in any fiscal year through 2028. When PepsiCo wrote down the value of its Rockstar Energy investment by $549 million in FY2025, it signalled that the capital allocated to that acquisition had been partially destroyed. A similar write-down on any of the three post-2024 acquisitions — at a time when net debt is already elevated — would confirm that the acquisition programme is destroying value at the same time as it is constraining financial flexibility.

Latest reading: No impairment charge has been recorded against poppi, Siete Foods, or SodaStream through 2026-06-13. The contingent earn-out liability for poppi fell in assessed value by $161 million during the first half of 2026 (reflecting lower confidence in the brand hitting its revenue milestones by mid-2027), but no impairment charge has been taken against the acquisition's goodwill or intangible assets. Exit condition (5) is not triggered.

The Share Price

At $139, the share is essentially at fair value. The owner earnings yield — the normalised cash the business generates each year divided by the total market value — works out to 4.72%, compared with the fair-value anchor of 4.70%. The difference is a rounding matter. Translating that yield into a price, fair value sits at approximately $140.

The implication is that the current price leaves no margin of safety. A purchase at $139 buys the business at exactly what a reasonable long-run discount rate says it is worth — no cushion for things going worse than expected, no reward for the three unresolved uncertainties (North American beverage volume recovery, tariff trajectory, and acquisition integration). The buy band — the point where the yield is wide enough to offer genuine margin of safety — begins at $101. The trim band, where the market is pricing in implausibly optimistic perpetual growth, begins at $205.

A separate valuation model based on peer and historical price multiples (what other similar businesses and PepsiCo itself have historically traded at) derives a different central estimate of approximately $216–229. That figure is meaningfully above the $140 yield-derived fair value. The two approaches sit in tension with each other and are flagged in Gaps in the Picture.

The Standing Orders

Two price alerts are set:

  • At $140 (fair value): the share is at the level where buying offers no margin of safety. No action is required, but the Case for the Bet should be reviewed to confirm the thesis is intact.
  • At $101 (the cheap edge): the normalised cash yield — owner earnings divided by market value — has widened to 6.5%, a level that offers genuine margin of safety above the long-run required return. At this price, the business can be purchased at approximately its discounted-cash-flow intrinsic value.

Three standing orders follow from the band edges:

  • If the price reaches $101 or below: Confirm no exit condition has been triggered and the operating profit margin is recovering toward 13%. If confirmed, add to the position in tranches over one to two quarters. The buy threshold aligns with the intrinsic-value estimate derived from a 7.5% discount rate applied to the three-year average owner earnings.
  • If the price reaches $205 or above: Trim 25–33% of the position. At that level, the normalised cash yield has compressed to 3.2% — a point where the market is pricing in perpetual growth well above the base-case scenario. Reassess the remaining position size after trimming.
  • If the price reaches $469 or above: Exit the full position. At that level, the market has priced in approximately 33 years of base-case compounding — implying that all the value the business will create over the next three decades is already embedded in the share price today. Redeploy proceeds at the next cheap-edge crossing or into a higher-conviction position. No re-entry within 90 days, to avoid anchoring on the exit price.

What's Wobbling

One thing is wobbling, and it has been wobbling for a long time: PepsiCo's beverage unit volumes in North America. The PepsiCo Beverages North America segment — which includes Pepsi, Mountain Dew, Gatorade, and the newly acquired poppi brand — reported a 4% decline in unit sales for the twelve-week period ending 2026-06-13 and a 3% decline for the full twenty-four weeks. Both carbonated soft drinks and non-carbonated beverages fell. That extends the negative run to at least nine consecutive quarters.

The reason this wobble does not trigger the exit is structural: exit condition (1) requires both a sustained operating margin decline and sustained volume losses. The margin leg is the exact opposite of threatened — the consolidated operating margin was 16.6% for the first half of 2026, more than five percentage points above the 11.5% exit floor. Volume alone, without the margin companion, does not breach the conditions.

What makes the wobble worth watching is the question it poses to the recovery timeline. The thesis was written assuming a gradual volume recovery in North American beverages as the business cycled through post-pandemic normalisation and tariff headwinds eased. Nine quarters without an inflection is a long time. The exit condition is deliberately a two-leg compound trigger, designed to avoid false exits on a volume problem that the pricing side is absorbing — and the pricing side, for now, clearly is absorbing it. But the longer the volume decline persists, the more the bear case gains credibility. The next two quarterly reports (for the period ending around 2026-09 and 2026-12) are the ones to watch.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Beverage volume never recovers. If the PepsiCo Beverages North America volume decline reflects a permanent shift in consumer preference — toward still waters, energy drinks managed by competitors, or the consumption patterns associated with widespread adoption of GLP-1 weight-loss drugs — rather than a cyclical dip, the segment's contribution to group earnings could permanently shrink. Roughly 18% of US adults are estimated to be using GLP-1 drugs as of spring 2026, and households using them are reported to spend 31% less on snack foods. The snack business faces a related risk.
  • Tariff costs remain elevated or worsen. Aluminium packaging tariffs at 50% represent a confirmed ongoing cost headwind. While the US Supreme Court ruled in mid-2026 that certain emergency-powers tariffs were invalid — and PepsiCo has filed for refunds — the trajectory of trade policy is uncertain. A further escalation would compress margins in the beverage business, where aluminium cans are a significant input.
  • Acquisition write-downs repeat the Rockstar outcome. PepsiCo spent approximately $3.15 billion in 2025 acquiring poppi and Siete Foods. The contingent earn-out for poppi fell in assessed value by $161 million in the first half of 2026, suggesting the brand is underperforming its milestone targets. A formal impairment charge on either acquisition — at a time when net debt is already at a multi-year high — would trigger exit condition (5) and consume capital that could otherwise be returned to shareholders.
  • Leverage constrains the dividend programme. At 2.48 times net debt relative to earnings, PepsiCo is at its highest leverage point in the three-year window. If free cash flow weakens — through a combination of margin pressure and higher capital spending — the capacity to continue annual dividend growth could come under pressure before the leverage ratio hits the formal 3.0 times exit threshold.
  • Private-label acceleration takes lasting share. Private-label (supermarket own-brand) snack sales were growing at three times the pace of branded equivalents in 2025, and total US grocery private-label sales reached a record $282.8 billion. If that acceleration reflects a lasting preference shift rather than inflation-driven trade-down, the premium that Frito-Lay commands for Lay's and Doritos relative to own-brand alternatives could erode over time.

Gaps in the Picture

Two structural gaps affect the precision of the figures in this evaluation.

First, PepsiCo's capital expenditure figures are not available through the standard electronic filing data-extraction process used here. The owner earnings figure ($8.96 billion, representing a three-year average from FY2023 to FY2025) is sourced from the value analysis rather than from the automatically extracted filing data. The practical effect is that the valuation is consistent with the underlying analysis, but the figures cannot be automatically cross-checked against the raw filing data without a manual step. The override was set when the analysis was last refreshed; it should be revisited whenever the value analysis is updated.

Second, two different valuation methods produce two different fair-value estimates for PepsiCo that sit in meaningful tension with each other. The yield-based method used as the primary anchor — dividing normalised owner earnings by a target yield — gives a fair value of approximately $140. A separate model based on what comparable companies and PepsiCo's own historical trading multiples imply gives a central estimate closer to $216–229. The $76–89 gap between the two figures is not a rounding difference; it reflects genuine methodological divergence. The yield-based figure is used as the primary anchor here because it is directly observable and because it directly relates to the conditions in the Case for the Bet. Reconciling the two models — or choosing one definitively — is deferred to a future analysis update.

The Rules

Six rules govern what action follows from each combination of conditions and share price. The rules were set in advance and are applied mechanically to whatever the evidence shows.

  1. If the thesis is confirmed (all conditions holding, no exit threshold breached): hold the position. Collect the approximately 4.3% annual dividend yield. Re-evaluate at the next quarterly filing, expected around 2026-10. The two near-term confirming signals to watch are North American beverage volume turning positive and the operating profit margin recovering toward 13%.

  2. If the thesis is weakened (one or more conditions deteriorating but no formal exit threshold crossed — the current state): do not add to the position. Wait for the next two quarterly reports before drawing further conclusions. The 90-day review cadence is deliberate given that three uncertainties — tariff direction, volume recovery, and acquisition integration — are all live simultaneously.

  3. If the thesis is broken (any of the five exit conditions triggered): exit the full position within 30 days. Do not average down. Record which exit condition fired and the price at which the position was closed.

  4. If the share price falls to $101 or below (the buy band): first confirm that no exit condition has been triggered and that the operating margin is recovering. If confirmed, add to the position in tranches over one to two quarters. The $101 level aligns with the intrinsic value estimate from the discounted cash flow analysis at a 7.5% discount rate.

  5. If the share price rises to $205 or above (the trim band): trim 25–33% of the position. At that level, the market is pricing in long-run growth well beyond the base case. Reassess the remaining position after trimming.

  6. If the share price reaches $469 or above (the exit band): exit the full position. The market would at that point be pricing in approximately 33 years of base-case compounding as an immediate certainty. Redeploy proceeds at the next cheap-edge crossing or into a higher-conviction position. No re-entry for 90 days.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
