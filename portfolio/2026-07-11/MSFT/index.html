Recent Development

In the three months ending in March 2026, Microsoft's cloud business delivered another quarter of sustained momentum. Azure, the company's cloud computing platform, grew 40 per cent compared with the same period a year earlier — matching its growth rate from the three months ending in September 2025, and sitting well above the 25 per cent floor at which the thesis would begin to wobble. The commercial backlog of contracted but not-yet-delivered revenue reached $627 billion, up 99 per cent year on year, which anchors the thesis's forward visibility argument.

The main source of tension is the capital spending programme. Over the nine months ending in March 2026, the company spent $80.1 billion on property and equipment — 33 per cent of revenue for the period, and a sharp step up from the 23 per cent ratio in the full financial year ending June 2025. That level of infrastructure investment has compressed the ratio of cash generated to reported profit to 0.48 for the nine-month period, well below the 0.65 floor written into the exit conditions. The exit condition itself, however, requires this compression to persist across two consecutive full fiscal years with no improvement in artificial-intelligence revenue — a test that cannot be judged against a partial year while cloud growth is running at 40 per cent.

For the full picture of how those conditions stack up, see the Case for the Bet, and for where the share price sits against the bands, see the Share Price.

About Microsoft

Microsoft makes most of its money from two interlocking businesses. The first is cloud computing: companies and governments run their software, hold their data, and connect their employees using Microsoft's Azure network of data centres, which spans more than 70 regions worldwide. The second is productivity software: the Office suite of word-processing, spreadsheet, and presentation tools, along with Teams for workplace communication and Exchange for email, sold predominantly to businesses under multi-year subscription contracts. These two businesses reinforce each other — Azure is the infrastructure on which the productivity software runs, and the productivity software keeps enterprise customers inside the Microsoft ecosystem.

The company also sells the Xbox gaming console and associated content subscriptions, manages the LinkedIn professional network, and generates meaningful revenue from search advertising through Bing. These are real businesses but secondary to the cloud and productivity core. Return on invested capital — the measure of how much profit a business generates relative to the capital it employs — has run at 22 to 27 per cent for five consecutive financial years, well above the company's estimated cost of capital, which confirms that the core franchise is generating genuine economic profit rather than simply growing for its own sake.

The Case for the Bet

The thesis rests on one structural argument: Microsoft's enterprise identity system, marketed as Azure Active Directory and now rebranded as Entra ID, is an all-or-nothing switching mechanism. Moving away from it requires re-provisioning every application, device, and security policy across an organisation simultaneously. No enterprise migrates that infrastructure on a whim. The capital spending surge is an extension of the same logic — building the data-centre capacity that turns that switching-cost moat into a wider structural advantage as artificial-intelligence workloads increasingly run on the same platform.

The conditions that must keep holding:

  1. Azure revenue growth stays above 25 per cent. The exit condition fires if growth falls below 25 per cent for two consecutive quarters while capital spending remains above 20 per cent of revenue. The compound structure is deliberate: a modest growth slowdown while the infrastructure programme is winding down is acceptable; a growth collapse while spending stays elevated is not.

Latest reading: Azure grew 40 per cent in the three months ending March 2026 — the same rate as the three months ending September 2025. Intelligent Cloud segment revenue (which includes Azure and related products) grew 30 per cent in the quarter and 29 per cent over the nine-month period. The 25 per cent floor is not approached.

  1. The artificial-intelligence revenue line keeps growing. The thesis argues that AI infrastructure spending builds a structural advantage only if that advantage translates into contracted revenue. The monitoring trigger is AI annual recurring revenue, which reached $37 billion — up 123 per cent year on year — prior to the March 2026 quarter.

Latest reading: The quarterly report ending March 2026 does not disclose a standalone AI revenue metric. GitHub Copilot, the programming assistant, is referenced as a driver of higher costs in the cloud segment, and Microsoft 365 Copilot is cited as contributing to higher revenue per user in the productivity segment. Growth is evident from the cost and revenue commentary; the specific revenue figure is not separately disclosed in this filing.

  1. Free-cash-flow conversion stays above 0.65. The ratio measures how much of reported profit actually lands as cash available to the business (cash from operations minus capital spending, divided by net income). The exit condition requires the ratio to fall below 0.65 for two consecutive full fiscal years with no improvement in AI revenue.

Latest reading: For the nine months ending March 2026, operating cash flow was $127.5 billion and capital expenditure was $80.1 billion, giving a ratio of 0.48 for the period — well below the 0.65 threshold. The exit condition requires two full fiscal years of this, not nine months; the full year ending June 2026 will determine whether year one is a miss. Azure's 40 per cent growth means the compound trigger is not met.

  1. Operating profit margin stays above 42 per cent. A sustained fall below that floor for two consecutive quarters signals cost growth permanently outrunning revenue — a different and more fundamental problem than the current capital spending cycle.

Latest reading: Operating margin was 46.3 per cent in the quarter ending March 2026 and 47.4 per cent over the nine-month period — both more than four percentage points above the floor. Operating income grew 20 per cent in the quarter and 22 per cent over nine months.

  1. The IRS transfer-pricing dispute does not escalate. The US Internal Revenue Service is seeking $28.9 billion in additional tax, plus penalties and interest, relating to how the company priced intercompany transactions between 2004 and 2013. The exit condition fires if the dispute is referred to Tax Court or if any single-quarter tax charge exceeds $5 billion.

Latest reading: As of March 2026, the dispute remains in the IRS administrative appeals process. No Tax Court filing has been made, no charge above $5 billion has been recorded, and the company does not expect a material change within the next 12 months.

  1. No formal antitrust action seeking structural remedies. The US Federal Trade Commission opened an investigation in late 2024 and escalated it in early 2026. The exit condition fires if a formal complaint seeking a structural remedy — such as divesting Teams or unbundling cloud and artificial-intelligence services — is filed with a court.

Latest reading: As of March 2026, no formal complaint seeking structural remedies has been filed. The quarterly filing's risk factors describe ongoing regulatory scrutiny as a background condition; no new enforcement action is reported.

  1. Gross profit margin stays above 65 per cent. Gross margin measures how much revenue is left after the direct cost of delivering services and software. Falling below 65 per cent for two consecutive quarters would signal that AI infrastructure costs are eating into the core economics of the business.

Latest reading: Gross margin was 67.6 per cent in the quarter ending March 2026 and 68.2 per cent over the nine-month period. The Microsoft Cloud gross margin declined slightly (to 66 per cent in the quarter, from higher prior-year levels) due to AI infrastructure investment, but consolidated gross margin stayed comfortably above the floor.

  1. Commercial backlog keeps growing. Remaining performance obligations — the value of contracts signed but not yet recognised as revenue — are the single best visible indicator of enterprise commitment to the platform. The exit condition fires if growth falls below 30 per cent year on year or if the absolute balance shrinks quarter on quarter.

Latest reading: Commercial remaining performance obligations stood at $627 billion at March 2026, up 99 per cent year on year. The weighted average duration is approximately 2.5 years. The exit condition is not approached.

The Share Price

At $389, Microsoft's stock sits just below the trim edge of $401 — the point at which the Rules call for reducing the position. The owner-earnings yield (a measure of the earnings a business generates relative to its market value, analogous to a dividend yield but based on the full economic output of the business rather than just the portion paid as dividends) stands at 2.06 per cent against a fair-value anchor of 3.5 per cent. That implies today's price is well above the neutral reading of $229.

The gap between the trim edge ($401) and today's price ($389) is about 3 per cent — narrow enough to be closed by a single strong earnings report. The buy edge, by contrast, is $160, which would imply a market capitalisation roughly a third of today's. That distance reflects the structural premium the market assigns to a franchise with locked-in enterprise relationships and contracted backlog of $627 billion.

The exit edge is $1,605, which would imply the market has already priced in roughly 17 years of base-case compounding in a single snapshot. At that reading, the long-run expected return would fall below the risk-free rate, and the Standing Orders call for exiting the full position.

What's Wobbling

Two drivers are delivering mixed signals rather than clean confirmation.

The first is free-cash-flow conversion. For the nine months ending March 2026, the ratio of cash generated to reported profit was 0.48 — well below the 0.65 floor in the exit conditions. The compression is entirely driven by capital spending: the company spent $80.1 billion on data centres and servers in nine months, versus $47.5 billion in the same period a year earlier. The exit condition requires this to persist for two full fiscal years with no improvement in artificial-intelligence revenue. Three quarters into the current fiscal year, Azure is growing at 40 per cent, which means the compound trigger — growth collapsing at the same time as spending is elevated — is nowhere near firing. But the full-year picture will not be available until the annual report in late 2026, and a second fiscal year of sub-0.65 conversion without meaningful AI revenue improvement would bring the condition into play.

The second is capital spending as a proportion of revenue. Nine-month capital expenditure ran at 33 per cent of revenue, and in the quarter ending March 2026 alone the ratio reached 37 per cent — up sharply from the 23 per cent full-year figure for the financial year ending June 2025. The same exit condition compound trigger applies: this is only worrying if Azure growth falls below 25 per cent simultaneously, and at 40 per cent that distance is substantial. The filing itself notes that these investments "will continue to increase our operating costs and may decrease our operating margins" — which is a useful confirmation that management regards the compression as a deliberate investment cycle, not a structural cost shift.

Neither wobble changes the Bottom Line. Each is real, each is worth tracking through the next quarterly filing, and neither crosses the line the conditions set.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • AI spending fails to generate returns. If Azure growth decelerates toward mid-teen rates while capital spending stays elevated — the bear case — the free-cash-flow compression stops being a cycle and starts being permanent. The three-year bear-case return is −3.3 per cent a year, implying the share price would be materially lower than today after accounting for the lost time value.
  • IRS dispute escalates. The $28.9 billion tax demand, plus penalties and interest, remains in the administrative appeals process. A Tax Court referral, or a settlement at the upper end of the range, would absorb a significant portion of one year's free cash flow and potentially affect the capital allocation available for buybacks and dividends.
  • Antitrust action forces structural change. A formal FTC complaint seeking to separate Teams from the broader Microsoft 365 bundle, or to unbundle cloud services from artificial-intelligence tooling, would require product redesign and could reduce the pricing power embedded in the current seat-based subscription model.
  • Cloud gross margins erode further. The Microsoft Cloud gross margin declined to 66 per cent in the March 2026 quarter as AI infrastructure costs rose. If that margin continues falling and breaches 65 per cent of consolidated revenue for two consecutive quarters, the exit condition fires — signalling that the cost of building AI capability is permanently undermining the economics of the cloud franchise.
  • Commercial backlog converts more slowly than expected. The $627 billion contracted backlog assumes enterprise customers take delivery at the pace implied by the current weighted-average duration of 2.5 years. A broader macroeconomic contraction could lead to renegotiation or extension of contracts, reducing the near-term cash conversion of that backlog without triggering the year-on-year growth exit condition.

The divergence reflects the AI-driven capital spending cycle compressing free cash flow; the fair-value anchor was set using normalised earnings before the CapEx inflection. A fresh calibration is deferred to the next full-year analysis.

The fair-value bands model produces a base-scenario midpoint of $2,095, which implies an owner-earnings yield of 0.38 per cent — diverging from the 3.5 per cent fair-value anchor by 89 per cent. This reflects a different modelling methodology rather than a computational error; the two figures are not directly comparable without reconciling the growth and discount-rate assumptions.

The filing itself does not disclose a standalone AI revenue metric, so the most recent quarterly read for this driver is absent from the filing evidence.

The risk-free rate used in the score calculation is read from the broad-market equity index fund's market-data snapshot rather than from Microsoft's own market-data file, as the field is not populated in company-holding snapshots. The rate used is 4.88 per cent.

The Rules

The following conditions determine what action follows from each combination of thesis state and share-price position. The rules were set before this evaluation and are not modified by it.

  • If the thesis is intact — all conditions holding, no exit trigger fired — no action is taken. The position is held and the conditions are monitored at each quarterly earnings release. No additional buying at the current share price: the margin of safety is insufficient for a new addition.
  • If any single driver deteriorates without crossing an exit threshold — a wobble rather than a break — no addition is made. The specific weakening driver is monitored for one additional quarter. If a second consecutive quarter confirms the deterioration, the position is escalated to a thesis-broken review.
  • If any written exit condition fires — one of the seven listed in the Case for the Bet — the full position is closed within 30 days. No averaging down. The triggered condition is logged for future analysis.
  • If the share price falls to or below $160 (the buy edge) while the thesis is intact, the rules call for adding in tranches: a first addition below $352 (40 per cent margin of safety against the base-case fair value), a second addition below $322 (40 per cent margin of safety against the intrinsic-value estimate). Each tranche is 25 to 33 per cent of the intended addition.
  • If the share price rises to or above $401 (the trim edge), the rules call for trimming 20 to 25 per cent of the position. If Azure growth continues confirming the base case after the trim, the remaining position is retained. No trim below 50 per cent of the current holding unless an exit condition also fires.
  • If the share price rises to or above $1,605 (the exit edge), the full position is closed. At that price, the market is pricing in roughly 17 years of base-case compounding; the implied forward return falls below half the risk-free rate, and there is no margin of safety. No re-entry within 90 days.
