Booking: The Bet Still Works

The Bottom Line

The bet on Booking Holdings — that Booking.com remains the last-standing global online travel marketplace through whatever disruption comes next — is still working as the case requires. Every measurable condition is either met or comfortably beating its line. The share price near $170 sits inside the acceptable range but unusually close to the level that would mark the shares as clearly cheap. The one open question to watch is whether management can show concrete evidence of an artificial-intelligence strategy that competes when travel-planning moves to chat-based agents — a deadline still more than five quarters out.

The Verdict

The latest reading puts Booking firmly in "still working" territory. That translates differently depending on the price paid for the shares.

  • As a fresh investment at today's price (around $170 per share). Wait. The current owner-earnings yield sits just inside the acceptable range; the rules call for setting a price alert near $172 and adding only on confirmation that the shares have crossed into the clearly-cheap band. The case is good; the discipline is to enter at a margin.
  • As an existing investment bought below $150 per share — currently sitting on a gain of 13 percent or more. Hold.   • As an existing investment bought roughly between $150 and $175 per share — currently anywhere between a small loss of about 3 percent and a small gain of about 13 percent. Hold. Adding shares is one tick of share-price movement away; the right discipline is to wait for either the cheap line to be crossed or the next quarterly filing to remove the Middle East conflict noise.
  • As an existing investment bought between $175 and $200 per share — currently showing a loss of roughly 3 to 15 percent. Hold. The case is still intact and the capital-return engine is accelerating; capitulating at a mild loss while the central network-effect bet is working would mistake a soft quarter for a broken case.

The two-sided marketplace is still compounding and the share count is shrinking through aggressive buybacks; the math still works.

About Booking

Booking Holdings runs the world's largest online travel marketplace. The flagship name is Booking.com, which lists about 4.4 million properties — most of them traditional hotels but, increasingly, alternative accommodations such as apartments, holiday homes, and unique places to stay. The portfolio also includes Priceline, Agoda (focused on Asia), Kayak (a metasearch site), and OpenTable (restaurant reservations).

The business model is a two-sided network. Property owners list their inventory because that is where the travellers are; travellers book on Booking.com because that is where the inventory is. Each side reinforces the other. The company takes a fee — its "take rate" — on the gross value of every booking that flows through the platform; in 2025 that rolled up to about $24 billion of revenue against $166 billion of gross bookings.

The cash these businesses throw off — roughly $9 billion of free cash flow in 2025 — is returned to shareholders almost in full. The company has run an aggressive share-buyback programme for years and now pays a quarterly dividend as well. The share count has been falling steadily; per-share value can keep compounding even when total business growth slows.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that Booking is the most likely company to be last standing among global online travel marketplaces when the dust settles on the artificial-intelligence disruption, Google's push into direct travel search, and hotel chains' attempts to drive direct bookings. The two-sided network is durable enough to absorb all of these pressures; disciplined capital return shrinks the share count and compounds per-share value even if total growth slows.

For the bet to keep working, the following conditions must keep holding:

  1. Room-night growth stays positive across regions. Year-on-year growth in nights booked should not slip into low single digits or negative territory in multiple regions for two or more consecutive quarters, ignoring isolated shocks (an earthquake, a regional conflict, a one-off cancellation wave).
  2. Marketing does not keep eating a bigger share of bookings. Marketing spending as a percentage of gross bookings should not rise meaningfully over two or more consecutive years. A creeping rise would mean Booking is buying the growth that its network used to produce for free.
  3. The take rate does not compress. Revenue as a percentage of gross bookings should not be cut down materially or persistently by competitive, regulatory, or chat-agent pressure.
  4. Capital return stays disciplined. Buybacks should keep running; no large value-destructive acquisition; no culture-shifting strategic pivot away from the return-of-cash model.
  5. An artificial-intelligence strategy emerges by late 2027. By the third quarter of 2027, management must have articulated and visibly shipped concrete products that show measurable engagement, direct traffic, or margin uplift — and must have acknowledged the agent-disintermediation risk openly. The current walled-garden approach (Connected Trip, Smart Filters, the Penny chatbot on Priceline) is a placeholder, not a strategy.
  6. No European structural remedy. Enforcement under the European Union's Digital Markets Act or Digital Services Act should not produce a formal decision forcing structural product changes (such as mandatory removal of price-parity clauses), and should not impose fines exceeding 3 percent of annual global revenue in a single year.

The Rules

The rules that govern what action follows from each combination of conditions and share price are fixed:

  • If all conditions are intact. Hold. No action. Let the compounding continue.
  • If any one condition is wobbling but none has clearly broken. Do not add to the position. Watch the deteriorating condition through the next quarterly filing — then a second — before reconsidering.
  • If any one condition has clearly broken. Sell the full position within thirty days. No averaging down.
  • If the share price has dropped into the bargain range and the conditions are still intact. Add to the position in stages over one or two quarters.
  • If the share price has run into the expensive range. Trim 25 to 33 percent of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding, then reconsider sizing.

The Conditions Today

The most recent quarterly filing — covering the three months ending in March 2026 — reads condition by condition as follows.

Room nights. Global room-night growth was 6 percent year on year for the quarter. March alone slowed to 1 percent because of the recent Middle East conflict, which raised cancellations and dampened bookings on travel corridors between Europe, Asia, and the region. Stripping out the conflict, the company estimates underlying growth at about 8 percent. The filing names the conflict explicitly as an isolated shock — which is precisely the carve-out the first condition allows.

Marketing efficiency. Marketing spending was 3.8 percent of gross bookings in the latest quarter, identical to the same quarter a year earlier. The slight uptick management mentions in commentary is conflict-driven (paid-channel bookings cancelled after the fact). Stripping out the conflict, the company says marketing actually levered slightly, helped by growth in the direct channel.

Take rate. Revenue as a percentage of gross bookings rose year on year, driven by higher revenue from facilitating payments and by favourable timing of bookings versus travel. The condition guards against a falling take rate; the figures show the opposite.

Capital return. Share buybacks ran at $3.8 billion in the quarter, against $2.2 billion in the same quarter a year earlier — a 73 percent year-on-year acceleration. The quarterly dividend was raised to $0.42 per share, up about 11 percent. The buyback authorisation has $18.2 billion remaining out of the $20 billion approved in 2025. No large or value-destructive acquisition was disclosed.

Artificial-intelligence strategy. Management articulates the Connected Trip vision — an "ideal artificial-intelligence-powered traveller experience" with personalised planning, booking, payment, and in-trip help. Mobile-app usage advanced from a mid-fifties percentage of room nights to a high-fifties percentage. What is still missing: a measurable agent-traffic figure, a direct-engagement uplift number, or a margin uplift attributable to the strategy. The deadline for that evidence sits in late 2027 — more than five quarters away. The clock is running but not close to ringing.

European regulation. Booking.com remains designated as a gatekeeper under the Digital Markets Act and as a very large platform under the Digital Services Act. No formal decision imposing structural remedies or material fines has been handed down. The condition is not approached.

The Share Price

At $170 the share price implies that, for every dollar paid for Booking stock, the business currently generates about 6.9 cents of free cash flow each year. The rules anchor a fair price at 5.5 cents per dollar — roughly $213 per share — and call the shares clearly cheap when the figure reaches 7.0 cents per dollar (a price around $168) or clearly expensive when it falls to 4.0 cents (a price near $293).

The 6.9 cents reading sits inside the acceptable range but unusually close to the cheap edge — a one-percent drop in the share price would cross the line into clearly-cheap territory. Put plainly: today's price is leaning attractive, not yet bargain. The share price is also well below the fair-value anchor of $213, which is the more interesting fact for a fresh purchase.

What's Wobbling

Two open questions cloud an otherwise clean reading.

The first is the near-term drag from the Middle East conflict. The company recognises booking revenue when travellers actually check in, not at the moment of booking, which means the cancellations that hit bookings in March will land in revenue figures over the second and third quarters of 2026. The drag is real, but the carve-out logic of the first condition treats this kind of regional shock as transient. Watching whether the softness stays confined or spreads into other corridors is the next quarter's task.

The second is the artificial-intelligence question. The strategy is articulated but not yet measurable. The deadline — concrete shipped product with measurable evidence by late 2027 — is the most important medium-term watch item on the file. It is not a near-term decision, but it is the condition most likely to be the one that breaks if anything does.

Neither pressure crosses a written line this quarter. The case is still squarely in the "working" state.

What Could Still Go Wrong

The case for Booking could still break in a small number of structurally distinct ways:

  • Chat-based travel agents take the front door. If artificial-intelligence assistants — operated by Google, Microsoft, OpenAI, or others — become the place where travellers actually plan trips, Booking.com risks being demoted to a back-end inventory provider with weaker pricing power. The mitigation is the company's own agent strategy; whether that mitigation is credible is the open question.
  • Google's direct-search push pulls traffic away. Google has been steering travel queries into its own booking interface, bypassing Booking.com's organic-search funnel. A multi-quarter acceleration would force marketing spending up — the second condition is the trip-wire.
  • A European structural remedy. A Digital Markets Act ruling forcing the removal of price-parity arrangements with hotels, or a Digital Services Act fine exceeding 3 percent of global revenue in a single year, would rerate the economics of the platform.
  • A travel recession. A consumer-spending downturn, prolonged conflict, or oil-price shock would compress room-night growth across regions simultaneously. The business has weathered this before, but a multi-quarter, multi-region weakness would start the clock on the first condition.
  • Capital-allocation drift. A large acquisition — a chain, a rival, a hospitality brand — would slow the buyback machinery and shift the case from "compounding per-share value through shrinkage" to "buying growth at uncertain returns."

The worst plausible drawdown — combining a soft-travel quarter with a credibility wobble on the artificial-intelligence strategy — could plausibly be a 30 to 40 percent fall in the share price over twelve to eighteen months. Permanent capital impairment is unlikely given the cash flows the business currently throws off and the strength of the property-listing network; a multi-year period of holding underwater capital while the case proves itself is the more realistic downside.

Gaps in the Picture

A few things in the underlying figures are worth flagging plainly.

The "free cash flow" used to anchor cheap and expensive treats every dollar of capital spending as it appears on the strict accounting line. For Booking that line is small ($322 million in 2025) because the business is asset-light, so the gap to any other definition is modest. There is no equivalent of the "headline-versus-strict capital spending" issue that distorts asset-heavy companies' readings.

The Middle East conflict is named as an isolated shock in this single quarterly filing. Whether the carve-out logic holds depends on the next two filings: a clean rebound confirms the shock interpretation; a second weak quarter starts the formal two-quarter clock on the first condition.

The artificial-intelligence strategy is the file's biggest open watch item, but its deadline is more than five quarters out. The verdict in the meantime is "monitor, do not act" — but a chat-based agent breakthrough by a rival, announced between filings, would compress that timeline materially.

The third-party take-rate is currently expanding. The narrative around it is straightforward (more facilitated payments, favourable booking-versus-travel timing); a future quarter could just as easily see the dynamic reverse — a flight-mix headwind, for instance, has lower take rates than hotel bookings. The reading is good today, not guaranteed tomorrow.
