Microsoft: The Bet Still Works

The Bottom Line

Data as of 2026-07-19 · Latest filing 2026-04-29.

The bet is working: Azure cloud revenue has grown at 40% annually through the nine months ending in March 2026, the enterprise contract backlog has nearly doubled to $627 billion, and operating margins are holding above 46% — all of which confirms that Microsoft's switching-cost moat is intact. The Share Price sits at the trim edge, well above fair value. The long-run total return at today's price is about 14% a year — a healthy equity premium. The one condition to watch is cash-flow conversion: infrastructure spending has compressed the ratio of free cash flow to net income well below the threshold written into the thesis, and whether that compression peaks or persists will determine whether the capital cycle was a moat-builder or a permanent earnings drain.

Recent Development

The three months ending in March 2026 confirmed that the AI infrastructure build is already generating revenue, not merely cost. Azure cloud services grew 40% for both the quarter and the nine-month period — unchanged from the rate disclosed in the prior filing and well above the 25% floor that would trigger the first exit condition. The commercial backlog reached $627 billion, up 99% year-on-year, meaning enterprises are extending and deepening commitments rather than reducing them. Operating margins of 46.3% for the quarter and 47.4% for the nine-month period are more than four percentage points above the 42% exit floor. All seven exit conditions remain untripped at this reading.

The one area that genuinely wobbles is cash-flow conversion. Capital spending in the nine months to March 2026 reached $80 billion — roughly 33% of revenue for that period and 37% in the quarter alone, against 23% for all of 2025. The ratio of free cash flow to net income for the nine months was 0.48, well below the 0.65 floor set in exit condition two. That condition requires the shortfall to persist across two consecutive full fiscal years with no improvement in Azure AI revenue contribution; the nine-month period represents only three quarters and Azure is still compounding at 40%, so the compound trigger is not yet met. But the trajectory bears watching through the full 2026 fiscal year, particularly as the uncommenced lease pipeline of $197 billion signals that capital intensity will remain elevated for several years. For more on the valuation implications, see The Share Price.

About Microsoft

Microsoft sells software, cloud infrastructure, and productivity tools to enterprises and consumers through a subscription-and-consumption model. The three operating divisions are Productivity and Business Processes (which includes Microsoft 365, LinkedIn, and Dynamics business-process software), Intelligent Cloud (Azure cloud computing and server products), and More Personal Computing (Windows, gaming via Xbox, and search advertising through Bing). Revenue in the twelve months ending in March 2026 was roughly $318 billion, earned from recurring subscriptions, consumption-based cloud charges, and a smaller slice of hardware and gaming content.

The business model creates its own gravity. Microsoft 365 is the backbone of enterprise communication and collaboration for over a billion users. The identity and access layer — the system that authenticates every user, device, and application in an organisation — is built into almost every installation. Replacing it requires swapping out email, file storage, security, and process software simultaneously, a project that most companies will not attempt unless forced. This makes customers sticky by design rather than by habit, and the stickiness deepens with each additional application or AI workload connected to the same platform.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that Microsoft's switching-cost moat is durable enough to sustain returns on invested capital well above the cost of capital for a decade or more, and that the current AI infrastructure spending cycle is building a wider structural moat rather than permanently compressing earnings. Specifically, three conditions must keep holding:

  1. Azure cloud revenue grows at or above 25% per year. Azure is the central monetisation engine for the AI spending cycle. If growth falls below 25% for two consecutive quarters while capital expenditure remains above 20% of revenue, that combination signals the infrastructure bet is failing to pay off — the first and most important exit condition.

Latest reading: Azure and other cloud services grew 40% in the three months ending in March 2026 and 40% for the nine-month period (2026-04-29). Intelligent Cloud segment revenue grew 30% for the quarter and 29% for the nine-month period. The 25% floor is not approached.

  1. Consolidated operating margin stays above 42%. Operating margin measures whether software revenue is scaling faster than the cost of running the platform. A sustained fall below 42% for two consecutive quarters would signal structural cost inflation beyond what the AI investment cycle can absorb.

Latest reading: Operating margin was 46.3% for the quarter ending in March 2026 and 47.4% for the nine-month period (2026-04-29). Both are more than four percentage points above the exit floor. Operating income grew 20% for the quarter and 22% year-on-year for the nine months.

  1. The cash-earnings ratio stays above 0.65 on an annual basis. When a company's cash profits fall well below its accounting profits, it usually means capital is being consumed faster than earnings are growing. The ratio floor of 0.65 — free cash flow divided by net income — allows for some capital-expenditure-driven compression; the exit condition requires the shortfall to persist for two consecutive full fiscal years with no accompanying improvement in Azure AI revenue.

Latest reading: The nine-month cash-earnings ratio was 0.48 (2026-04-29), materially below the 0.65 threshold. This is driven by $80 billion in capital spending across the nine months. The exit condition requires fiscal-year persistence across two consecutive years; with Azure growing at 40%, the compound trigger is not currently met. The reading is below the floor but not yet triggering the exit condition.

  1. The tax dispute with United States authorities stays out of court. Microsoft has a $28.9 billion assessment pending from a dispute covering the fiscal years 2004 to 2013. The exit condition fires if the dispute is formally escalated to Tax Court or if a single quarter produces a tax charge exceeding $5 billion.

Latest reading: As of March 2026, Microsoft is contesting the assessment through the Internal Revenue Service's administrative appeals process and does not anticipate a significant change to its tax contingencies within twelve months (2026-04-29). No Tax Court filing. No single-quarter charge above $5 billion. Exit condition not triggered.

  1. No formal antitrust complaint seeking structural remedies. The exit condition fires if a regulator files a formal complaint seeking structural remedies such as the forced divestiture of Teams or the unbundling of Azure.

Latest reading: No formal antitrust complaint or structural remedy order is disclosed in the latest filing (2026-04-29). Regulatory scrutiny is noted in risk factors but no enforcement action has been filed. Exit condition not triggered.

  1. Consolidated gross margin stays above 65%. Gross margin — the share of every sales dollar left after the direct cost of delivering the product — falling below 65% for two consecutive quarters would signal that AI infrastructure costs are being absorbed rather than passed through to customers, compressing the core software moat.

Latest reading: Gross margin was 67.6% for the quarter and 68.2% for the nine months ending in March 2026 (2026-04-29). The Microsoft Cloud gross margin decreased to 66% for the quarter due to AI infrastructure investment, but consolidated gross margin remains well above the 65% floor. Exit condition not triggered.

  1. Commercial backlog grows above 30% year-on-year and does not decline quarter-on-quarter. The remaining performance obligation — the contracted revenue not yet recognised — is the best leading indicator of whether enterprise customers are extending or reducing their commitments. A growth rate below 30% or an absolute quarterly decline would signal weakening enterprise conviction in the platform.

Latest reading: The commercial remaining performance obligation reached $627 billion at 2026-03-31, up 99% year-on-year, with a weighted average duration of approximately 2.5 years (2026-04-29). Exit condition not approached.

The Share Price

The current share price is $392. Under the earnings-yield framework — which measures what the business actually earns in cash relative to what the market charges for it — Microsoft's yield is approximately 2.05%. The framework's fair-value anchor sits at a 3.5% yield, which corresponds to a share price of $229. The trim edge sits at a 2.0% yield, corresponding to $401.

In plain terms: at $392, the share price sits nine dollars below the trim line. The market is pricing in substantially more than the fair-value anchor implies. The gap between the current price ($392) and the written fair-value price ($229) is 71%, which puts the share price well above fair value by the measure written into the thesis at entry.

Two cautions about these numbers are worth stating plainly. First, the thesis was written with a fair-value yield of 3.5%, but Microsoft's earnings yield as of the latest filing is 2.05% — a gap of over 40% from the anchor in the thesis. The written anchor may not reflect the current investment case accurately; the Fair-Value-Bands analysis, which uses a wider set of market comparisons, places a base-case fair value closer to $561, which would imply today's price is actually modest rather than extreme. Second, the exit edge at a 0.75% earnings yield corresponds to a price of $1,606 — a level that implies roughly 17 years of base-case compounding priced in immediately, well above any reasonable near-term expectation.

The practical read is that the share price is in the upper part of the hold band as the thesis defines it. At $392, no action is triggered unless the trim edge is crossed (currently at $401) or an exit condition fires.

What's Wobbling

Two drivers are reading below their thresholds rather than confirming.

The more significant is cash-flow conversion. The ratio of free cash flow to net income for the nine months to March 2026 was 0.48 — materially below the 0.65 floor in exit condition two. This is driven by $80 billion in capital spending for the nine-month period, up from $47 billion in the prior year's comparable period. The thesis explicitly anticipated this compression and characterised it as a capital-allocation choice rather than an earnings-quality problem; the exit condition was deliberately structured as a compound trigger requiring fiscal-year persistence alongside no Azure AI improvement. With Azure growing at 40%, the compound trigger is not met. But the full 2026 fiscal year's data will be the real test, and the uncommenced lease pipeline of $197 billion signals that capital intensity remains elevated well into 2027 and beyond.

The second is capital expenditure as a percentage of revenue. At 33–37% of revenue in the first three quarters of 2026, the ratio has risen sharply from 23% in the full 2025 fiscal year. Exit condition one — which requires this elevated spending combined with Azure growth falling below 25% — is not triggered at Azure's 40% growth rate. The two conditions form an interlocking pair that can only be broken simultaneously: as long as Azure sustains above 25%, elevated capital expenditure alone cannot fire the exit.

Two drift warnings are active in the underlying analysis. The fair-value yield written into the thesis (3.5%, implying $229 per share) diverges from both the current earnings yield (2.05%) and the Fair-Value-Bands base-scenario estimate by amounts that exceed the drift-warning threshold. These warnings do not change the hold instruction but signal that the trim-edge trigger at $401 may be closer to the current price than the thesis originally contemplated. A reviewer should assess whether the fair-value anchor needs updating before the next trim-edge crossing. See Gaps in the Picture for more on this.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Azure decelerates below 25% while capital spending stays elevated. This is exit condition one — the scenario where AI infrastructure spending fails to pay off. The bear case assumes Azure drops to mid-teens growth; at that level, the investment case breaks. The AI workloads most exposed are commodity inference workloads at the edge of the Azure estate, where open-weight model competition is sharpest.
  • The tax dispute escalates to a court proceeding or produces a large single-quarter charge. A settlement at the full assessed amount of $28.9 billion plus penalties would represent a roughly 10% hit to market capitalisation at current levels. The bear case assumes a $20 billion settlement; the upper bound remains open.
  • Free-cash-flow compression proves permanent rather than cyclical. If the $197 billion uncommenced lease pipeline translates into sustained capital spending above 30% of revenue through 2027 and 2028, with Azure growth moderating as the market matures, the thesis that capital spending peaks and then leverages breaks. The exit condition captures this scenario, but detection requires two full fiscal years of evidence.
  • Regulatory remedies structurally separate the product portfolio. Teams unbundling under European Digital Markets Act pressure, or Azure cloud-interoperability requirements under a formal antitrust action, would reduce the portfolio lock-in that is the thesis's core mechanism. The probability is assessed as medium but the magnitude of impairment would be high.
  • OpenAI partnership losses scale further. Microsoft took a $4.7 billion equity-method charge in 2025 on its OpenAI partnership. If OpenAI's losses scale with its capital requirements, the drag on Microsoft's net income could become material before the revenue from that partnership offsets it.

Gaps in the Picture

The earnings calculation uses operating cash flow minus total capital expenditure, net of stock-based compensation. Maintenance capital expenditure is not separated from growth capital expenditure in the filing's disclosures — both are captured together. This means the earnings figure overstates normalised earnings power during periods of heavy growth investment, as the current period is. The thesis notes this explicitly, but any valuation built on the current earnings figure carries an embedded assumption that growth capital spending will eventually peak.

The fair-value anchor in the thesis (3.5% earnings yield, implying $229 per share) diverges from both the current earnings yield (2.05%) and the Fair-Value-Bands base-scenario estimate (which implies a price closer to $561). This divergence exceeds 40% on the yield measure and 59% on the band-model measure. The threshold for a drift warning is 30%; both exceed it. The standing orders' trim edge at $401 is derived from the written 2.0% trim threshold — if the fair-value anchor were updated to reflect the Fair-Value-Bands base scenario, the trim edge would shift materially upward. A reviewer should assess whether the thesis needs updating before the next trim signal is acted on.

The Rules

If the thesis remains intact — Azure growth sustained, operating margins above 42%, no exit condition triggered — the rules call for holding without adding. No new capital is deployed at the current price, which offers insufficient margin of safety relative to the base-case intrinsic value.

If the thesis weakens — a driver deteriorates but no exit condition is formally triggered — the rules call for not adding to the position. The specific weakening driver should be re-validated against the next filing. If a second consecutive quarter confirms the signal, the review escalates to a thesis-broken assessment.

If the thesis breaks — any one of the seven exit conditions fires — the rules call for exiting the full position within 30 days, with no averaging down. The triggered exit condition is logged for future learning.

If the share price falls below the buy band — the earnings yield crosses above 5.0%, corresponding to approximately $161 per share at the current earnings base — the rules call for confirming the thesis is intact first, then adding in tranches: a first tranche below $352 and a second below $322, each representing 25–33% of the intended add-on allocation.

If the share price crosses the trim edge — the earnings yield falls below 2.0%, corresponding to approximately $401 per share — the rules call for trimming 20–25% of the position to harvest the premium, retaining the core holding if Azure growth continues confirming the base case, and not trimming below 50% of the current holding unless a thesis exit condition is also triggered.

If the share price crosses the exit edge — the earnings yield falls below 1.5%, corresponding to approximately $1,606 per share — the rules call for closing the full position. At that level the implied forward return falls below half the risk-free rate. Proceeds are redeployed at the next cheap-edge crossing or into a cheaper alternative, with no re-entry for 90 days.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
