About Microsoft

Microsoft makes its money by selling software, cloud infrastructure, and productivity tools to enterprises, governments, and consumers around the world. The vast majority of that revenue arrives as subscriptions: companies and governments pay recurring fees for access to email and calendaring (through the Microsoft 365 suite, which encompasses Outlook, Teams, Word, Excel, and much more), cloud-computing capacity and services (through Azure), business applications covering sales, finance, and supply-chain management (through Dynamics), and developer tooling (through GitHub). A smaller slice comes from device sales (Surface laptops and tablets), the Xbox gaming platform, and LinkedIn, the professional-networking service with over a billion members.

The economic engine runs on switching costs rather than on the kind of brand loyalty that sells fizzy drinks. The hinge point is Azure Active Directory — now rebranded as Microsoft Entra ID — the identity and access-management system that sits at the centre of nearly every enterprise Microsoft customer's technology estate. Every application, every device, every email account, every conditional-access security policy in the organisation is registered against it. Migrating away means replacing all of those things simultaneously: email, cloud storage, security, financial software, and collaboration tools in one giant coordinated move. No IT department will take that on without a compelling alternative that does not yet exist. The result is near-permanent customer retention, confirmed by the $627 billion in contracted future revenue that enterprise customers have voluntarily extended and deepened as of the most recent quarter.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that Microsoft's enterprise identity lock-in and expanding AI-infrastructure moat will sustain returns on invested capital well above the cost of capital for at least a decade, and that the current surge in AI infrastructure spending will prove to have been a capital-allocation choice — a temporary drag on free cash flow — rather than a permanent compression of earnings power. Three conditions must keep holding for the bet to work:

  1. Azure keeps growing fast enough to justify the infrastructure spend. The exit trigger is Azure quarterly revenue growth falling below 25% for two consecutive quarters while capital expenditure simultaneously exceeds 20% of revenue — a compound signal that the AI infrastructure bet is failing to produce revenue. The floor is not arbitrary: at 25% growth, the business is still expanding the consumption base; below that level with costs still rising, the economics begin to turn.

Latest reading: Azure grew 40% year-on-year in the quarter ending in March 2026, matching the rate from the quarter ending in September 2025. Intelligent Cloud segment revenue grew 30% for the quarter and 29% for the nine-month period. The 25% floor has not been approached. Capital expenditure is elevated at 33–37% of revenue, but the compound trigger requires Azure growth to drop first — which at 40% it has not.

  1. Free cash flow remains a real and recoverable percentage of net income. The exit condition fires if the ratio of free cash flow to net income falls below 0.65 for two consecutive full fiscal years with no improvement in Azure's AI revenue contribution. The threshold distinguishes a deliberate capital-allocation cycle — where management is choosing to invest at the cost of near-term cash conversion — from a structural impairment where spending is rising without commensurate revenue.

Latest reading: The nine-month ratio was 0.483 (operating cash flow of $127.5 billion minus capital expenditure of $80.1 billion, divided by net income of $98 billion). This is below the 0.65 threshold, but the exit condition requires the reading to hold across two consecutive full fiscal years — and the most recent full fiscal year (ending June 2025) recorded a ratio of 0.70, above the threshold. The nine-month period covers only three quarters. With Azure growing at 40%, the compound trigger is not met.

  1. Operating and gross margins hold above their respective floors. The exit conditions on operating margin (below 42% for two consecutive quarters) and gross margin (below 65% for two consecutive quarters) serve as early-warning systems for pricing-power deterioration — the first sign that AI infrastructure cost is passing through to customers or that competitive pressure is compressing the software margin.

Latest reading: Operating margin for the quarter ending in March 2026 was 46.3%; for the nine months it was 47.4%. Gross margin for the quarter was 67.6%; for the nine months it was 68.2%. Both are well above the respective exit floors. Operating income grew 20% in the quarter and 22% for the nine-month period. The floors are not threatened.

The Share Price

At $389 per share as of 2026-07-07, the share price sits at the trim edge of the valuation framework — within about 3% of the level at which the Standing Orders call for a partial trim. Put in the framework's own terms, an earnings yield computed on normalised net income (what is sometimes called an owner earnings yield — a measure of what the business earns for every dollar of market value) stands at 2.06%, just above the 2.0% trim threshold. The fair-value anchor for this framework is an earnings yield of 3.5%, which corresponds to a price of roughly $229 per share. The stock is trading at nearly 70% above that anchor.

This does not mean the business is overpriced in an absolute sense. That earnings yield framework, anchored to today's earnings, is deliberately conservative — it does not credit the business for future growth. At a 12% annual growth rate in normalised earnings over the next decade, which is the base-case assumption, the yield earned on today's entry price climbs each year as earnings expand. At $389 today, assuming the base case materialises, that translates to approximately 14% a year over many years — a return that reflects both the current 2% yield and the expected 12% annual compounding. That is a reasonable return for a wide-moat compounder but not a bargain. The stock is fairly priced for the base case with little room for multiple expansion.

What's Wobbling

Two of the nine monitored conditions sit on a mixed reading.

Free-cash-flow conversion is the more visible wobble. Capital expenditure for the nine months ending in March 2026 reached $80.1 billion — 33% of revenue. In the third quarter alone it was 37% of revenue. This is the AI infrastructure build in full expression: Microsoft is spending at a rate that exceeds its current free cash flow generation by a wide margin. The ratio of free cash flow to net income for the nine months was 0.483, below the 0.65 threshold. The exit trigger requires this to persist for two consecutive full fiscal years with no improvement in Azure's AI contribution. Azure is growing at 40% and AI annual recurring revenue reached $37 billion, up 123% year-on-year. The wobble is real; the trigger is not.

AI capital expenditure efficiency sits on a mixed reading for the same structural reason. The investment is elevated and the exit compound trigger — CapEx above 20% of revenue while Azure growth falls below 25% simultaneously — is not active while Azure grows at 40%. The concern is not that the spending is irrational but that the timeline for CapEx leverage (declining capital expenditure as a share of revenue as infrastructure fills) is longer than originally modelled. Management has guided that CapEx will continue to increase. If Azure growth does not sustain 25%+ into the next fiscal year, the compound trigger begins to approach.

Neither wobble changes the call. Both are contingent on the Azure growth rate deteriorating — which has not happened.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • The AI infrastructure bet fails to monetise. Azure growth decelerates to mid-teens as open-weight AI models — available for free from competitors — commoditise the inference workloads that drive Azure AI consumption revenue. Capital expenditure at 33% of revenue with no revenue growth to cover it permanently compresses free cash flow margins. The bear case fair value is roughly $342 per share, about 12% below the current price.

  • The IRS transfer-pricing dispute escalates. The $28.9 billion assessment plus interest and penalties, covering the years 2004 to 2013, remains in administrative appeals. If the dispute is escalated to Tax Court or a single-quarter tax charge exceeds $5 billion, the exit condition triggers a mandatory review. A full adverse settlement at the upper bound would absorb approximately one year's worth of free cash flow.

  • Antitrust action produces a structural remedy. The Federal Trade Commission's investigation, escalated in early 2026 by the Trump-appointed chair, spans cloud bundling, Teams integration, AI market structure, and Microsoft's relationship with OpenAI. A formal complaint seeking a structural remedy — such as a requirement to unbundle Teams from Microsoft 365, or to give competitors interoperability access to Azure Active Directory — would weaken the switching-cost moat that is the primary reason for holding.

  • Operating margin deteriorates below 42%. If AI infrastructure costs pass through to customers in the form of higher pricing and demand weakens, or if competitive pressure forces Microsoft to discount its cloud services, operating margin could compress toward the exit threshold. The current 46–47% level provides meaningful headroom, but the filing explicitly warns that AI infrastructure investments "will continue to increase operating costs."

  • Commercial contract backlog growth stalls. The $627 billion in remaining performance obligations represents confirmed future revenue. If growth in that backlog falls below 30% year-on-year or the absolute level declines quarter-on-quarter, enterprise commitment is weakening — a leading indicator that the renewal dynamic that drives the moat is deteriorating.

The Rules

The rules below describe what the pre-committed action plan calls for in each state of the evaluation. They read as states of the assessment, not as instructions issued here.

  • If the thesis is confirmed (as it is today): hold — no action. Azure growth sustained, AI annual recurring revenue compounding, moat intact. Continue monitoring at each quarterly earnings release. No additional position sizing at the current price — the margin of safety is insufficient for a new addition. The hold is a compounding bet, not a distressed recovery.

  • If the thesis weakens: do not add to the position. Run the re-validation against the specific deteriorating driver — Azure growth, free cash flow to net income conversion, operating margin, or commercial contract backlog. Monitor for one additional quarter before reassessing position size. Determine whether the weakening is cyclical (capital expenditure timing) or structural (competitive displacement). Escalate to a thesis-broken review if a second consecutive quarter confirms the signal.

  • If the thesis breaks: exit the full position within 30 days. No averaging down. Log the triggered exit condition — whether it is Azure growth below 25% for two consecutive quarters combined with capital expenditure above 20% of revenue, or free cash flow conversion below 0.65 for two consecutive full fiscal years with no Azure AI improvement, or operating margin below 42% for two consecutive quarters, or the IRS dispute escalating to Tax Court or a charge above $5 billion, or a formal FTC complaint seeking structural remedies, or gross margin below 65% for two consecutive quarters, or commercial remaining-performance-obligation growth below 30% or an absolute quarterly decline.

  • If the price falls below the buy edge: confirm the thesis is intact first. If intact: initiate a position addition in tranches — a first tranche below $352 (which represents a 40% margin of safety against the base-case scenario midpoint of approximately $570 per share), a second tranche below $322 (40% margin of safety against the discounted-cash-flow intrinsic estimate at 8.5%). Each tranche covers 25 to 33% of the intended addition allocation. The earnings yield buy threshold of 5% corresponds to roughly $253 per share on current normalised earnings and is reachable only in a severe bear scenario; the $352 and $322 price levels are the actionable near-term guideposts.

  • If the price rises above the trim edge: trim 20 to 25% of the position to harvest the premium. An earnings yield below 2% implies a market capitalisation above roughly $5.1 trillion — pricing in the base case with limited additional upside. Re-evaluate the remaining position size after trimming. If Azure growth continues to confirm the base case, retain the remainder. Do not trim below 50% of the current holding unless a thesis exit condition is also triggered.

  • If the price rises above the exit edge: exit the full position. An earnings yield below 1.5% implies a market capitalisation above roughly $6.8 trillion, pricing in the full bull case fair value of approximately $938 per share with the three-year annualised return already fully embedded. At 1.5% earnings yield the position earns less than half the risk-free rate with no margin of safety remaining. Redeploy the proceeds at the next buy-edge crossing or into a cheaper alternative. No re-entry within 90 days to remove the pull of anchoring bias.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
