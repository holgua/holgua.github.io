Amazon: The Bet Still Works

The Bottom Line

The bet is working. AWS, Amazon's cloud infrastructure division, grew its revenue by 28% in the three months ending March 2026 — a rate well above the target the bet requires to justify the investment in the business — and its operating profit margin held at 37.7%, far above the floor below which the bet would be called off. The share price sits in the upper half of the hold band, well above fair value, which means the market has already priced in a great deal of the good news. The one condition to watch is the colossal capital expenditure programme: Amazon is spending roughly $147 billion a year building data centres and AI infrastructure, and whether that spending converts into commensurate revenue over the next two years remains the central open question for the thesis.

About Amazon

Amazon is three businesses operating under one roof. The first and largest is the retail and logistics operation — an online shop backed by a network of warehouses and delivery infrastructure that generated $589 billion in combined revenue in 2025. It earns relatively thin margins on its own, but its scale subsidises the delivery speeds and product breadth that attract customers, which in turn makes the platform attractive to the 3.6 million third-party sellers who pay Amazon to list and ship their goods. Those seller fees, not the direct sale of goods, are where much of the retail profit lives.

The second business is Amazon Web Services, or AWS. AWS rents computing power, data storage, and a growing suite of artificial intelligence tools to businesses over the internet. It generated $128.7 billion in revenue in 2025 — 18% of the company's total — but contributed 57% of consolidated operating profit, at a 35.4% profit margin (meaning 35 cents of every revenue dollar became profit). AWS is the engine room: its profits fund the capital reinvestment that drives the rest.

The third business is digital advertising. When someone searches on Amazon or watches a sponsored product scroll past, that is Amazon's advertising network at work. With $68.6 billion in revenue growing at 22% a year, advertising is now comparable in scale to Google's YouTube, and it enjoys structural advantages that most advertising platforms do not — specifically, that the people seeing the adverts are already in a buying frame of mind.

The three businesses reinforce each other. Retail generates customer data. Data makes the advertising network more precise. AWS profits fund the logistics and AI infrastructure that improves both retail and advertising. That flywheel is the reason the business is held.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that Amazon is in the middle of a multi-year transition from a logistics-plus-cloud company into the dominant infrastructure provider for artificial intelligence workloads — and that the profits generated by AWS and advertising will compound faster than the headline revenue for three to five more years, at a total return that more than compensates for the share price paid.

For the bet to keep working, the following conditions must hold:

  1. AWS revenue growth stays above 18% per year. Below 15% for two consecutive quarters signals that AI workloads have not absorbed the capital Amazon is pouring into data centres, which would break the central thesis.
  2. AWS operating margin stays above 28%. A sustained margin below 28% for two consecutive quarters — absent a clearly explained one-off charge — signals that the economics of reinvestment are not holding.
  3. No structural remedy is imposed in the ongoing FTC antitrust case. A court order requiring Amazon to separate its marketplace from its own retail business would fundamentally alter the flywheel.
  4. Capital expenditure above $120 billion does not persist into 2027 and 2028 without commensurate AWS revenue acceleration. The bet assumes AI-data-centre spending eventually normalises as a share of revenue; prolonged overspend without demand to match would signal stranded assets.
  5. Advertising revenue growth stays in double digits. A sustained fall to single-digit growth for two consecutive years signals that the third compounding engine has saturated.
  6. No material weakness in financial reporting is disclosed, and the auditor (EY, in place since 1996) is not replaced unexpectedly.
  7. No single Form 144 insider-sale filing from founder Jeff Bezos exceeds 40 million shares, which would signal an abnormal acceleration beyond his established pre-planned sale programme.
  8. The owner-earnings yield (a measure of cash profitability relative to the share price) stays above 3%. Falling below signals the market has priced in the full optimistic capex-normalisation scenario with nothing left for the shareholder.

The Rules

The five branches of the pre-committed action plan:

  1. If the thesis is confirmed (all conditions working, no exit triggered, price inside the bands): hold — no action. Continue compounding.
  2. If the thesis is weakening (one or more conditions deteriorating but not yet broken): do not add to the position. Re-validate after the next two quarterly filings; one quarter of data, then confirm with a second.
  3. If the thesis is broken (any written exit condition has fired): close the full position within 30 days. No averaging down. The AWS revenue or margin exits require confirmation across two consecutive quarters; an FTC structural remedy or auditor replacement is an immediate close.
  4. If the share price falls to the buy line ($140): confirm the thesis is intact (no exit triggered; AWS growth still above 18%), then add to the position in tranches over one to two quarters.
  5. If the share price rises to the trim line ($263) or the exit line ($421): trim 25–33% of the position at $263 to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding; close the full position at $421, where the market is pricing in roughly eleven years of base-case compounding in one go and the implied future return falls below a reasonable threshold.

The Conditions Today

AWS revenue growth — confirming. In the three months ending 2026-03-31, AWS net sales of $37.6 billion grew 28.4% compared to the same period a year earlier, the highest growth rate in at least six quarters. Amazon also disclosed $364 billion in remaining contracted AWS revenue commitments (with roughly 45% to be recognised over the next two years), which provides multi-year visibility. This is well above the 18% target and far from the 15% exit line.

AWS operating margin — mixed. The AWS margin in the first quarter of 2026 was 37.7% — calculated as $14.2 billion operating income divided by $37.6 billion revenue. This is about 1.7 percentage points lower than the same quarter in 2025, as Amazon invested heavily in AI infrastructure. At 37.7%, the margin is roughly ten percentage points above the 28% floor at which the exit condition would fire. The compression is attributable to planned spending, and the revenue acceleration validates that the capacity is being converted into demand. This driver is classified as mixed rather than confirming because the compression is real, even if it does not yet cross a threshold.

Advertising revenue growth — confirming. Advertising services reached $17.2 billion in the first quarter of 2026, a 24% increase year-on-year. The filing notes that increased advertising sales were a primary driver of improved operating income in both the North America and International segments. Single-digit growth for two consecutive years remains the exit trigger; the current trajectory is nowhere near it.

International segment profitability — confirming. The International segment earned $1.4 billion in operating profit on $39.8 billion in revenue in the first quarter of 2026, a margin of 3.6%. That compares to a 3.0% margin in the same quarter a year earlier ($1.0 billion on $33.5 billion). The segment, which lost money for years as Amazon built its global logistics network, is now a third source of compounding operating income.

Capital expenditure trajectory — mixed. Total capital expenditure on a trailing twelve-month basis reached approximately $147 billion as of the first quarter of 2026, nearly double the $88 billion rate a year earlier. The relevant exit condition (condition 4 above) applies to 2027 and 2028, not to today's trailing figure, and it fires only if the high spending persists without commensurate AWS revenue acceleration. With AWS growing at 28% and $364 billion in contracted backlog, that clause is not met today. The capex trajectory is the single most important number to watch in the next two annual results.

FTC antitrust case — silent. Amazon's quarterly report references ongoing FTC litigation in its risk factors but discloses no new trial schedule and no structural remedy. Exit condition 3 is not triggered.

Capital allocation and insider activity — mixed. Amazon has not repurchased any shares since 2022, leaving $6.1 billion of a $10 billion authorisation untouched. In March 2026, Amazon issued $37 billion in US dollar bonds and €14.5 billion in euro bonds to fund AI infrastructure spending. Jeff Bezos's most recent Form 144 filing disclosed plans to sell 25 million shares — well below the 40 million threshold that would constitute an exit signal. The thesis explicitly characterises Amazon's capital allocation as "acceptable but not excellent," and the facts are consistent with that characterisation.

Retail tariff exposure — mixed. The quarterly filing explicitly notes that "China-based sellers account for significant portions of our third-party seller services and advertising revenues" and flags tariff policy changes as a recurring risk. Despite those headwinds, the North America segment's operating income grew 41% in the first quarter of 2026. The advertising growth rate (condition 5) is nowhere near single digits. This is a watch item that does not yet approach any exit line.

Overall revenue growth — confirming. Consolidated revenue in the first quarter of 2026 was $181.5 billion, up 16.6% compared to the first quarter of 2025. Amazon guided for $194–199 billion in the second quarter of 2026, implying 16–19% growth.

The Share Price

At $250, Amazon's share price sits in the upper half of the hold band — meaning it is above the mid-point between the fair-value anchor and the trim line, but below the trim line itself.

The valuation framework works from an owner-earnings yield: a measure of how much cash profit the business generates relative to what the market charges to own it. At the full-year 2025 figures, owner earnings (broadly: operating cash flow minus all capital expenditure, then adjusted downward for stock-based compensation paid to employees) came to roughly $113 billion. Against a market value of $2,690 billion at $250 per share, that yields approximately 4.2%. The written fair-value anchor for this holding is a 6.0% yield, which implies a fair-value share price of around $176. The buy line is set at a 7.5% yield ($140). The trim line is at a 4.0% yield ($263).

In plain terms: at $250, the share price is already well above what the rules define as fair value, and only $13 below the price at which trimming is called for. The market has priced in a great deal of confidence that Amazon's AI-infrastructure investment will convert into cash returns. That confidence may well be warranted — the Q1 2026 figures support it — but it means there is no bargain available at today's price.

What's Wobbling

The capital expenditure surge. Amazon spent approximately $147 billion on property, equipment, and data-centre infrastructure in the twelve months ending 2026-03-31 — nearly equal to its total operating cash flow of $148.5 billion over the same period. That leaves almost no free cash after spending. The thesis is that this spending will generate commensurate returns as AI workloads scale onto the capacity; AWS growing at 28% with a $364 billion contracted backlog supports that narrative. But exit condition 4 watches whether the heavy spending persists into 2027 and 2028 without the expected revenue lift — and the evidence for 2026 alone is not yet sufficient to close that question. The next two annual results matter more than any other single data point for this holding.

Retail tariff exposure. Amazon explicitly flagged that Chinese sellers constitute a significant share of its marketplace and advertising revenue base. US tariff policy changes represent a real and growing headwind for the economics of cross-border commerce. So far the numbers hold up — North America operating income grew 41% in the first quarter of 2026 despite the tariff environment — but tariff escalation is a risk that has not yet been fully tested in the figures.

The fair-value drift warning. The valuation methodology used by this holding anchors fair value at a 6% owner-earnings yield, implying a share price of about $176. A separate scenario analysis — built by triangulating peer earnings multiples, historical self-multiples, and scenario cash flows — derives a base-case central estimate of $554. The two anchors diverge by 215%. The higher figure reflects a methodology that applies market multiples to forward earnings; the lower figure reflects the orthodox Buffett-style insistence on what the business actually earns today. Both are defensible. The gap is flagged in the underlying data and warrants deliberate review before the next add or trim decision.

No share buybacks. Amazon has not repurchased a single share since 2022, leaving $6.1 billion of authorised capacity untouched, while simultaneously spending $128 billion on capital infrastructure. The 2022–23 period, when the share price fell as low as $84, would have been an unusually productive time to buy back shares. That opportunity was not taken. This does not breach any exit condition, but it is the clearest illustration of why the thesis characterises Amazon's capital allocation as acceptable rather than excellent.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • AWS demand disappoints. If competing cloud providers — particularly Microsoft Azure via its OpenAI partnership — capture an outsized share of new AI workloads while Amazon's own capacity build continues, the capex that has been committed could sit underutilised. In that scenario, cash flow would remain compressed while the market re-rates AWS away from a high-growth asset toward a mature infrastructure asset. The bear-case share price implied by the scenario analysis is around $216, representing roughly a 14% decline from today's level — and the tail risk, if a forced structural separation were also imposed by the courts, would be considerably larger.
  • An FTC structural remedy is imposed. The FTC's monopoly trial is ongoing. A court order requiring Amazon to separate its marketplace from its own retail operations would strip the data, logistics, and advertising flywheel that gives each segment its structural advantage. This risk is low-probability but high-impact; it is the scenario that would most directly impair the moat.
  • Capital expenditure fails to normalise. The current thesis assumes that infrastructure spending as a share of revenue will decline toward 13–15% by 2027. If AI demand grows more slowly than capacity, or if competitive dynamics force continued spending to defend market position, Amazon could find itself in a cycle of high spending and thin cash generation for longer than the model assumes.
  • Tariff escalation bites deeply into marketplace economics. A material decline in the number or revenue contribution of China-based third-party sellers would reduce both marketplace fees and advertising revenue simultaneously, hitting two of the three compounding engines at once.
  • A broader market re-rating. Amazon's share price embeds considerable optimism about growth. If the general market environment moves to price growth assets more conservatively — as it did in 2022, when the stock fell from $167 to $84 — the share price can move sharply even when the underlying business is intact.

Gaps in the Picture

Capital expenditure split. Amazon does not separately disclose maintenance capital expenditure (the spending required simply to keep existing capacity running) versus growth capital expenditure (the spending to add new data centres and logistics facilities). The valuation uses total operating cash flow minus total capital expenditure as the measure of cash earned by the business, and then adjusts for stock-based compensation. This is conservative in the sense that it treats all capital spending as equivalent — but it means earnings figures used to set the buy and trim prices understate the business's true earning power if the majority of capex is genuinely growth-oriented. A realistic estimate, using approximately 40% of depreciation as the maintenance-capex fraction, would put owner earnings at around $118 billion in 2025 rather than $113 billion — a meaningful but not decisive difference.

Two fair-value anchors coexist. The valuation band used to set the buy ($140), trim ($263), and exit ($421) prices is derived from a 6% owner-earnings yield applied to the 2025 cash figures. A separate bottom-up scenario analysis, which triangulates the business's value by applying peer and historical earnings multiples to forward profit scenarios, produces a base-case central estimate of $554 per share. The two figures measure different things — the yield-based anchor emphasises current earnings power; the multiple-based anchor emphasises expected growth. A future schema update will align the two into a single coherent framework.
