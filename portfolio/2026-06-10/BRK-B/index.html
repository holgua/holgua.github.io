Berkshire: The Bet Is Wobbling

The Bottom Line

Berkshire's central engine is intact — book value advanced, the insurance float grew, and the railroad earned more per dollar of operating cost than it has in years. The wobble sits in one place, but it is a load-bearing one: GEICO had its weakest underwriting quarter in some time, with claim costs and customer-acquisition spending both climbing. The share price, at around $484, sits exactly on its long-run fair-value anchor. None of the pre-committed rules call for buying, trimming, or selling at this combination of working conditions and fair-priced shares, and the rule for a wobble is explicit: do not add. The one condition to watch is the GEICO read in the next quarterly filing.

The Verdict

The overall call is the same regardless of how the shares were acquired: continue holding what is already owned, do not add new capital to the position, and wait for the next quarterly update on the GEICO situation before changing anything. New money is not called for at today's price. The fine print differs slightly by purchase price, which is what the five situations below address.

  • As a fresh investment at today's price (around $484 per share). Wait. The share price sits at the fair-value anchor, not in the bargain range, and one condition has weakened. The rules call for a fresh thesis reading after the next quarterly filing — or for a meaningful pullback toward the cheap-edge price of $417 per share — before any new money goes in. Price alerts at $484 and $417 would do the work.
  • As an existing investment bought below $400 per share — currently sitting on a gain of roughly 21% or more. Hold. The big-gain reading does not trigger a trim. The base verdict is hold, the underlying compounding engine is working, and none of the four written conditions that would call for an exit have been crossed. Sitting still is the action.
  • As an existing investment bought between $400 and $500 per share — currently somewhere between a 3% loss and a 21% gain, give or take. Hold. This is the band today's share price sits in. The price reading is neutral — neither cheap nor expensive — and the GEICO wobble converts a "do nothing" into a "do nothing and also don't add." No exit condition is crossed. Existing shares stay.
  • As an existing investment bought between $500 and $600 per share — currently showing a loss of up to about 19%. Hold. A mild loss with a wobbling condition still does not trigger a sell. The deteriorating GEICO read is a watch item, not a sell trigger, and none of the four exit conditions has been touched. The discipline here is to resist the urge to average down on the loss; the rules say no new money until the wobble settles.
  • As an existing investment bought above $600 per share — currently showing a loss of roughly 19% or more. Hold. Even at a deeper loss the bet is not broken — the balance-sheet equity rose by close to ten billion dollars in the latest quarter, on top of a double-digit gain over the prior year. Selling at a deep loss into a wobbling-but-not-broken bet is the path the rules were written to prevent. Wait for the next quarterly filing.

About Berkshire

Berkshire Hathaway is a collection of operating businesses held inside one parent company, plus a very large portfolio of shares in other listed companies. The collection makes money in three reinforcing ways. First, its insurance businesses — most visibly the car insurer GEICO, plus large reinsurance arms — write policies for premium income and hold the cash from those premiums until claims are paid. That pool of held cash, called the float, is roughly $177 billion and currently costs Berkshire less than nothing — policyholders are, in effect, paying Berkshire to hold their money. Second, the wholly-owned operating businesses — the BNSF railroad spanning the western United States, the regulated electricity and pipeline utilities grouped under Berkshire Hathaway Energy, the aerospace-parts maker Precision Castparts, the lubricants business Lubrizol, the truck-stop chain Pilot, and dozens of others — earn their own operating profits. Third, the parent company invests the float and the retained operating profits into shares of other companies and into Treasury bills, building a stockpile that currently sits at roughly $374 billion in cash and government paper alongside a roughly $298 billion equity portfolio.

The structure means the parent's central job is allocation: deciding where the next dollar of retained earnings should go — into a wholly-owned business, into a share purchase, into a new acquisition, into buying back Berkshire's own shares, or into the cash pile waiting for a better opportunity. That allocation job passed from Warren Buffett, who had done it for six decades, to Greg Abel on the first day of 2026. The bet on Berkshire today is largely a bet on Abel continuing to make that allocation call well.

The Case for the Bet

The bet, stated simply: Berkshire's new chief executive and the management culture built around him will keep reinvesting retained earnings and insurance float into high-returning businesses and securities at rates that beat what a passive index would deliver, for years to come.

For the bet to keep working, five conditions must keep holding:

  1. Balance-sheet equity per share keeps growing at least as fast as a broad world-equity index over rolling five-year windows. If the compounding edge disappears for five consecutive years against the index, the central premise has failed.
  2. No large acquisition reveals a loss of capital discipline. Specifically, no deal larger than $20 billion gets paid for at more than one-and-a-half times the target's book value, and no past deal subsequently requires a writedown of $5 billion or more.
  3. The decentralised owner-operator culture stays intact. No regular dividend, no breakup, no surrender to an activist campaign, no abandonment of the model that gives the operating businesses long leashes.
  4. The shares stay priced below twice book value. Above that line, the price has fully priced in optimistic compounding and the bet loses against a passive index on opportunity cost.
  5. The insurance and underwriting engine keeps performing. Float keeps growing, the cost of float stays at or below zero, and the combined ratio at the major insurance arms — particularly GEICO — stays in a healthy range against the longer-term trend.

The Rules

The pre-committed rules cover every combination of how the bet is doing and where the share price sits.

  • If the conditions are all working, hold and let the position compound. No action.
  • If a condition is wobbling but the bet is not broken, do not add new money. Re-read the wobbling condition after the next quarterly filing before making any other decision.
  • If the bet is broken — meaning any of the four exit conditions in §5 above has been crossed — exit the full position within thirty days. No averaging down.
  • If the share price falls below the cheap-edge price, confirm the conditions are still working first. If they are, add to the position.
  • If the share price rises above the trim-edge price, trim a quarter to a third of the position to bank the premium while keeping the core holding.
  • If the share price rises above an extreme upper edge — the line where the market is pricing in roughly ten years of base-case compounding all at once — wind the whole position down. Redeploy the proceeds at the next cheap-edge crossing or into a cheaper alternative, and do not re-enter for ninety days, so the previous price is not anchored.

The Conditions Today

Balance-sheet equity per share. Equity rose by $9.8 billion in the three months ending in March 2026, reaching $727.2 billion. Over the full prior year, the same line gained 10.5%. The compounding engine, measured the way the bet asks it to be measured, is working.

No reckless acquisition. The first sizeable allocation event of the new chief executive's tenure was the purchase of OxyChem, completed at the start of 2026 for roughly $9.5 billion. That is comfortably below the $20 billion size line and shows no early signs of needing a writedown. Settled.

Cultural model intact. No regular dividend has been introduced, no breakup proposed, no activist demand accepted. The decentralised operating model is unchanged. Settled.

Price below twice book value. Today's shares trade at 1.45 times book — well below the 2.0-times line. Settled.

Insurance and underwriting engine. This is where the news from the latest filing is split. The float grew by half a billion dollars in the quarter to $176.9 billion, and management explicitly states the cost of the float was negative in both the latest quarter and the comparable quarter a year earlier — meaning the float remains an exceptional funding source. At the aggregate level, insurance underwriting earned more before tax than it did a year ago ($2.265 billion versus $1.722 billion). But that aggregate hides the wobble: there were no significant catastrophe events in the latest quarter, which flattered the comparison, and within the mix, GEICO — the bet's most visible insurance arm — deteriorated sharply. The deterioration is the load-bearing piece of the story this quarter and is treated in its own section below.

Two further conditions that are not exit triggers but are worth a single sentence each. The operating-profit pillar at the BNSF railroad improved: the ratio of operating costs to operating revenues fell 2.3 percentage points to 65.6%, and pre-tax earnings rose 13.5%. Operating earnings across the five main reporting buckets totalled $10.1 billion in the quarter, up about 5% from a year earlier.

The Share Price

The shares closed the period at around $484. Berkshire's measured fair-value anchor sits at 1.45 times book value, which currently works out to a per-share price of about $484. In other words, the shares sit exactly on the line that the rules treat as neutral — neither cheap nor expensive. The cheap-edge price, where the buying rule activates, is $417 per share, about 14% below today's price. The trim-edge price, where the selling rule activates on the way up, is $584 per share, about 21% above. Reading: fair, not cheap, not expensive.

One number to know: at 1.45 times book, the share price is roughly equal to what would be received if every business and security inside Berkshire were liquidated at the values stated on its own balance sheet — a level Berkshire's own buyback policy has historically treated as the line below which it is willing to buy its own shares.

The Standing Orders

The rules translate the share-price bands into three standing orders and two price alerts. Each is the operationalisation of a pre-committed rule — not a new judgement made here.

Price alerts (informational).

  • A first alert at $484, the fair-value anchor. A crossing means the share price has returned to a neutral reading; no action follows, but a fresh read of the conditions is warranted.
  • A second alert at $417, the cheap-edge price. A crossing arms the buy order described below; before any new money goes in, the latest re-validation should confirm the conditions are still working.

Standing orders (actionable).

  • A standing buy order at $417. The rules call for adding in tranches once the share price closes at or below this line and the conditions are still working. The order may be staged across one or two quarters rather than filled in a single fill.
  • A standing trim order at $584. The rules call for winding down a quarter to a third of the position when the share price closes at or above this line, to bank the premium while keeping the core holding.
  • A standing wind-down order at $651. The rules call for closing the full position when the share price closes at or above this line. The market is pricing in roughly ten years of base-case compounding instantly at that reading; the implied forward return falls below what a passive index would be expected to deliver, and reallocation beats continuing to hold. No re-entry for ninety days after the wind-down, so the previous price is not anchored.

The three order levels do not contradict the situation-by-situation verdicts above: those verdicts describe the current reading; the standing orders describe what the rules will execute if the share price moves to each line.

What's Wobbling

The single condition that has weakened this quarter is the underwriting result at GEICO. The numbers are unambiguous in direction.

Pre-tax underwriting earnings at GEICO fell 34.8% from a year earlier, to $1.416 billion from $2.173 billion. The combined ratio — the share of every premium dollar consumed by claims and operating expenses, where below 100% means a profit and above means a loss — climbed 7.5 percentage points to 87.3%. The deterioration came from two places at once: claim costs rose, with the loss ratio up 4.9 percentage points to 73.9% on higher frequency and severity of bodily-injury claims; and customer-acquisition spending rose, with the expense ratio up 2.6 percentage points to 13.4%. Premiums earned grew just 1.5%, a notable deceleration.

A combined ratio of 87.3% is still profitable in absolute terms — GEICO is making money on every premium dollar — but the trajectory matters: claim costs and acquisition costs rising together while premium growth slows is the pattern the bet's GEICO condition was written to watch for. The question for the next quarterly filing is whether this is an early sign of share loss to telematics-led competitors like Progressive, or a transient claims-environment effect that fades. The next filing will tell.

Two smaller wobbles to file but not act on. First, the utilities business under Berkshire Hathaway Energy saw earnings from its U.S. utilities sub-segment fall 16.1% on the step-down of production tax credits, higher wildfire-prevention spending, and higher interest expense — though the consolidated utilities result was up modestly thanks to natural-gas pipelines. Second, share buybacks were minimal: just $235 million in the quarter, which management itself described as "relatively minor", at a time when the cash pile sits at roughly $374 billion. The buyback discipline is intact (buy back only when shares are clearly below intrinsic value) but the small size signals management does not currently view the share price as a bargain.

None of these wobbles, including the load-bearing GEICO one, has crossed any of the four exit conditions listed in §5. That is why the verdict is wait and watch, not sell.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • The capital allocation premium fades. The bet's hinge is that Berkshire's chief executive can keep reinvesting the cash pile and the operating profits at higher returns than a passive index would deliver. If that premium fades — if the $374 billion cash pile is deployed at mediocre returns, or sits too long earning Treasury-bill yields while the world compounds elsewhere — the case for owning Berkshire over a low-cost world index weakens steadily.
  • A reckless acquisition. A single large purchase at a high price for a business without a real moat would cross the second exit condition and break the bet. The size threshold is $20 billion at more than one-and-a-half times the target's book value.
  • GEICO's underwriting trajectory does not recover. If the next quarter shows the same direction — claims costs and acquisition spending rising together, premium growth still slow — GEICO moves from a watch item to a serious structural concern, particularly if a Progressive-led share-loss interpretation gains weight.
  • A catastrophic insurance loss event. A hurricane season, an earthquake, or a series of large natural disasters severe enough to consume underwriting profit for a year or more. The float economics do not collapse from a single event, but the earnings power can.
  • Utility wildfire liabilities widen. The PacifiCorp wildfire cases at Berkshire Hathaway Energy continue. A recent appellate ruling went Berkshire's way, but the cumulative accrual remains around $2.9 billion and the underlying exposure is real.

Gaps in the Picture

A handful of measurement caveats live behind the numbers above.

The share count Berkshire publishes in its filings does not flow through to the standard data feeds in a clean way — Berkshire has two share classes (A and B) with a fixed conversion ratio, and the typical industry data extraction tools do not capture both. The share count used for the per-share calculations above is therefore implied from the published market capitalisation divided by the current share price (2.15 billion B-equivalent shares). The implied figure ties to within a fraction of a percent of the actual share count reported in the latest quarterly filing.

The book value figure used for the price-to-book calculation reflects the position at the end of 2025 ($717 billion). The end-of-quarter position three months later was $727 billion; using that more recent figure would shift the price-to-book ratio from 1.450 to about 1.430 — still inside the band, still well below the trim line, and not a change that affects the verdict.

The share price used in the verdict and the standing orders is $484, the figure reflected in the most recent re-validation. The market data fetched separately a few days earlier reports a slightly different intraday price of around $488. Both readings sit inside the same band; the verdict is identical either way.
