SP500: The Bet Still Works

The Bottom Line

The S&P 500 basket — the 500 largest US-listed companies, owned in proportion to their market value — continues to operate largely as the bet requires. Five of the six conditions that must hold are confirmed working; only one, the return premium over government bonds, has tipped briefly into negative territory. What has changed is the share price: at $725 per share, the basket now yields less to its owners than a ten-year government bond pays to its creditors — a signal the rules treat as a trigger to reduce the position, not abandon it. The one condition to watch closely is whether that negative premium persists into a second consecutive quarterly reading.

The Verdict

The basket's conditions are largely intact, but the valuation signal has fired. The rules call for trimming 25 to 33% of the holding — not because anything is broken, but because the basket is priced for optimism at a moment when risk-free alternatives pay more. That is a harvesting of a premium, not an exit.

  • As a fresh investment at today's price (around $725 per share). The rules say wait. The basket's earnings yield of 3.85% sits below the fair-value threshold of 5%, and below the 10-year government bond yield of 4.54% — meaning risk-free government bonds currently pay more than the basket while carrying none of the equity volatility. The rules set a price alert at $558 (the fair-value level) and a buy threshold at $447; neither has been reached. No new position is warranted at current prices.

  • As an existing investment bought below $700 per share — currently sitting on a gain of around 4% or more. The rules say trim 25 to 33%. This is the cleanest situation for harvesting the premium: reducing the position crystallises a gain and brings the remaining holding closer to the kind of price the rules consider fair. The basket's valuation signal has fired on purely mathematical grounds — the earnings yield has crossed below the 4% trim threshold — and the rules do not require the thesis to be broken to act on it.

  • As an existing investment bought roughly between $700 and $800 per share — currently around break-even, give or take. The rules say trim 25 to 33%. The valuation trigger fires regardless of whether the individual holding is in profit or at a small loss. The trim signal is about the basket's pricing relative to risk-free rates, not about the individual cost basis. A small paper loss does not exempt the holding from the valuation call.

  • As an existing investment bought between $800 and $900 per share — currently showing a loss of roughly 9 to 19%. The rules say trim 25 to 33%. This is the uncomfortable situation: the valuation trim rule does not make an exception for holders sitting on a loss. The basket is expensive relative to risk-free rates regardless of what was paid for it. A decision to hold rather than trim is a choice to override the rules; that may be reasonable given tax considerations or the specific loss size, but the rules' call is clear.

  • As an existing investment bought above $900 per share — currently showing a loss of around 19% or deeper. The rules say trim 25 to 33%. The same valuation logic applies; the deeper loss makes trimming psychologically harder and may warrant a tax-loss conversation, but the rules do not exempt deep-loss holders from the valuation signal. The rules treat the basket's price-relative-to-earnings as the deciding variable, not the individual acquisition cost.

About the Fund

The SPDR S&P 500 ETF Trust, traded as SPY, is the oldest and largest exchange-traded fund in the United States, with roughly $784 billion in assets. It owns fractional stakes in the 500 largest US-listed companies, in proportion to their stock-market value — so a company worth twice as much as another gets roughly twice the weight in the basket. Buying one share of SPY is, in effect, buying a tiny slice of each of those 500 businesses simultaneously.

The fund charges 0.0945% of the investment per year in fees — less than one dollar for every thousand dollars invested — and distributes a small income of around 0.98% annually. It is managed by State Street Investment Management and has operated under the same structure since 1993. Technology companies account for 39% of the basket at the current reading, reflecting the outsized market value of companies like NVIDIA, Apple, Alphabet, and Microsoft. The top ten holdings account for roughly 39% of the total; that concentration at the top is structural — it reflects what the market has decided these businesses are worth, not a deliberate call by the fund's managers.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is not that the S&P 500 is always a good place to deploy new money. It is narrower: that over the holding period, the basket's earnings yield — the share of each dollar's value that represents the underlying businesses' annual profits — will exceed what a government bond pays, by at least two percentage points. When that condition holds, owning the basket instead of government bonds earns an extra return for the extra risk taken; when it does not, the basic premise of holding equities rather than cash has weakened.

For the basket to keep delivering on that bet, six conditions must hold:

  1. The earnings premium over government bonds stays positive and meaningful. The basket's earnings yield must exceed the 10-year government bond yield by at least two percentage points. Currently the premium is negative — the basket yields less than bonds.
  2. The annual management fee stays below 0.15%. State Street must not raise the fee above that ceiling without a competitor response.
  3. Total assets stay above $100 billion. The fund must remain large enough for large trades without price distortion.
  4. No single holding exceeds 13% of the basket. That would signal a regime-change level of concentration, not the broad-market exposure the bet is for.
  5. The S&P 500 index rules stay intact. S&P Dow Jones Indices — the independent body that defines which companies belong in the 500 — must not materially change the rules in a way that undermines the broad-market mandate.
  6. The quality of the underlying businesses stays high. The median return on equity (a measure of how profitably a business uses the money shareholders have put in) among the top ten holdings must not fall below 15%.

The Rules

The rules translate the basket's current valuation and condition readings into one of five actions. They are applied in a fixed order; the first matching condition wins.

  • If vehicle integrity is breached — any of conditions 2, 3, or 4 above is broken: exit the full position. Move the proceeds to risk-free instruments or to a holding that clears the value test.
  • If the basket's earnings yield falls below the 4% trim threshold — currently 3.85%, which is below 4%: trim 25 to 33%. The basket is meaningfully expensive relative to risk-free rates; reduce the position and harvest the premium.
  • If the basket's earnings yield rises above the 6.25% buy threshold — currently not reached: add to the position. The basket is meaningfully cheap relative to risk-free rates.
  • If any of the six conditions is weakening but not broken — currently, condition 1 is in negative territory: do not add. Re-validate after the next quarter. If the negative premium persists or the underlying quality falls further, consider moving to a lower-cost alternative fund or to cash.
  • If all conditions are confirmed and the basket is fairly priced: hold. The basket is reasonably priced and the vehicle is sound.

Today the second rule fires first: the earnings yield of 3.85% is below the 4% trim threshold.

The Conditions Today

Earnings premium over government bonds. The basket currently yields 3.85% in earnings terms; the 10-year government bond yields 4.54%. The difference — the extra return earned for accepting equity risk — is negative 0.69 percentage points. In other words, the basket pays its owners less than bonds, yet carries full equity market risk. This is the one condition that is currently working against the bet. The written exit condition requires this to persist for two consecutive quarters and the basket earnings yield to fall below 3.0%; neither has happened yet. This is the reading to watch at the next quarterly check.

Underlying business quality. The median return on equity among the top ten deduplicated holdings is 37.28% — well above the 15% floor set in condition 6. NVIDIA leads at 114%, Apple at 141%; both figures are inflated by share buybacks that shrink the equity base, rather than by the underlying business literally earning more than the equity annually. The median strips out that distortion and is the more reliable read. Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft, Broadcom, Meta, and Micron all report returns on equity comfortably above 20%. Tesla is the sole outlier at 4.9%. The basket's quality remains strong; this is not the concern today.

Management fee. The annual fee is 0.0945% — well below the 0.15% ceiling. State Street has not raised prices. No concern.

Single-name concentration. NVIDIA, the largest single holding, accounts for 7.89% of the basket. The ceiling is 13%. There is room for NVIDIA to grow further before the concentration condition is triggered. No concern.

Fund size. Total assets stand at $784 billion. The survival floor is $100 billion. The fund would need to lose about 87% of its assets to breach this condition. No concern.

Index rules. No change to the S&P 500's definition or rules has been announced. This condition is monitored through external sources; no signal of concern in the current snapshot.

The Share Price

At $725 per share, the basket is priced above what the rules consider fair value. The earnings yield on the basket — computed by dividing the profits of the underlying businesses by the total market value of the basket — is 3.85%. The rules define fair value as the price at which that yield equals 5%, which corresponds to roughly $558 per share. The basket is trading at a 30% premium to that level.

Put plainly: at today's price, every dollar invested in the basket earns about 3.85 cents annually in look-through profits; at fair value, that same dollar would earn 5 cents. The gap represents the premium being paid for the expectation of continued earnings growth — an optimism that needs to keep materialising to justify the price. The trim threshold sits at a yield of 4%, corresponding to a price of roughly $698 per share; the basket crossed above that price, which is what fired the trim signal. The buy threshold — the level the rules define as genuinely cheap — is 6.25%, corresponding to a price of roughly $447. That level is 38% below today's price.

The Standing Orders

The rules translate the share-price bands into two price alerts and two standing orders. Each is the operationalisation of a pre-committed rule.

Price alerts (informational). - A first alert at $558, the fair-value anchor. Reaching this level means the basket has repriced to neutral; no action follows automatically, but the conditions should be re-read before deploying new capital. - A second alert at $447, the cheap-edge. Reaching this level arms the buy order below; before adding, the most recent re-validation should confirm the conditions are still working.

Standing orders (actionable). - A standing buy order at $447. The rules call for adding when the basket's earnings yield reaches 6.25% or above — meaning the basket is genuinely cheap relative to risk-free rates. Adding should be staged across one or two quarters rather than in a single purchase. - A standing trim order at $698. The trim threshold has already been crossed: at today's price of $725 the trim signal is active. The rules call for reducing the holding by 25 to 33% while the basket remains above this price.

Vehicle-integrity breaches — not price — govern the sell side of an index basket at the extreme end. No exit-price rule has been set for this holding; if vehicle integrity fails (conditions 2, 3, or 4 above), the rules call for a full exit regardless of price.

What's Wobbling

The earnings premium over government bonds is the single wobbling condition. At negative 0.69 percentage points, the basket yields less to its owners than a ten-year government bond pays to its creditors. That asymmetry — more risk, less yield — is the core case against deploying new money here today. It is also the first step toward the written exit condition: if the yield stays below the bond yield for a second consecutive quarter and the basket earnings yield drops below 3%, the exit triggers. Today, the earnings yield is 3.85%, which is above the 3% floor; the exit condition has not fired.

The technology sector accounts for 39% of the basket, above a 30% structural flag. This is not unusual drift — it reflects the market-cap weights of the largest technology businesses — but it does mean the basket is more sensitive to a technology-sector correction than a sector-neutral broad-market measure would be. That amplifies the downside in the scenarios below.

What Could Break the Fund

  • A second consecutive quarter of negative earnings premium with basket earnings yield below 3%. That combination triggers the written exit condition. It would require either a further rise in government bond yields or a further rise in the basket's price, or both, at the next quarterly snapshot.
  • A sharp multiple contraction from current levels. The basket's five-year forward return in the bear scenario — modest earnings growth and reversion toward long-run valuation norms — is negative 2.82% annually. The dominant driver is multiple compression: starting from a trailing price-to-earnings ratio of roughly 26, even a fall to 18 times earnings (still above the long-run average) overwhelms earnings growth and income yield.
  • A concentrated technology correction. Nearly 40% of the basket sits in technology-related businesses. A sector-specific shock — through regulation, competitive disruption, or a reversal of the artificial-intelligence investment cycle — would hit the basket harder than the headline diversification suggests.
  • A fee increase by State Street. Competitive dynamics make this unlikely, but a meaningful fee hike above 0.15% would trigger exit condition 2. State Street would need to raise fees by roughly 58% above the current rate to breach the guardrail.
  • A single holding ballooning past 13% of the basket. NVIDIA is at 7.89%. A significant further increase in its relative weight — possible if the technology cycle continues to reward it disproportionately — would cross the concentration threshold and trigger exit condition 4.

Gaps in the Picture

The five-year forward return estimates use a simplified model: the current earnings yield, an assumed earnings growth rate, and a terminal price-to-earnings ratio. A cyclically adjusted earnings measure — which would smooth out single-year earnings swings — is not available from the current data source, as it requires aggregated earnings history across all 500 constituents. The forward scenarios should be read as directional illustrations, not precise forecasts.

The earnings premium is computed from a single quarterly snapshot. One quarter of negative premium does not establish a trend; the rules require two consecutive quarters before the exit condition can fire, precisely because one-quarter readings can be noisy.

The index-rules and sponsor-structure conditions — conditions 5 and 6 in the bet — are monitored through external sources rather than automated signals. No breach is currently flagged, but these conditions cannot be verified programmatically; they rely on staying current with announcements from S&P Dow Jones Indices and State Street.
