Booking: The Bet Still Works

The Bottom Line

The bet is working. Booking Holdings' online travel marketplace is holding its economic position: room-night growth is running ahead of the exit threshold, the share of revenue kept from every booking ticked upward in the three months ending March 2026, and the capital-return machine returned $4.4 billion to shareholders in that single quarter. The share price sits in the lower half of the hold band, modestly below fair value — the market is not offering a bargain, but it is not pricing in unrealistic optimism either. The one condition to watch is AI strategy: the Connected Trip vision has been articulated, but no concrete shipped-product metrics have been disclosed, and the clock on a Q3 2027 deadline is running.

About Booking

Booking Holdings is the company behind Booking.com, the world's largest accommodation-reservation website, which lists roughly 4.5 million properties across more than 220 countries — hotels, apartments, villas, and everything in between. The group also operates Priceline (discount travel), Agoda (Asia-focused accommodation), KAYAK (flight and hotel search aggregation), and OpenTable (restaurant reservations). The overwhelming majority of the group's revenue comes from Booking.com.

The business model is a two-sided marketplace: travellers search and book on one side; hotels, apartments, and short-term rental operators list their inventory on the other. Booking Holdings takes a percentage of each completed booking — roughly ten cents of every dollar in gross bookings flows through as revenue, after netting out cancellations and currency movements. The marketing machine that keeps both sides of the marketplace engaged runs largely on paid internet search, with a growing share of customers arriving directly through Booking.com's mobile app (a high-fifties percentage of room nights in the twelve months ending March 2026, up from the mid-fifties range the prior year). In the full year 2025, the group generated $26.9 billion in revenue and kept about 34 cents of every revenue dollar as free cash (cash produced by the business after maintenance spending).

Capital returns are central to the investment story. The group has repurchased more than $54 billion of its own shares over the years, leaving book equity negative — a feature, not a flaw, of a business that returns so much cash that the accounting value of assets falls below the accounting value of liabilities. The share count falls year by year, meaning each remaining share owns a larger slice of the business's earnings even if total earnings grow only modestly. In the three months ending March 2026 alone, the group returned $4.4 billion to shareholders through buybacks and dividends, with $18.2 billion of authorised repurchase capacity remaining.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that Booking Holdings is the most durable global online travel marketplace, and that its two-sided network — millions of properties on one side, hundreds of millions of travellers on the other — is strong enough to survive the pressures that threaten it. The supporting bet is that disciplined capital return compounds per-share value even if headline growth slows.

For the bet to keep working, all of the following conditions must continue to hold:

  1. Room-night growth stays above the deterioration threshold. Bookings of accommodation room nights must not decline to low-single-digit or negative territory across multiple regions for two or more consecutive quarters, absent an isolated external shock. A sustained broad-based slowdown would signal that the network effect is leaking — that travellers are finding rooms elsewhere.

  2. Marketing spend as a share of bookings does not rise persistently. The percentage of total gross bookings spent on marketing must not rise meaningfully for two or more consecutive years. A sustained rise would mean the business is spending money to win customers the network used to attract naturally — a structural warning that the moat is shrinking.

  3. The take-rate does not compress materially. The fraction of gross bookings that becomes revenue must not fall in a sustained or material way. Persistent compression would signal that competitors, regulators, or AI-based booking agents are squeezing the economics of the marketplace.

  4. Capital-return discipline holds. Buybacks must not slow or stop while the business is generating attractive cash returns. If cash is redirected into a large acquisition or a culture-shifting strategic pivot rather than returned to shareholders, the per-share compounding thesis breaks.

  5. The earnings yield stays above 4%. The cash generated by the business as a percentage of market value must not fall below 4%. Below that level, the shares are priced for optimistic compounding, and the per-share arithmetic no longer justifies holding given the AI-disintermediation risk.

  6. A coherent AI-first strategy is visibly executing by Q3 2027. By the third-quarter 2027 earnings cycle, Booking Holdings management must have articulated and visibly executed a response to AI-mediated trip planning — with concrete shipped products, measurable engagement or direct-traffic uplift, and explicit acknowledgement of disintermediation risk in public communications. The current Connected Trip vision is articulated but not yet demonstrated in product-level metrics.

  7. EU regulatory enforcement stays within compliance costs. European Digital Markets Act or Digital Services Act enforcement must not result in a formal decision imposing structural product remedies — such as mandatory removal of pricing-parity clauses — or fines exceeding 3% of annual global revenue in a single year. Either outcome would cross the line from compliance cost into structural economic impairment.

The Rules

The pre-committed rules translate the state of these conditions and the position of the share price into five possible courses of action:

  • If the thesis is confirmed (all conditions working, no exit signals): hold the position, no action needed. Continue compounding.
  • If the thesis is weakening (one or more conditions deteriorating but no exit condition yet triggered): do not add to the position. Watch the deteriorating driver across the next two quarterly filings — one quarter of data, then confirm with a second — before deciding.
  • If the thesis is broken (an exit condition fires): exit the full position within 30 days. No averaging down. Record which exit condition triggered and why.
  • If the share price falls to or below the buy edge ($156): confirm the thesis is intact first. If confirmed, add to the position in tranches over one to two quarters.
  • If the share price rises to or above the trim edge ($273): trim 25 to 33 percent of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.
  • If the share price rises to or above the exit edge ($437): exit the full position. At that level, the market is pricing in roughly seven years of base-case compounding instantly; the implied forward return falls below the long-run equity premium, and reallocation beats continuing to hold. No re-entry within 90 days.

The Conditions Today

Room-night growth. In the three months ending March 2026, Booking.com recorded 338 million room nights, 5.9% higher than the same period a year earlier. A conflict in the Middle East weighed on March results by an estimated two percentage points; stripping that out, management estimates underlying growth of about 8%. Neither the raw figure nor the ex-conflict estimate is close to the "low-single-digit or negative across multiple regions for two or more consecutive quarters" line that would trigger exit condition (1). That said, this is one quarter of softness, and the Q2 2026 figures will matter for confirming whether the Middle East effect was truly isolated.

Marketing efficiency. Marketing spend as a share of gross bookings was 3.8% in the three months ending March 2026, flat compared with the same period a year earlier. The conflict in the Middle East reduced the return on paid-search spending — cancellations from conflict-affected routes eroded the economics of bookings that had already been paid for through performance marketing. Management states that ex-conflict, the quarter showed modest leverage. The structural decline in search-engine optimisation traffic is a separate watch item: organic search remains a smaller component of total distribution but the multi-year direction matters for condition (2). Neither flat marketing spend nor gradual SEO decline crosses the exit threshold — which requires a meaningful rise over two consecutive years.

Take-rate. Revenue as a percentage of gross bookings ticked up to 10.3% in the three months ending March 2026, from 10.2% in the same period a year earlier. The improvement was driven by faster growth in revenue from facilitating payments — credit-card processing fees and the like — relative to the booking volumes that underlie them. Exit condition (3) requires material or persistent compression; a modest improvement in the opposite direction is the reading here.

Capital return. In the three months ending March 2026, the group repurchased $3.8 billion of its own shares and paid $343 million in dividends, for a total of $4.4 billion returned. The board's remaining authorised repurchase capacity stands at $18.2 billion. No acquisition was announced. Exit condition (4) is not triggered.

Earnings yield. The yield — the business's annual cash generation divided by today's market value — stands at 6.45%, calculated on full-year 2025 free cash flow of $8.5 billion (after deducting share-based compensation). The exit threshold is 4.0%; the current reading is well above that line.

AI strategy. Booking Holdings describes its AI strategy as the "Connected Trip" — an aspiration to serve every element of a journey through an integrated, AI-powered experience. Mobile-app room nights (a proxy for direct engagement) grew from a mid-fifties to a high-fifties percentage of bookings over the past year. What is missing is concrete shipped-product evidence: no measurable engagement uplift from specific AI features, no direct-traffic change attributable to AI, no product-level disclosure of how Connected Trip is influencing conversion or margins. The exit-condition deadline is Q3 2027 — roughly eighteen months away. The clock is running, and the next two or three quarters of filings are where this condition will either start to be addressed or begin to look increasingly at risk.

EU regulatory environment. The European Digital Markets Act gatekeeper designation for Booking.com came into live compliance in late 2024. No formal enforcement decision imposing structural remedies or fines exceeding 3% of global revenue has been issued. The regulatory overhang continues as a compliance cost, not yet a structural economic impairment.

Revenue growth. Full-year 2025 revenue rose 13.4% compared with full-year 2024 ($26.9 billion vs. $23.7 billion). In the three months ending March 2026, revenue grew 16.2% year-on-year ($5.5 billion vs. $4.8 billion), or roughly 10% stripping out a six-percentage-point currency tailwind. Both figures are consistent with the base-case assumption of low-double-digit sustained growth.

The Share Price

At $170 (as of the latest data point used in this review), the shares sit modestly below the fair-value anchor of $199. That anchor is derived from the yield that has historically represented fair value for this business: an owner-earnings yield of 5.5% implies a share price of $199 at today's level of cash generation.

Put plainly, the market is not offering a bargain — the shares are above the buy edge of $156 — but they are also not priced for optimism. The distance to fair value is not large enough to call the shares cheap; the distance to the trim edge ($273) is substantial enough that no harvesting action is warranted. That is what sitting in the lower half of the hold band means in practice.

The separate scenario analysis that builds fair value from peer multiples and self-historical multiples produces a higher central estimate of $305. The two approaches differ because they use different inputs: the yield-based method anchors to current cash generation and a stable yield assumption, while the peer-multiple method reflects how markets have recently valued comparable businesses. The two figures coexist; neither is wrong. The gap between them — $106 per share — reflects genuine uncertainty about the right anchor and is surfaced in Gaps in the Picture.

What's Wobbling

Four of the nine conditions are showing mixed signals rather than clean confirmations.

Room nights. The headline growth rate of 5.9% in the three months ending March 2026 is softer than prior quarters, though the conflict explanation is credible and the ex-conflict figure of ~8% remains well above the exit threshold. The wobble here is temporal: one quarter of softness is fine; two or more of low-single-digit growth across multiple regions — conflict or no conflict — would require a harder look.

Marketing efficiency. The flat marketing ratio is not a problem today, but the underlying SEO trend bears watching. Paid search has always been a cost of doing business for Booking.com; organic search was the cost-free complement. If the organic share continues shrinking and paid search does not offset it with better conversion, the cost structure will eventually show up in the marketing ratio.

AI strategy. This is the wobble that matters most over the medium term. The Connected Trip aspiration is coherent. The mobile-app trend is encouraging. But the eighteen-month window to Q3 2027 is not long, and the exit condition requires not just an articulated strategy but visible execution with measurable outcomes. No products with disclosed engagement metrics have appeared yet.

Forward demand. Booking Holdings stopped providing formal quarterly guidance after the COVID period. The cautionary language about the Middle East conflict carrying into future quarters is appropriate context, not an alarm — but the absence of any positive forward-booking commentary means there is no offsetting signal from the company's own visibility into summer 2026 bookings.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • AI agents disintermediate the booking funnel. If tools built by Google, OpenAI, Perplexity, or others become the primary point where travellers search and book, and if those tools route bookings directly to hotels rather than through an online travel agency, the marketing spend that currently earns a return could stop earning one almost simultaneously. The business would need to rebuild its consumer funnel from scratch, which has never been done successfully at this scale.
  • Google paid-search inflation tightens take-rate economics. Marketing currently consumes roughly 3.8% of gross bookings. If Google raises the effective cost of paid-search placements — through auction dynamics, quality-score changes, or product redesign — the company faces a choice between paying more and losing bookings. There is no clean hedge.
  • EU regulatory enforcement crosses the structural threshold. The Digital Markets Act and Digital Services Act obligations are live. National competition authorities across Europe are active. A formal enforcement decision imposing structural product remedies — particularly on pricing parity, which protects Booking.com's ability to offer the lowest price on its own platform — could meaningfully impair the marketplace economics.
  • Macro travel demand shock. The COVID period showed that travel demand can collapse by half in a single year. Booking Holdings recovered fully, and faster than many expected, but the experience illustrated that no marketplace is immune to external demand shocks. A severe recession, new pandemic, or sustained geopolitical disruption across major travel corridors would compress gross bookings and the economics attached to them.
  • Capital allocation misstep. The company's track record on acquisitions is mixed: KAYAK has held its value (a $457 million goodwill impairment was taken proactively in 2025, which is a sign of honest accounting rather than concealment), but OpenTable has not been a visible contributor to the travel network thesis. A large acquisition that diverts the buyback engine toward a strategically questionable target would shift the risk profile significantly.

Gaps in the Picture

A separate scenario analysis built from peer and self-historical multiples produces a central estimate of $305 per share. The two approaches use different inputs and are designed for different purposes; neither is wrong, but the $106-per-share gap is wide enough to affect practical conclusions. A future update to the thesis setup will resolve which anchor governs the standing orders.

Maintenance capital spending is not separately disclosed. The earnings-yield calculation deducts total capital spending from operating cash flow. Booking Holdings does not separately break out maintenance capital spending (the amount required to keep the existing business running) from growth capital spending (the amount invested in expansion). The risk is that the yield is overstated if maintenance capital has been underestimated. The financial brief estimates maintenance at roughly $200 million of the $322 million total spent in 2025; this split is an estimate, not a disclosed figure.

Share-count input used market-implied dilution. The 2026 quarterly filing reported a diluted share count using standard structured-data conventions that diverged from the market-implied count by a factor of roughly 24, consistent with a 25-for-1 stock split effected in April 2026. The valuation uses the market-implied share count, which produces results consistent with the post-split share price. The cash-flow-based earnings yield is invariant to share count, so this adjustment has no effect on the yield calculation — but it is flagged here for completeness.

No formal forward guidance. Booking Holdings does not provide quarterly earnings guidance. The forward demand commentary available from the latest filing is qualitative and cautionary in tone because of the Middle East conflict. There is no positive forward-booking signal from the company's own visibility that could inform confidence in near-term performance.
