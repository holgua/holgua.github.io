Booking: The Bet Still Works

The Bottom Line

The latest quarterly figures from Booking Holdings — the company behind Booking.com, Priceline, Agoda, KAYAK and OpenTable — reinforce every load-bearing piece of the bet that brought the shares into the portfolio. Bookings are growing, the cut Booking takes on each one is widening, the share buyback machine has shifted into a higher gear, and no rule the conditions are working has been crossed. The share price, near $170, sits just on the cheap side of fair but is not yet in the bargain range that would trigger fresh buying. The one thing worth watching — the open watch item — is whether the company can articulate a credible answer to artificial-intelligence-powered travel agents before the autumn of 2027.

The Verdict

The overall call is hold what is owned; wait to buy more. The bet is working, the share price is fair-leaning-cheap, and the rules call for patience rather than action across every plausible reader situation.

  • As a fresh investment at today's price (around $170 per share). Wait. The shares are roughly 1% above the line the rules use to switch from neutral to actively cheap. A price alert at $213 (the fair-value anchor) and a second at $168 (the cheap line) are the disciplined moves; capital deploys when the second alert fires and the next quarterly filing again confirms the conditions are working.
  • As an existing investment bought below $150 per share — currently sitting on a gain of about 13% or more. Hold. A big-gain cost basis with the bet still intact calls for letting the position run. The one rule that could change this — the company failing to articulate an artificial-intelligence strategy by the autumn of 2027 — is still more than five quarters out.
  • As an existing investment bought roughly between $150 and $175 — currently around break-even, give or take. Hold. This is the band that includes today's share price. The bet is working, the share price sits just shy of the cheap line, and the rules call for sitting still until either the price crosses $168 or the next filing removes the one transient negative in the latest report.
  • As an existing investment bought between $175 and $200 — currently showing a loss of up to about 15%. Hold. A mild-loss cost basis with no rule crossed and the central marketplace bet reinforced this quarter. The rules say neither add nor capitulate; the compounding engine is intact and the share buybacks accelerated sharply year-on-year.
  • As an existing investment bought above $200 per share — currently showing a loss of roughly 15% or more. Hold. A deep-loss cost basis with the bet still intact. The underlying business — the bookings, the take rate, the $3.8 billion of repurchases in a single quarter — is running, and the rules do not call for selling into weakness when nothing has broken.

About Booking Holdings

Booking is the parent company of the world's largest online travel marketplace. The flagship brand, Booking.com, lists roughly 4.5 million places to stay — about four million of them homes, apartments and other alternative accommodations, the rest hotels, motels and resorts — and connects them to travellers in more than two hundred countries. The company takes a small commission on every reservation that completes through its platform. Around that core sit Priceline, Agoda (the Asia-focused brand), KAYAK (a price-comparison search engine) and OpenTable (restaurant reservations).

The financial engine is unusually clean. Booking owns no aeroplanes, no hotel buildings, no warehouses, so almost every dollar of operating cash flow falls through to free cash — the money left over after the business has paid for everything it needs to keep running. On nearly $27 billion of sales last year, free cash was $9.1 billion. That money is returned to shareholders through aggressive share buybacks (the share count has shrunk by more than a fifth since 2022) and a small but growing dividend. The capital-return programme is the second engine behind the bet: even in years when the business grows more slowly, the per-share figures compound because the share count keeps shrinking.

The Case for the Bet

The bet, in one sentence, is that Booking.com is the most likely online travel marketplace to be the last one standing — and that while it stands, the company will compound per-share value by handing the cash back to owners.

The bet works only if the following conditions keep holding:

  1. Bookings keep growing. The number of room-nights reserved through the platform must not fall to low single digits, or turn negative, across multiple regions for two or more consecutive quarters — except where the cause is plainly an isolated regional shock.
  2. The marketing bill does not run away. The share of total bookings that the company spends to acquire customers must not rise meaningfully for two consecutive years. If it does, it is a sign that Booking is buying growth its network used to produce for free.
  3. The cut the company takes does not shrink. The ratio of revenue to total bookings — the take rate — must not compress materially or persistently. Compression would signal that competitors, regulators, or artificial-intelligence agents are squeezing the economics.
  4. The cash keeps coming back to shareholders. Buybacks must continue while shares are reasonably priced, and the cash must not be redirected into a large, value-destructive acquisition or a culture-shifting strategic pivot.
  5. The yield does not get squeezed by the share price. Free cash flow divided by the company's market value — call it the cash yield — must not fall below 4%. Below that line, the share price is pricing in optimism the business cannot back up.
  6. The company has a credible answer to artificial-intelligence agents by the autumn of 2027. Booking must show shipped product and measurable improvements in direct traffic, engagement or margin from its artificial-intelligence strategy by the third-quarter 2027 earnings cycle. The current walled-garden approach is not yet a strategy.
  7. The European regulators do not break the pricing model. Enforcement under the European Union's digital-markets and digital-services rules must not result in structural product remedies that meaningfully impair pricing power, nor in a fine exceeding 3% of annual global revenue in a single year.

The Rules

The rules that govern what happens next are fixed, and each one ties a specific reading to a specific action.

  • If the conditions are all working — every condition holding, no rule crossed — the position is held and left to compound. No new buying, no selling.
  • If one condition has weakened but no rule has been broken, the position is held but no new shares are added. The next two quarterly filings are read carefully on the weakened condition; the call is made after that confirms.
  • If a rule has been broken — any of the seven conditions above failing — the full position is wound down within 30 days. No averaging down.
  • If the share price drops into the bargain range (the cash yield rising above 7%), and the conditions are confirmed to be still working, fresh capital is added in stages over one or two quarters.
  • If the share price climbs into the expensive range (the yield falling below 4%), a quarter to a third of the position is trimmed to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.
  • If the share price climbs into the extreme range (the yield falling far below 4%, to the point where the market is pricing in roughly seven years of base-case compounding instantly), the full position is exited and the proceeds redeployed at the next cheap line or into a cheaper alternative.

The Conditions Today

Bookings keep growing. Global room-nights grew 6% in the first three months of 2026 against the same period a year earlier; total reservations measured in dollars rose 15% to $53.8 billion. Strip out the conflict in the Middle East — which the company describes as an isolated regional shock — and underlying growth was estimated at 8%. Alternative accommodations widened their share of bookings to 38%, from 37%. The condition is comfortably above the line that would call it into question; the next filing will show whether the March softness was a single-quarter event.

The marketing bill does not run away. Marketing as a share of total bookings was 3.8% in the first quarter — the same as a year earlier. The conflict-driven cancellation hit dampened paid-channel returns intra-quarter; absent that drag the company claims modest underlying leverage from growth in its direct channel. The rule requires the ratio to rise meaningfully for two consecutive years before any clock starts; one flat year is no concern.

The cut the company takes does not shrink. The share of bookings the company kept as revenue rose to 10.3%, up from 10.2% a year earlier. The increase came from higher revenues on payments handling and favourable timing between when travellers book and when they travel. The condition is working in the right direction.

The cash keeps coming back to shareholders. Share buybacks in the first quarter ran to $3.8 billion, up 73% on the same quarter a year earlier. The quarterly dividend was raised to $0.42 per share, up 10% year-on-year. The repurchase authorisation has $18.2 billion remaining — substantial dry powder. No value-destructive acquisition was disclosed. The condition is not only intact, it is reading stronger than at any point in the recent past.

The yield is not squeezed. Free cash flow divided by the company's market value sits at 6.9% — well above the 4% line that would call the share price expensive, and almost touching the 7% line that would call it cheap. The condition is doing its job.

A credible artificial-intelligence answer. The strategy is articulated — Booking calls it the "Connected Trip" — and the mobile-app share of room-nights advanced from a mid-fifties percentage to a high-fifties percentage over the past year, with a significant majority of those mobile bookings made direct. But the filing still describes Connected Trip as a future-state vision; no measurable engagement metrics, no shipped-product direct-traffic uplift, no margin gain from artificial intelligence is yet disclosed. The autumn-2027 deadline is more than five quarters away; the clock has not started ticking, but this is the open watch item.

European regulators do not break the model. Booking.com remains designated a "gatekeeper" under the European Union's digital-markets rules and a "very large online platform" under the digital-services rules. Italy opened a fresh competition probe in April 2026; investigations in Switzerland, France, Greece, Hungary, the Netherlands and Spain continue. No formal decision imposing structural product remedies has been issued, and no fine exceeding 3% of annual global revenue has been levied. The condition is not crossed, though the regulatory cadence is elevated.

The Share Price

The share price is $169.62. The fair-value anchor — the price at which the company's free cash flow yields 5.5%, the historical centre of its trading range — is $213. The cheap line, at a 7% yield, is $168. The expensive line, at a 4% yield, is $293.

The current share price is therefore $1.62 above the cheap line and roughly 20% below fair value. In ordinary English: cheap-ish, not yet a bargain. The market is being more pessimistic about Booking than even the analysis's bear scenario would justify — implicitly pricing in long-term free-cash-flow growth of roughly 4% to 5%, below the 8% the bear case assumes. That is the kind of discount that pays the position for carrying the artificial-intelligence-disintermediation worry, and then some.

Standing orders (actionable).
- A standing buy order at $168. The rules call for adding in tranches once the share price closes at or below this line and the conditions are still working. The order may be staged across one or two quarters rather than filled in a single fill.
- A standing trim order at $293. The rules call for trimming a quarter to a third of the position when the share price closes at or above this line, to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding.
- A standing exit order at $469. The rules call for closing the full position when the share price closes at or above this line. The market is pricing in roughly 7 years of base-case compounding instantly at that reading; the implied forward return falls below the long-term equity premium even before growth, and reallocation beats continuing to hold.

The three order levels do not contradict the verdicts above: those verdicts describe the current reading; the standing orders describe what the rules will execute if the share price moves to each line.

What's Wobbling

Three readings deserve a second look, though none crosses a rule.

The first-quarter room-night softness — global growth of 6%, with March down to 1% on the conflict in the Middle East — is the "isolated regional shock" carve-out built into the first condition. The condition would only fail if the slowdown spread across multiple regions for two consecutive quarters. If growth ex-conflict snaps back to the estimated 8% trajectory in the next filing, the watch closes; if the softness drifts into Europe or Asia, the clock starts.

The marketing bill, flat at 3.8%, is not a problem in itself — flat is not rising — but two consecutive years of stability after years of modest leverage merit attention in the second half of 2026. Search-engine traffic is in explicit and expected decline; direct-channel traffic is holding but no longer growing as a share of total.

The artificial-intelligence strategy is the most important open question. Booking's "Connected Trip" framing is real; the mobile-app share is rising; direct booking behaviour in the app is sticky. But the company has not yet shown shipped product with measurable engagement, direct-traffic or margin gains from artificial intelligence. The autumn-2027 deadline gives management five quarters to convert articulation into evidence. For now it is a watch, not a wobble that calls for action.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Artificial-intelligence agents cut Booking out of the journey. Google's Gemini, OpenAI's interfaces and other agentic tools could become the front door to trip planning, routing travellers around the online travel marketplace entirely.
  • Take rate compression. If competitors, regulators or artificial-intelligence agents force commissions lower across the industry, the economics of every transaction get worse, and the cushion that supports the capital-return engine narrows.
  • A large, value-destructive acquisition. Management has been disciplined for a decade. A splashy deal that uses the cash mountain unproductively would call the capital-allocation framework into question.
  • A formal European structural remedy. A binding decision that strips price-parity or alters the auction mechanics could meaningfully impair pricing power; a fine exceeding 3% of annual global revenue in a single year would breach the seventh condition outright.
  • A demand shock that lasts. A protracted travel slowdown — from war, recession or pandemic — would compress bookings for long enough that even the buyback engine could not fully offset the per-share drag.

Gaps in the Picture

The first-quarter room-night softness includes a regional carve-out that has not yet been tested by a second quarter of data. The reading for the second condition — the marketing bill — is exactly flat year-on-year, which leaves it ambiguous between "leverage running out" and "noise in a single quarter." The artificial-intelligence strategy is, by the company's own framing, articulated rather than evidenced; the next two filings will reveal whether the gap between articulation and shipped product is closing. None of these are problems with the underlying figures themselves — they are simply places where one or two more data points would settle a question that today reads "watch."

One technical note from the calculation behind the share-price bands: the share count is taken from current market data rather than the latest filing, because Booking executed a 25-for-1 stock split that the underlying tagged filing data did not yet reflect at the time of analysis. The yield calculation is unaffected by which share count is used.
