About the Fund

All World tracks the MSCI World Index, a float-adjusted, market-capitalisation-weighted index covering roughly 1,300 large and mid-sized companies across 23 developed markets. The geographic mix is approximately 70% United States and 30% non-US developed markets — principally Japan, the United Kingdom, France, Switzerland, Germany, and Canada. This makes it meaningfully broader than a US-only large-cap index, which holds only about 500 companies drawn entirely from one country.

The fund is managed by iShares, BlackRock's exchange-traded fund business. The annual cost is 0.24% of the invested amount, deducted continuously from the fund's assets rather than billed separately — a shareholder buying $10,000 worth of the fund pays roughly $24 a year in implicit fees. Tracking-difference and tracking-error figures (measures of how closely the fund shadows the index before and after fees) are not available in this snapshot. The fund is structured as a Delaware statutory trust and does not issue the partnership tax forms (known as K-1s) that some other fund structures require; whether its dividends qualify for the lower US qualified-dividend tax rate and its history of capital-gain distributions are not confirmed in this snapshot.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that over the holding period the basket's earnings yield — the aggregate share of each company's price that its underlying businesses earn — will exceed the yield on risk-free government bonds by at least 2 percentage points per year. That gap (called the equity risk premium, defined here as basket earnings yield minus the ten-year US Treasury yield) is the financial return earned for taking on equity-market volatility rather than holding government paper. If the premium is positive and wide enough, holding the basket is rewarded. If it is zero or negative, the market is pricing equities as if they were as safe as Treasuries — which they are not.

The conditions that must keep holding for the bet to work are:

  1. Equity risk premium: The basket's earnings yield must exceed the ten-year US Treasury yield by at least 2 percentage points over time; if the premium is negative for two consecutive quarters, the risk-premium case has failed.
  2. Expense ratio: The annual management fee must stay below 0.35% (current 0.24%; early-warning flag above 0.30%).
  3. Fund size: Assets under management must stay above $2.0 billion (current $8.1 billion; early-warning flag below $4.0 billion).
  4. Single-name concentration: The largest single holding must stay below 10.0% of the basket (current NVIDIA at 5.42%; early-warning flag above 8.0%).
  5. Index methodology: The MSCI World Index must not be materially restructured in a way that undermines the developed-markets mandate — for example, reclassifying major developed-market countries as emerging markets, dropping large constituents, or switching away from float-adjusted market capitalisation.
  6. Basket quality: The median return on equity of the top ten largest holdings (deduplicated across related share classes) must not fall below 15% — a level that would signal the basket has lost its quality as a compounding vehicle (current median: 37.3%).

What's Wobbling

The equity risk premium has been negative since the first measurement and has not recovered. Six weekly snapshots, all negative, with the latest reading of −0.333 percentage points. The underlying arithmetic is simple: the basket earns about 4.15 cents per dollar of price, while a ten-year US Treasury note pays about 4.49 cents per dollar — so equity-market volatility is being accepted for a lower expected return than the government bond.

The gap is small in absolute terms, and a modest shift in either direction (basket earnings growth or a decline in Treasury yields) would restore a positive premium. But the direction has been persistent, and the primary exit condition was specifically designed to measure quarterly persistence rather than weekly fluctuations. The next formal test is the three months ending in September 2026.

What is not wobbling: the basket's underlying quality is strong. The top holdings — NVIDIA, Apple, Alphabet, Microsoft, Amazon — generate returns on their equity ranging from 24% to over 100% (the latter inflated by buybacks, as noted in the conditions above). The vehicle itself is operationally sound. The structural exit conditions (expense ratio, fund size, concentration, methodology) are all passing. The wobble is entirely in the valuation and premium dimension, not the quality or operational dimension.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Persistent negative equity risk premium. If Treasury yields stay elevated while basket earnings growth stalls, the two-quarterly-negative threshold could be met as early as the three months ending in September 2026. That would fire exit condition (1) and call for closing the position regardless of price.
  • Multiple compression. The basket currently trades at roughly 24× earnings. A return to the long-run average of 20× earnings (a contraction of four turns) would reduce the share price by approximately 17% even with no change in underlying earnings — to roughly the $168 fair-value anchor.
  • Technology sector correction. Technology accounts for 31.3% of the basket — above the single-sector 30% threshold — driven by the current dominance of US mega-cap technology companies in global float-adjusted market capitalisation. A material de-rating of technology valuations would pull the basket disproportionately.
  • Expense ratio drift. At 0.24%, the fund already runs four basis points above the equivalent UCITS-domiciled version (IWDA at 0.20%). Any upward revision would erode the cost advantage relative to switching.
  • Index methodology change. An MSCI decision to reclassify a major developed market, drop large constituents, or change the weighting methodology could alter the mandate the fund was purchased to fulfil.

(For this fund, exit fires when the share price reaches the exit edge, when a vehicle-integrity condition breaches, or when the named exit-condition trigger fires — any of three paths.)

The Rules
