About Dynatrace

Dynatrace sells software that watches other software. Specifically, it runs an AI-powered observability platform that tells enterprise technology teams — at large banks, airlines, retailers, and government agencies — exactly why their applications are slow, failing, or consuming unexpected cloud resources. The answer matters: a payments platform that is five seconds slower than it should be costs the bank money every second it stays broken; a government portal that crashes under load becomes a headline. Dynatrace tells teams not just that something is wrong but why, tracing the problem back through hundreds of interconnected services automatically.

The business model is pure subscription: enterprises pay an annual recurring fee for the right to have Dynatrace's software monitor their entire computing environment. As customers grow, add new applications, or expand into new modules — logs, security, AI governance — their spending expands without requiring a new sales conversation. Roughly 97% of revenue arrives this way, which makes the revenue base unusually visible and predictable. For the fiscal year ending March 2026, total revenue reached just over $2 billion, up 19% from the prior year.

The Case for the Bet

The central idea is that Dynatrace has built a switching cost that is genuinely difficult to escape. Its OneAgent software deploys automatically across an enterprise's entire computing environment — every application, container, and cloud function — producing a unified map of how everything connects. Replacing OneAgent means re-instrumenting the whole environment, a multi-year project that most IT organisations will not undertake unless the platform is failing them. That is the moat. As long as the moat holds, customers stay, spend more each year, and provide the recurring revenue base that compounding rests on.

The numbered conditions that must remain true for the thesis to hold:

  1. Net revenue retention rate stays at 107% or above. This is the single most important signal. Net revenue retention measures what existing customers spent this year compared with last year, expressed as a percentage. At 110%, customers collectively spent 10% more than last year — even after accounting for any who left. If this number falls below 107% for two consecutive reporting periods, the switching cost thesis is in question.

  2. Annual recurring revenue growth (measured in constant currency) stays above 13%. Annual recurring revenue — the contracted, predictable forward subscription revenue — grew 18% in the most recent fiscal year. A sustained fall below 13% for two consecutive quarters would signal that the business is growing more slowly than the competitive dynamics allow it to sustain.

  3. Cash profit margin stays above 20% for a full fiscal year. Cash profit (money generated from operations minus the capital the business spends on infrastructure) was 26% of revenue last year. A fall below 20% for a full year would indicate that the operating model is deteriorating structurally, not temporarily.

  4. No senior executive makes large open-market share sales. When the chief executive or chief financial officer sells shares on the open market — outside of tax-withholding transactions that happen automatically — it signals that they believe the stock is fairly priced or overpriced. Sales of $5 million or more within any 90-day window would cross this threshold.

  5. The platform subscription adoption rate does not stall below 72% of annual recurring revenue for three consecutive reporting periods. The platform subscription model (which gives customers a committed budget they draw down across modules) is the mechanism through which existing customers expand spending. Stalling here would suggest the expansion engine is slowing.

  6. No documented evidence that a major competitor has displaced named Dynatrace customers. Cisco (which acquired Splunk in 2024 and now runs a combined security and observability platform) and Palo Alto Networks (which acquired Chronosphere in 2026) are the specific competitors named. The exit condition requires confirmed named customer wins — not just the acquisition itself.

  7. Net cash stays above $600 million without a disclosed acquisition rationale. Dynatrace holds over $1 billion in cash against minimal debt. A decline to below $600 million without an explained strategic use would indicate capital erosion.

The Rules

If all conditions confirm: maintain the full position. Watch quarterly data for net revenue retention, annual recurring revenue growth, cash profit margin, and platform subscription adoption. Consider adding modestly on share-price dips below $38 while condition 1 holds at 107% or above.

If the thesis is weakening (one or two conditions showing stress without crossing exit thresholds): reduce to half position within 30 days of the trigger. Watch for two more quarters before deciding whether to exit fully or rebuild. The specific warning signs are: net revenue retention between 107% and 109% for one quarter; annual recurring revenue growth between 13% and 15% for one quarter; platform adoption stalling below 72%.

If the thesis is broken (any numbered exit condition is confirmed): exit fully within one quarter. Do not average down on a broken thesis.

If the share price falls to or below $31 (the cheap edge): accumulate toward a full position. Management's own buyback at an average of $38 per share in the last fiscal year sets a practical floor. Below $35, the cash profit yield rises above 6.2%, providing a compelling entry zone when the thesis is intact.

If the share price rises to or above $55 (the trim edge): trim toward a half-to-two-thirds position. At that price the share approaches the base-case estimated fair value under a discounted cash flow framework, and the remaining upside requires bull-case execution to materialise.

The Share Price

The share-price band works as follows. The fair-value anchor is set by the cash profit yield (the annual cash profit divided by the market value of the whole company). When the yield rises to 5.9% — implying a share price around $31 — the rules treat the share as cheap. When the yield falls to 3.3% — implying a price around $55 — the rules treat the share as expensive. The current yield is 4.4% at today's price of $41, which sits squarely in the middle of the hold band.

The current price of $41 is effectively at the fair-value anchor. The market appears to be pricing in roughly 10–11% long-run cash profit growth, which is below even the bear-case scenario projection of 11% annual revenue growth. Put plainly: the market is valuing Dynatrace as though it will grow more slowly than its own worst-case scenario. A separate scenario-based valuation (which applies historical and peer-based multiples to projected cash profits under each of the three scenarios) produces a wider range of estimates — from roughly $60 to well above $100 at the base and bull ends — but the yield-based anchor at $41 governs the standing orders.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • OpenTelemetry — an open-source standard for software monitoring — is gaining adoption across enterprise computing environments. If the majority of new workloads are instrumented via OpenTelemetry rather than Dynatrace's proprietary OneAgent, the switching cost advantage erodes. Datadog has invested heavily in OpenTelemetry-compatible infrastructure. Dynatrace acquired Bindplane in April 2026 specifically to add OpenTelemetry pipeline compatibility.

  • Datadog is growing faster. At roughly 28% annual revenue growth versus Dynatrace's 19%, Datadog is compounding scale advantages in developer mindshare and integrations. If Datadog captures the AI-workload monitoring market first, Dynatrace risks becoming the second choice in a market that tends toward one or two dominant platforms.

  • Cisco's unified security and observability platform, built around the 2024 Splunk acquisition, is the most resourced bundled-suite competitor. If enterprise security buyers gain control of observability budgets, Dynatrace's position in IT operations could be displaced by a platform sold from a different buyer relationship.

  • Agentic AI startups — companies building monitoring tools specifically for AI applications using newer approaches (including one called eBPF, a kernel-level instrumentation method that avoids OneAgent entirely) — are targeting the next-generation workload category that Dynatrace hopes Dynatrace Intelligence will capture. These startups have not yet achieved scale, but they are competing for the same budget.

  • The deferred tax position from an intellectual property transfer to Switzerland introduces some uncertainty in the effective tax rate. The most recent filing confirmed that a major U.S. tax law change had no material impact, but the position remains under management.
