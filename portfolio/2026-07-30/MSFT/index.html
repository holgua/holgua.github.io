About Microsoft

Microsoft makes most of its money selling software subscriptions and cloud computing capacity to large enterprises — companies and government agencies that use its products to run their day-to-day operations. The business divides roughly into three streams. The first is the productivity suite: email, document editing, videoconferencing, and the workflow tools that hundreds of millions of professionals use every working day, sold primarily as a monthly subscription called Microsoft 365. The second is the cloud-computing platform, Azure, which rents computing power, database storage, and increasingly artificial-intelligence processing capacity to businesses that prefer not to run their own data centres. The third is a mix of consumer and gaming products, from the Windows operating system to Xbox, which is profitable but not the engine of the thesis.

What makes the business unusual is the depth of the lock-in. Microsoft's identity infrastructure — the software that handles who is allowed to log in to which systems, on which devices, under what security conditions — sits inside approximately nine out of ten large Western companies. Unplugging it does not mean switching suppliers; it means simultaneously migrating every application, every device policy, and every access rule across the whole enterprise to a different platform. That kind of all-or-nothing switching cost is what sustains the margins: return on invested capital has held between 22% and 27% for five straight financial years (ending June 2021 through June 2025), well above the 8–10% cost of capital, despite a data-centre building programme that has absorbed tens of billions of dollars in each of the recent years.

The Case for the Bet

The bet on Microsoft rests on the premise that its enterprise-identity switching cost is durable enough to sustain above-average returns through an unusual capital-expenditure cycle — one that is temporarily compressing cashflow but buying contracted future revenue at scale. The conditions that must keep holding are:

  1. Azure cloud revenue grows at least 25% a year, not below this rate for two consecutive quarters while capital expenditure remains above 20% of revenue. The compound formulation matters: a slowdown alone, without the elevated spending, would not trigger a mandatory review. But a slowdown combined with continued heavy infrastructure investment would signal that the spending is failing to produce returns — permanently impairing the cashflow picture rather than temporarily delaying it.

Latest reading: Azure grew 40% in both the third quarter and cumulatively for the nine months ending 2026-03-31. The exit-condition floor of 25% is 15 percentage points away. Capital expenditure at 33–37% of revenue exceeds the 20% secondary condition, but Azure's 40% reading prevents the compound trigger from firing.

  1. The ratio of cashflow from operations minus capital expenditure to net profit does not fall below 0.65 for two consecutive full fiscal years, with no improvement in cloud-AI revenue. This condition separates a deliberate investment choice from an erosion in the underlying earnings quality. If two consecutive full years show compression and cloud-AI revenue is not growing, the bear case — that the infrastructure spending is destroying capital rather than building a moat — gains weight.

Latest reading: The nine-month ratio for the period ending 2026-03-31 is approximately 0.48, below the floor. However, the full financial year ending June 2025 came in at 0.70, above the threshold. The exit condition requires two full fiscal years, not two interim periods. The verdict arrives when the year ending June 2026 closes.

  1. Consolidated operating profit margin stays above 42% on a rolling quarterly basis, not below this for two consecutive quarters. A margin below this level for two quarters would signal that the cost of the artificial-intelligence investment cycle is exceeding the revenue it produces — structural compression rather than a timing effect.

Latest reading: Operating margin was 46.3% in the three months ending 2026-03-31 and 47.4% for the nine months. Both are well above the 42% floor and above the prior-year levels, demonstrating that software revenue is scaling faster than operating costs even as the data-centre build accelerates.

  1. The IRS transfer-pricing dispute does not escalate to Tax Court proceedings, and no single quarterly tax charge exceeds $5 billion. The current outstanding claim of $28.9 billion plus penalties covers transfer pricing for the tax years 2004 to 2013. A Tax Court escalation would move the dispute into the public litigation phase with unpredictable outcomes; a charge above $5 billion in a single quarter would signal that the balance-sheet reserve is being exhausted.

Latest reading: As of 2026-03-31, the dispute remains in administrative appeals. The quarterly income tax provision for the three months ending March 2026 was $7.6 billion, which is the standard effective-rate provision on pre-tax profit — not a discrete settlement charge. Exit condition not triggered.

  1. No formal antitrust complaint from the Federal Trade Commission seeking structural remedies such as a forced divestiture of Teams or unbundling of cloud, artificial-intelligence, and identity products. This condition monitors the primary regulatory risk that could directly impair the switching-cost moat: if identity products were required to be offered separately from cloud and productivity tools, the all-or-nothing character of the enterprise lock-in would weaken.

Latest reading: As of 2026-03-31, the Federal Trade Commission's investigation is in a documentation phase; no formal complaint has been filed. Risk Factors in the quarterly report acknowledge ongoing antitrust scrutiny in general terms but disclose no specific action seeking structural remedies.

  1. Consolidated gross margin (the proportion of revenue remaining after the direct cost of delivering products and services) stays above 65% for each consecutive pair of quarters. A sustained fall below 65% would signal that artificial-intelligence infrastructure costs are being passed through to gross margins rather than absorbed by the operating leverage of the software business.

Latest reading: Consolidated gross margin was 67.6% in the third quarter of the financial year and 68.2% for the nine months — both above the 65% floor. Microsoft's cloud gross margin, a narrower measure covering only its cloud products, was 66% for the quarter.

  1. Commercial remaining performance obligations (the contracted future revenue committed by enterprise customers but not yet recognised) grow at least 30% a year, and the absolute balance does not decline quarter over quarter. This metric is the leading indicator of how much future Azure and cloud revenue is already locked in contractually. A growth rate below 30% or an outright decline would signal weakening enterprise demand before it appears in recognised revenue.

Latest reading: Commercial remaining performance obligations reached $627 billion as of 2026-03-31, up 99% from a year earlier. The 30% growth floor is approximately 69 percentage points away from being breached.

The Share Price

At $392, the share price sits just below the trim edge of $403 — the level at which an earnings yield measured against normalised annual profit falls to 2%, the point at which the rules call for beginning to reduce the position. Expressed differently: on the profit measure this framework uses, Microsoft earns 2.06% on its current market value of roughly $2.9 trillion, against a fair-value anchor of 3.5%.

The fair-value calculation sets the buy edge at a 5.0% earnings yield and prices fair value at a 3.5% yield, implying a notional per-share fair value of approximately $230. The market is pricing the company at $392 — 70% above that anchor — reflecting expectations of continued double-digit growth that the $627 billion contract backlog and Azure's sustained 40% growth give plausible support to. A more growth-adjusted valuation model, incorporating the base-case trajectory, suggests a fair-value range of $561–$613 per share, which would make the current price look modestly conservative rather than extended. Both figures are provided as context; the operative measure for placing limit orders is the earnings yield, not the growth-adjusted model.

In short: the share is not cheap, and it is not expensive enough to trigger a rule-based reduction. It sits at the upper boundary of what the rules treat as neutral territory.

The Standing Orders

The rules translate the share-price bands into three standing orders and two price alerts. Each is the operationalisation of a pre-committed rule — not a new judgement made by any subsequent review.

Price alerts (informational).
- A first alert at $230, the fair-value anchor. A crossing below this level would mean the share price has fallen to a level where the earnings yield reaches 3.5% — the point at which the business is priced at fair value with no premium for growth. No automatic action follows, but a fresh review of the conditions is warranted before making any commitment.
- A second alert at $161, the cheap edge. A crossing to or below this level would mean the earnings yield has reached 5.0%, the level at which the rules arm the buy order. Before adding, a current re-validation should confirm no exit condition has triggered.

Standing orders (actionable).
- A standing buy order at $161. At that level, the rules call for adding in tranches once the conditions remain intact. The order is staged across one or two quarters rather than filled in a single transaction.
- A standing trim order at $403. At that level, the rules call for trimming 20–25% of the position, to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding. The trim does not imply the thesis has weakened — it is a valuation-discipline rule that fires regardless of the underlying condition readings.
- A standing exit order at $1,613. At that level, the rules call for closing the full position. The earnings yield at that price falls to 1.5%, implying the market is pricing in the full bull-case scenario (fair-value estimate of $953 per share) with approximately 17 additional years of compounding already embedded in the price. The implied forward return at that point falls below half the risk-free rate with no margin of safety remaining.

The three order levels do not contradict the current reading: those levels describe what the rules will execute if the price moves to each threshold, not a verdict on today's price.

What's Wobbling

Two of the nine thesis drivers read as mixed rather than confirming. Neither has triggered a rule-based exit, but both require monitoring at the full-year close.

The first is the ratio of cashflow from operations minus capital expenditure to net profit. For the nine months ending March 2026, this ratio was approximately 0.48 — materially below the 0.65 level that would, if repeated for a second consecutive full fiscal year with no improvement in cloud-AI revenue, require a mandatory 30-day review of the position. The reason the exit condition has not triggered is structural: the exit rule was written to require two full fiscal years below the floor (not quarterly or nine-month readings), and the full financial year ending June 2025 came in at 0.70. The verdict on whether the sequence is forming will be available when the year ending June 2026 closes. What is clear already is that the data-centre spending absorbed $80 billion in capital expenditure across nine months, and the company itself states that spending will continue to increase.

The second is the capital expenditure intensity as a proportion of revenue. The nine-month figure is 33%, and the third-quarter figure alone is 37%, both above the 30% monitoring threshold written into the thesis. The compound exit condition — capital expenditure above 20% of revenue and Azure growth below 25% for two consecutive quarters — cannot currently fire because Azure's 40% growth prevents the second element from being met. But the trajectory of spending implies that if Azure growth were to decelerate, the compound condition could come into range. For how this connects to the longer-term outlook, see The Standing Orders.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Azure decelerates faster than the contract backlog implies. The $627 billion commercial backlog is contracted future revenue, not delivered revenue. If Azure consumption growth slows because enterprise customers defer AI workload deployments or open-source alternatives reduce reliance on Microsoft's hosted models, the conversion rate could disappoint even as the backlog number looks reassuring. A sustained fall below 25% quarterly growth — the exit condition — would be the confirming signal.
  • The data-centre building cycle fails to produce adequate returns. At 33–37% of revenue, capital expenditure is running at more than double the historical rate. The uncommenced lease commitments total $196.6 billion. If AI workload demand does not scale to absorb this infrastructure, the spending will have permanently impaired cashflow without producing the contracted backlog conversion that currently justifies it.
  • The IRS dispute escalates or settles at the upper bound. The outstanding claim is $28.9 billion plus penalties and interest — potentially $40 billion or more at the upper end. A Tax Court escalation, or a settlement disclosure that exhausts the $29.3 billion unrecognised tax benefit provision, would create a near-term balance-sheet shock even if it leaves ongoing earnings power intact.
  • The Federal Trade Commission files a structural antitrust complaint. A formal complaint seeking to separate Microsoft's identity products from its cloud and productivity tools would directly attack the all-or-nothing switching cost that anchors the thesis. The investigation has been running since late 2024 with no formal complaint; the risk is real but currently low in probability.
  • Gross margin compresses below the 65% floor. The cloud gross margin is already at 66% — one percentage point above the exit-condition floor — as artificial-intelligence infrastructure costs absorb revenue. A further decline driven by cost escalation or competitive pricing pressure on Azure would breach the threshold and trigger a mandatory review.

The thesis tracks an external estimate of $37 billion (as of the filing prior to the one reviewed here); this cannot be confirmed or denied from the filing text and is therefore carried as a silent reading rather than a confirmed one.

The Rules

The following rules govern what action the framework calls for under each combination of thesis state and valuation position. They are stated directly — no other document needs to be consulted to apply them.

  • If the thesis is confirmed (all or most drivers reading as confirming or silent, no exit condition triggered): hold the position without adding. Azure growth sustained, the artificial-intelligence annual revenue is compounding, and the moat is intact. No additional sizing at or near the current price — the margin of safety is insufficient for a new commitment. The position earns its return by continuing to compound, not by recovering from distress.
  • If the thesis is weakened (one or more drivers deteriorating but no exit condition yet triggered): do not add to the position. Run a fresh thesis re-validation focused on the specific weakening driver (Azure growth, cashflow conversion, operating margin, or the contract backlog). Monitor for one additional quarter before reassessing position size. If a second consecutive quarter confirms the deteriorating signal, escalate to a thesis-broken review.
  • If the thesis is broken (any one of the seven exit conditions has triggered): exit the full position within 30 days. No averaging down. Record which exit condition fired — Azure growth below 25% for two quarters combined with capital expenditure above 20% of revenue; cashflow-to-profit below 0.65 for two fiscal years with no cloud-AI improvement; operating margin below 42% for two quarters; IRS escalation or single-quarter charge above $5 billion; Federal Trade Commission formal complaint seeking structural remedies; gross margin below 65% for two quarters; or commercial backlog growth below 30% or an outright decline.
  • If the price falls below the buy threshold (earnings yield rises above 5.0%, corresponding to approximately $161 per share on the cashflow-based earnings measure): confirm the thesis is intact before adding. If intact, add in tranches — a first tranche below $352 (40% margin of safety against the base-case fair-value midpoint of $587), a second tranche below $322 (40% margin of safety against the discounted-cashflow intrinsic value of $429). Each tranche represents 25–33% of the intended add-on allocation.
  • If the price rises above the trim threshold (earnings yield falls below 2.0%, corresponding to approximately $403 per share): trim 20–25% of the position to harvest the valuation premium. At a yield below 2.0%, the market value exceeds the base-case midpoint with limited additional upside without further Azure acceleration. Retain the remaining position if Azure growth continues confirming the base case. Do not reduce below 50% of the current holding unless a thesis exit condition is also triggered.
  • If the price rises above the exit threshold (earnings yield falls below 1.5%, corresponding to approximately $1,613 per share): exit the full position. At that yield, the position earns less than half the risk-free rate with no margin of safety. Redeploy proceeds at the next cheap-edge crossing or into a cheaper alternative. No re-entry within 90 days, to avoid anchoring to the exit price.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
