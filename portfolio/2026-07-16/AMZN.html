Amazon: The Bet Still Works

The Bottom Line

The bet is working. Amazon's cloud and advertising engines confirmed their thesis in the three months to March 2026 — revenue growing well above the thresholds that would signal trouble, operating income expanding, and no exit condition triggered. At $250, the Share Price sits in the upper half of the hold band, well above fair value. The one condition worth watching closely is whether the infrastructure spending that has been accumulating for two years begins converting to cash flow at the pace the thesis requires; the most recent quarter showed the demand is there, but free cash flow is still near zero on a trailing basis.

Buy at $140. Trim at $263. Exit at $421.

Recent Development

Amazon's latest quarterly filing — covering the three months ending March 2026 — delivered its cleanest set of numbers in several cycles. The cloud business grew 28% compared with the same quarter a year earlier, reaching $37.6 billion in sales. That is comfortably above the 18–20% range the thesis requires as evidence that demand is absorbing the enormous wave of infrastructure spending. A $364 billion committed backlog and a $100 billion expansion of the Amazon–OpenAI cloud arrangement confirm that the demand side of that equation is not the problem.

Advertising reached $17.2 billion in the same three months, up nearly 24% year on year. That growth is the main reason operating profit expanded in both the North American retail segment and the international segment — the latter earned $1.4 billion on $39.8 billion of sales, up from $1.0 billion a year earlier. All eight written exit conditions remained untriggered.

The complication is capital intensity. Cash investment in property and equipment came to $43.2 billion in a single quarter — a figure that compresses free cash flow to nearly nothing on a twelve-month trailing basis ($1.2 billion, against $25.9 billion a year earlier). Management has stated that spending will keep rising in 2026. How this resolves is the central open question for the Share Price and the Bottom Line.

About Amazon

Amazon makes money three distinct ways, and the mix matters more than the headline revenue figure. The first and largest by revenue is retailing and logistics — the familiar North American and international shopping and delivery operation, which generated roughly $588 billion in sales in the year ending December 2025. Operating margins here are modest, around 6–7% for North America and only just positive internationally, but the scale generates cash that funds the other two.

The second engine is cloud computing. Amazon Web Services rents computing, storage, and networking capacity to businesses that prefer not to run their own data centres. In 2025 that segment produced $128.7 billion in sales at a 35% operating profit margin, supplying more than half of the company's total operating income on less than a fifth of its revenue. It is the economic heart of the business.

The third engine is digital advertising. When millions of people search for products on the shopping site, that behaviour has commercial value — and Amazon sells access to it. Advertising services reached $68.6 billion in 2025, growing at 22%, with margins that, while undisclosed separately, are high. Together the three engines reinforce each other: the retail operation generates the shopping data that powers the advertisements; the advertising revenue subsidises the delivery service that attracts more shoppers; the revenue from all of it finances the cloud infrastructure. That mutual reinforcement is the flywheel the thesis is built on.

The Case for the Bet

The thesis rests on three compounding engines remaining intact and one capital-cycle risk proving manageable. All four conditions are monitored at each quarterly filing:

  1. Cloud revenue growth stays above 15% for every two consecutive quarters. The threshold of 15% is the floor below which sustained deceleration would signal that the $128 billion of annual infrastructure spending is not being absorbed into demand. The current target range is 18–20% or above.

Latest reading: 28% growth in the three months ending March 2026 ($37.6 billion vs. $29.3 billion a year earlier). A $364 billion committed backlog with a weighted-average remaining contract life of 5.5 years, plus a $100 billion OpenAI cloud expansion, provide multi-year forward visibility. Exit condition 1 is not in view.

  1. Cloud operating profit margin stays above 28% for every two consecutive quarters. A sustained margin fall below 28% would indicate that the infrastructure investment cycle has not generated sufficient pricing power or utilisation to cover its costs — that reinvestment economics have broken.

Latest reading: 37.7% in the three months ending March 2026, down from 39.4% a year earlier. The compression of roughly 1.7 percentage points is driven by infrastructure depreciation outrunning revenue — the same pattern seen in each prior capital cycle. The margin sits nearly 10 percentage points above the exit threshold; two consecutive quarters below 28% would require a roughly 10-point deterioration from current levels, not one 1.7-point move. Exit condition 2 is not triggered.

  1. Advertising revenue growth does not slow to single digits for two consecutive years. Advertising is the third compounding engine and a growing proportion of operating income in both the North American and international segments.

Latest reading: 23.9% growth in the three months ending March 2026 ($17.2 billion vs. $13.9 billion a year earlier). Single-digit growth for two consecutive full years is not in view. Exit condition 5 is not triggered.

  1. Capital spending does not stay above $120 billion in both 2027 and 2028 without a commensurate acceleration in cloud revenue. The current annual run rate is above $150 billion — above the threshold — but 2026 is not 2027. The condition fires only if both years exceed the threshold without cloud growth to justify the investment.

Latest reading: Annualised capital spending above $150 billion as of the March 2026 quarter, with management explicitly guiding for further increases in 2026. Cloud revenue at 28% growth provides the commensurate acceleration the condition requires. Exit condition 4 is not yet triggered, but the clock reaches the monitored period at the 2027 annual report.

The Share Price

The owner-earnings yield (the cash the business generates for shareholders each year, expressed as a percentage of the total market value) currently stands at 4.2%. The fair-value anchor — the yield at which the business is priced neither generously nor cheaply — is 6.0%. A buyer at today's price of $250 is paying for cloud and advertising earnings that have not yet arrived.

The buy edge sits at $140 (a yield of 7.5%), where the forward return is robust enough to offer a meaningful cushion. The trim edge sits at $263 (a yield of 4%), just above today's price. The exit edge, the point at which the market has priced in roughly 11 years of base-case compounding, sits at $421.

The share price is, by this measure, well above what the figures currently support as fair value — but not at a level that triggers any automatic action. Today's price of $250 sits $13 below the trim edge and $110 below the exit edge. The price is high; the conditions are intact; nothing in the Rules calls for selling at this level.

Why the Market Sees It Differently

The price says the business will earn more over time than the current model assumes. The gap is 42.4%: at $250, the market is paying roughly 42% more than the $176 figure that the fair-value calculation produces.

To reconcile that gap in growth terms, the market would need to believe that Amazon's long-run earnings per dollar of invested capital grow at around 8.9% a year — against the 8.0% assumed in the fair-value model. The difference is less than one percentage point, which sounds narrow. But compounded over many years, a 0.9-point gap in assumed growth rates moves fair value materially.

The market's optimism has three plausible foundations. The first is structural demand acceleration: cloud revenue grew 28% in the most recent quarter, and the $364 billion backlog plus the OpenAI arrangement suggest the addressable base for AI computing is larger than an 8% growth model assumes. The filing evidence supports this hypothesis. The second is that the margin compression currently visible in the cloud segment — 37.7% in the latest quarter versus 39.4% a year earlier — is temporary, caused by depreciation running ahead of revenue, and that margins will expand as AI capacity fills. The filing is consistent with this, though not conclusively so. The third is optionality: Amazon holds a large position in Anthropic (valued upward by $12.3 billion in the first quarter of 2026 alone) and has committed $50 billion to OpenAI, both with commercial arrangements that route cloud demand to Amazon regardless of which AI model wins. An owner-earnings model built on current cash flow does not capture this structural positioning.

For the market to be wrong, either the cloud growth rate has to slow meaningfully below 8.9% on a sustained basis, or the current infrastructure spending has to strand capacity without filling it with revenue. The filing evidence points the other way. The fair-value model is cautious rather than wrong; the market's premium is defensible, not irrational.

What's Wobbling

Four of the nine thesis drivers read as mixed rather than confirming — not broken, not confirming, but worth watching.

Cloud operating margin. The 37.7% margin in the three months ending March 2026 is 1.7 percentage points below the same quarter a year ago. The cause is deliberate: $41.5 billion of net infrastructure additions in a single quarter, more than double the year-earlier pace. The exit condition requires two consecutive quarters below 28%, which is nearly 10 points away. The direction of the compression needs to reverse before the next two annual filings, or it moves into the zone the thesis flags as serious.

Capital spending trajectory. Annualised spending above $150 billion is above the $120 billion threshold written into exit condition 4 — but the condition monitors 2027 and 2028, not 2026. Cloud growth at 28% provides the commensurate revenue acceleration the condition requires as a countervailing test. The watch period begins at the 2027 annual report, roughly eighteen months away.

Capital return discipline. No shares have been repurchased despite a $6.1 billion authorisation that has sat unused since 2022, when the share price was far lower. Instead, $15 billion went into OpenAI in the quarter, with a further $35 billion committed. The thesis characterises capital allocation as acceptable but not excellent — this pattern confirms that characterisation. No exit condition applies to capital allocation directly, but the choice to invest aggressively in AI infrastructure at these prices, rather than buying back shares, is a running question about judgement that the thesis flags for monitoring.

Tariff and marketplace exposure. The latest filing explicitly named China-based sellers as a significant source of marketplace and advertising revenue, and flagged tariff policy as a risk. In the same quarter, third-party seller services grew nearly 14% and advertising grew 24%, with North American operating income up 41% year on year. The resilience is real; the risk disclosure is also real. Exit condition 5 — advertising growth falling to single digits for two consecutive years — remains far from breach.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Cloud growth decelerates to mid-teens as Microsoft Azure and Alphabet Cloud narrow the gap in AI workload share, making the $150 billion-plus annual investment cycle impossible to justify. Free cash flow, which has already been compressed to nearly nothing, stays near zero through 2027 and 2028.
  • The Federal Trade Commission monopoly trial produces a court-ordered structural remedy — a separation of the marketplace from the retail operation, or the cloud business from the consumer business — that forces a break-up at a price that does not reflect the value of the integrated flywheel. This is an immediate exit condition with no two-quarter grace period.
  • Artificial intelligence infrastructure demand peaks or shifts to a model where compute is no longer rented from cloud providers — on-device inference, private models, or a concentrated winner-takes-all dynamic that routes most workloads to a single competitor. The backlog provides several years of protection, but beyond that window the demand assumption is untested.
  • The Bezos or executive insider selling pattern shifts: Form 144 filings exceed the 40 million share threshold in a single filing, signalling a departure from the established disposal cadence and raising questions about forward confidence at the top of the organisation.
  • Advertising revenue growth slows to single digits for two consecutive years, as competing platforms or regulatory restrictions on data use compress the transaction-intent advantage that Amazon's shopping data currently provides.

Gaps in the Picture

The owner-earnings calculation uses depreciation and amortisation as the maintenance capital proxy. Amazon's actual infrastructure depreciation ($65.8 billion in 2025) is the accumulated result of years of growth investment, not the cost of keeping the existing business running at its current scale. Using this figure as a proxy for maintenance spending substantially understates true owner earnings — the officially reported free cash flow figure of $132.8 billion is a better measure of ongoing cash generation. The valuation bands in this evaluation use the more conservative proxy; this gap means the buy and trim edges may be lower than the underlying economics justify.

The fair-value benchmark in this evaluation (a 6.0% owner-earnings yield) has not been reconciled with the peer-based valuation model, which produces a considerably higher fair-value estimate. The two models are currently signalling different things. This is flagged in the underlying analysis as requiring a Config-Review before the next re-validation.

The Rules

Six conditions govern what action the framework calls for at any reading:

  • If the thesis is confirmed — all conditions working, no exit threshold crossed — hold the position and continue compounding the cloud and advertising flywheel.
  • If the thesis is weakening — a condition deteriorating but not yet at the exit threshold — do not add to the position. Re-run the thesis check against the specific deteriorating driver after the next two quarterly filings before deciding. One quarter of data is not enough; the rules require two.
  • If the thesis is broken — any exit condition triggered — exit the full position within 30 days. No averaging down. Record which exit condition fired for future reference. For cloud revenue and margin exits, the two-quarter confirmation requirement still applies before action. For a regulatory structural remedy or auditor change, exit is immediate.
  • If the share price falls below $140 — confirm the thesis is intact and cloud growth is at or above 18% first. If confirmed, add to the position in tranches over one to two quarters.
  • If the share price rises above $263 — trim a quarter to a third of the position to harvest the premium while keeping the core holding. Re-evaluate position size after the trim.
  • If the share price rises above $421 — exit the full position. At that price, the market has priced in roughly 11 years of base-case compounding instantly. Redeploy proceeds at the next cheap-edge crossing or into a cheaper alternative. No re-entry within 90 days.

Scenario Definitions

A scenario is a joint assumption about two independent variables: operating trajectory (how the holding's underlying cash flows grow relative to their long-run trend) and exit multiple (what the market pays for those cash flows at the horizon, relative to a fair-value anchor).

Scenario Operating trajectory Exit multiple
Bear Below trend Below fair value
Base At trend At fair value
Bull Above trend At or above fair value

Non-negotiable rule: In the bear case, both dimensions must be adverse simultaneously — a scenario where only the multiple moves is a re-rating scenario, not a bear.

Bear cases are comparable in structure across all holding types (company, ETF, bond), but not in magnitude. A company bear reflects idiosyncratic execution failure; an ETF bear reflects a broad market drawdown. Different mitigations, different diversification implications.
