PayPal: The Bet Is Wobbling

The Bottom Line

The bet on PayPal Holdings is that an unloved global payments network — discounted by the stock market because checkout volume has been migrating to Apple Pay, Google Pay, and other rivals — can still compound per-share value through aggressive share buybacks at depressed prices. Most of the case still holds. Total payment volume grew 11 percent year on year, the company bought back $1.5 billion of stock in three months, and the share count is shrinking fast. But one important condition is wobbling: the operating profit margin compressed by 2 percentage points in the latest quarter, driven by a shift in business mix toward Braintree (the unbranded merchant-processing arm) and away from the higher-margin PayPal-branded checkout. The share price near $43 sits below the fair-value anchor of about $60 and at the cheap edge of the rules' acceptable range. The right discipline is to hold and watch one more quarterly filing before deciding whether the margin slip is noise or a trend.

The Verdict

The latest reading puts PayPal in "still cheap, but one condition just slipped" territory. That translates differently depending on the price paid for the shares.

  • As a fresh investment at today's price (around $43 per share). Wait. The share price sits below the bargain line of $46 — a setup that would normally argue for adding — but the margin slip means the case is no longer cleanly intact. The rules call for setting a price alert near $46 and waiting until either margin direction clarifies in the next quarterly filing or the share price falls further.
  • As an existing investment bought below $40 per share — currently sitting on a gain of about 7 percent or more. Hold. Harvesting now means walking away from a buyback engine that is shrinking the share count at 3 percent per quarter, while the company still earns roughly $6 billion of operating profit a year.
  • As an existing investment bought roughly between $40 and $50 per share — currently anywhere between a loss of about 15 percent and a gain of 7 percent. Hold. The valuation is at the cheap edge of the rules' band, but the margin question argues against adding. The two pressures cancel out into "wait for the next quarter."
  • As an existing investment bought between $50 and $60 per share — currently showing a loss of roughly 15 to 29 percent. Hold. A modest loss on a wobbling-but-not-broken case is the textbook moment to wait, not to capitulate. Selling here would crystallise a structural-bet loss that the buyback machinery is supposed to be working to repair.
  • As an existing investment bought above $60 per share — currently showing a loss of about 29 percent or more. Hold. The buyback at an average price near $44 is the cost-basis rescue mechanism the case actually relies on — selling at the bottom would undo the work the company is doing on the position's behalf.

About PayPal

PayPal is a global digital-payments network. About 439 million people and businesses have active accounts on its platform, spread across roughly 200 countries. The company makes money by taking a small slice of every transaction that flows through it. The business runs across three identifiable surfaces.

The first is the PayPal-branded checkout button — the orange-and-blue button on tens of thousands of websites' check-out pages that customers can click to pay without entering card details. This is the highest-margin part of the network and the part that has been losing share to Apple Pay, Google Pay, and merchants' direct-card flows.

The second is Braintree, an "unbranded" merchant-processing service that powers payments inside other companies' apps and websites (Uber and Airbnb are well-known examples). Braintree volumes have been growing fast, but each Braintree transaction earns the company less in margin than a PayPal-branded one because the network has to share more revenue with credit-card issuers.

The third is Venmo, a peer-to-peer payments app popular in the United States, which has been adding merchant-payment features.

The company also runs a credit business (buy-now-pay-later loans, consumer instalments, merchant cash advances) and is developing PYUSD, a regulated US-dollar stablecoin. A new chief executive, Enrique Lores, took over on 2 February 2026.

The financial backdrop is straightforward. PayPal earned about $6.07 billion of operating profit in 2025 (up 14 percent year on year), generated $5.6 billion of free cash flow, and used most of it to buy back its own shares. Total debt is modest; the balance sheet is healthy.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that the market has priced PayPal as if its checkout volume were already in steep decline, while the actual figures show a network still adding accounts, still processing more payment volume each year, and — most importantly — still throwing off enough cash to retire 8 to 10 percent of the share count every year through buybacks. The cheap price plus the disciplined share-shrinking means per-share value can compound at a double-digit rate even if total revenue only grows in the mid-single digits. The case is not that PayPal wins the checkout wars; it is that $5 to $7 billion of annual cash returned through buybacks is enough.

For the bet to keep working, the following conditions must keep holding:

  1. Branded-checkout share doesn't collapse. PayPal-branded checkout volume should not lose more than 2 percentage points of share in a single fiscal year. A bigger loss would mean the slide is structural, not cyclical, and the cash-throwing-off engine would slow.
  2. No regulatory order kneecaps a core product feature. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, the Department of Justice, or another regulator should not impose a consent order that materially restricts a core product feature (such as how transactions are presented to linked bank accounts, or how the PayPal Credit and buy-now-pay-later products work).
  3. Consumer credit losses don't spike. The consumer net charge-off rate — the share of consumer loans the company writes off each year — should not rise above 10 percent without a credible recovery path.
  4. Capital return discipline holds. Management should not abandon the buyback programme, and net debt should not rise above two times annual cash operating earnings without a compelling strategic reason.
  5. Operating profit doesn't collapse. Reported (GAAP) operating profit should not fall below $5.5 billion in any fiscal year, and management should not guide adjusted operating profit below $5.0 billion for the year ahead. Either failure would mean margin discipline has been abandoned.

The Rules

The rules that govern what action follows from each combination of conditions and share price are fixed:

  • If all conditions are intact. Hold. No action. Let the buyback engine continue compounding per-share value.
  • If any one condition is wobbling but none has clearly broken. Do not add. Watch the deteriorating condition through two consecutive quarterly filings — one quarter of weakness is not enough — before reconsidering.
  • If any one condition has clearly broken. Sell the full position within thirty days. No averaging down. Document which condition fired for future reference.
  • If the share price has dropped into the bargain range and the conditions are still intact. Add to the position in stages over one or two quarters.
  • If the share price has run into the expensive range. Trim 25 to 33 percent of the position to harvest the premium, then retain the core position for ongoing compounding.

The Conditions Today

The most recent quarterly filing — covering the three months ending in March 2026 — reads condition by condition as follows.

Branded-checkout share. The filing does not break out PayPal-branded payment volume as a separate line, so the 2-percentage-point share-loss line cannot be tested directly. What the filing does show is the source of the latest quarter's revenue increase: of the $485 million year-on-year rise in transaction revenue, roughly 85 percent came from Braintree, only $140 million from PayPal-branded, and $70 million from Venmo. That mix is consistent with the bear case — unbranded growth outpacing branded growth — but does not prove a 2-percentage-point loss. A securities lawsuit filed in February 2026 (the Darcy action) alleges that PayPal failed to disclose impediments to its branded-checkout growth strategy; this is an allegation, not a finding.

Regulatory orders. Two active information requests sit with the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau — one on consumer-protection compliance, one on the PayPal Credit and buy-now-pay-later products. Neither has progressed to a consent order. A prior shareholder lawsuit was dismissed in March 2026; the plaintiffs have appealed. The political climate has shifted toward favourable: the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau lost legislative jurisdiction over payment apps in 2025, the GENIUS Act has set a friendly framework for the company's stablecoin, and a May 2026 executive order supports a pending banking-charter application. The condition is decisively not crossed.

Consumer credit losses. Consumer credit charge-offs ran at 3.0 percent of average outstanding balances — a sharp improvement from 4.5 percent a year earlier and far below the 10 percent line. The merchant-credit charge-off rate, by contrast, jumped from 4.6 percent to 7.0 percent — a separate and smaller-dollar concern that does not feed into the written condition but is worth watching.

Capital return. The company bought back $1.5 billion of stock in the first quarter at an average price of $44.60 per share, retiring 34 million shares. The share count fell from 920 million to 892 million in a single quarter — a 3 percent reduction. The 2025 buyback authorisation still has $12.4 billion remaining. No material new debt has been taken on. The condition is met with the strongest single confirmation in the filing.

Operating profit. This is the wobble. The operating profit margin fell to 18 cents per dollar of revenue in the latest quarter, from 20 cents a year earlier. Operating profit was $1,488 million versus $1,530 million — a 3 percent decline on 7 percent revenue growth. Annualised, this still runs above the $5.5 billion floor that would clearly break the case (the run-rate works out near $6 billion), but the direction has reversed for the first time since the case was committed. Management explicitly attributes the slip to a higher transaction-expense growth rate driven by Braintree mix shift — which is the precise dynamic the case was supposed to be insulated against because the buyback was doing the per-share compounding work in spite of it.

The Share Price

At $43 the share price implies that, for every dollar paid for PayPal stock, the business currently generates about 14.8 cents of free cash flow each year. The rules anchor a fair price at 10.5 cents per dollar — roughly $60 per share — and call the shares clearly cheap when the yield reaches 12.5 cents per dollar (a price near $46) or clearly expensive when it falls to 8.5 cents (a price around $74).

Put plainly: today's price sits at the cheap edge of the band, about 28 percent below what the rules call fair. There is meaningful room for the share price to rise if the margin compression proves transient — closing the gap to fair would imply roughly a 40 percent gain. The cheap price is also the precise reason buybacks are so effective right now: every $1 billion of cash redeployed at $44 retires considerably more shares than the same dollar would have at fair value.

What's Wobbling

One condition has clearly shifted from working to wobbling, and two secondary items are worth noting.

The primary wobble is the operating profit margin. A 2-percentage-point drop in a single quarter is significant — but more important than the level is the mechanism. The decline is being driven by a structural mix shift: Braintree's unbranded volume is growing fast, but Braintree earns thinner margins per transaction than the branded checkout button. As long as branded volume grows more slowly than unbranded volume, the company's average margin will keep drifting down. The case was supposed to absorb that — the cheap valuation and the buyback machinery were the answer to flat or shrinking margins. The first quarter under new chief executive Enrique Lores is the first time the trend has actually started showing up in the reported numbers. One quarter is not a trend; the rules require the second quarter to confirm or contradict before acting.

The secondary wobbles are softer. Active-account growth slowed to a thin 0.7 percent year on year — sluggish but still positive. Payment transactions per active account fell slightly. And the merchant-credit charge-off rate worsened from 4.6 percent to 7.0 percent, a separate dynamic from the consumer side and not part of the written exit condition, but worth watching for spread.

Neither of these crosses a written line. The case has moved from "working at a deep discount" to "still working at a deep discount, with a margin question to answer."

What Could Still Go Wrong

The case for PayPal could still break in a small number of structurally distinct ways:

  • The margin slide continues for two more quarters. If the operating profit run-rate falls below the $5.5 billion line over a full fiscal year — driven by continued Braintree mix shift without offsetting cost discipline — the case has clearly broken. The rules call for selling.
  • Branded-checkout share actually does collapse. A confirmed loss of more than 2 percentage points of branded share in a single year, perhaps surfaced in the discovery phase of the Darcy lawsuit, would mean the cash-throwing-off engine slows faster than the buyback can compensate for.
  • A regulatory consent order on a core feature. A Consumer Financial Protection Bureau enforcement action that restricts how PayPal Credit, buy-now-pay-later, or Regulation E error-resolution works would erode the moat directly. The current political tailwind makes this less likely than it was 12 months ago, but the active information requests are real.
  • Buyback discipline breaks. A large acquisition, an end to the share-repurchase programme, or material new debt issued without strategic justification would each remove the engine the case actually depends on.
  • Consumer credit losses spike. A consumer recession that pushes the charge-off rate from 3 percent toward 10 percent would force write-downs and slow the cash-return engine. The current trajectory is the opposite — improving, not worsening — but the cycle can turn.

The worst plausible drawdown — combining a confirmed margin trend with a regulatory order and a soft consumer-credit cycle — could plausibly take the shares down 30 percent or more, though from already-depressed levels. The chance of permanent capital impairment over a ten-year horizon is moderate given the strength of the cash flows and the size of the buyback. The chance of a multi-year stretch of holding undervalued capital while the case proves itself one way or the other is real.

Gaps in the Picture

A few things in the underlying figures are worth flagging plainly.

The branded-checkout share figure — the metric that directly maps to the first condition — is not published by the company. The case has to rely on transaction-revenue decomposition (Braintree contributed 85 percent of the latest quarter's revenue rise) and on shareholder-lawsuit allegations as indirect signals. The exact line in the first condition cannot be tested mechanically.

The 2025 annual operating-profit figure of $6.07 billion was up 14 percent year on year — but that comparison stops at the end of December. The first-quarter 2026 reversal is the first sign the trend has changed direction. One quarter is not a trend; the second quarter will start to clarify.

The new chief executive, Enrique Lores, has been in role for less than five months. Any read on his capital-allocation discipline, his approach to the margin-mix question, or his willingness to make structural choices is necessarily provisional. The next two earnings cycles will start to fill in the picture.

The merchant-credit charge-off rate jumped to 7.0 percent from 4.6 percent — a meaningful deterioration on a small base. The written condition addresses consumer credit losses, not merchant credit losses, so this does not directly trigger any rule. But it is the kind of small detail that often precedes broader credit-cycle pressure, and it sits in the "watch but do not act" pile.
