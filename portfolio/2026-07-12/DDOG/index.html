Recent Development

The three months ending in March 2026 were the strongest quarter Datadog has reported on its primary growth metrics: revenue reached $1,006 million, up 32% from a year earlier, with roughly three-quarters of that increase coming from existing customers expanding their usage rather than new customers being added. Net retention — the measure of how much the existing customer base grows its spending each year — improved from the high-110%s to the low-120%s over the trailing twelve months, a clear re-acceleration of the land-and-expand flywheel that sits at the centre of the thesis.

Enterprise penetration continued to deepen. Customers spending $100,000 or more per year numbered 4,550 as of March 2026, up 21% from 3,770 a year earlier, and this cohort now accounts for 90% of total recurring revenue, up from 88%. Product adoption widened at the same pace: 11% of customers now use ten or more products, up from 6% a year ago, and 56% use four or more, up from 51%. Each additional product a customer adopts raises the cost and complexity of switching to an alternative.

The one exception was cash-flow margin. Free cash flow for the quarter was $289 million on $1,006 million of revenue — a margin of 29%, down from 32% in the same quarter a year earlier. The compression was driven by $42 million in higher cloud-hosting costs and increased headcount. The margin remains well above the 25% watch threshold, and the year-over-year decline is at least partly seasonal, since onboarding costs tend to cluster in the first quarter. No exit condition was triggered. The full case for why the business is structured the way it is lives in the Case for the Bet; the current share price is discussed in the Share Price.

About Datadog

Datadog is a software company that sells a single platform for monitoring the health and behaviour of digital infrastructure. Its customers — mostly large enterprises running complex cloud environments — pay a subscription to connect Datadog's software agents to their servers, containers, databases, and application code. Those agents collect measurements continuously: how fast each request is, where errors occur, what the security posture looks like, how much each resource costs. Datadog correlates all of that data in one place, so an engineer who sees a slowdown in one service can trace it back to a bottleneck in another without switching between tools.

The business model is usage-based: the more infrastructure a customer runs, the more data Datadog collects, and the higher the bill. This means revenue grows automatically as customers' operations grow — a structure that explains the roughly 120% net retention figure. Datadog makes its money not primarily by winning new logos but by growing inside logos it already has. The company's platform now spans 23 products, from infrastructure monitoring to security to AI observability, and the depth of adoption within each customer base is a direct measure of how hard it would be to replace. A customer running ten Datadog products across a multi-cloud environment would face months of engineering work to replicate that coverage with alternatives.

The Case for the Bet

The bet is that Datadog's switching-cost franchise is durable enough, and its land-and-expand economics are strong enough, to justify a position — but only at a price low enough to compensate for the genuine uncertainties about where the competitive ceiling sits. Six conditions must keep holding for the thesis to remain intact:

  1. Net retention above 115%, with exit risk at or below 112%. Net retention is the single most important metric: if existing customers are spending more each year, the franchise is compounding. The exit condition fires if this measure falls below 112% for two consecutive quarters — the point at which hyperscaler substitution or AI-native spend optimisation would be demonstrably taking hold.

Latest reading: Low-120%s trailing twelve months as of March 2026. Up from high-110%s a year earlier. Re-accelerating, not slowing. No approach to the 112% exit threshold.

  1. Gross margin above 78%, with exit risk below 77%. Gross margin (the share of each revenue dollar left after paying cloud-hosting and direct service costs) needs to stay above 78% as a watch threshold and above 77% for two consecutive quarters to avoid triggering the exit condition. Compression below these levels would signal that rising third-party cloud costs are being absorbed rather than passed through, structurally impairing the economics per customer.

Latest reading: 79% in both the three months to March 2026 and the same period a year earlier. Stable and above both thresholds.

  1. Stock-based compensation below 25% of revenue. Datadog compensates engineers heavily in stock rather than cash. That stock compensation is a real cost to shareholders — it dilutes their ownership — even though it does not reduce the reported free-cash-flow figure. If stock compensation rises above 25% of revenue with no clear plan to bring it down within two years, the gap between free cash flow and genuine shareholder earnings becomes too wide to bridge with a growth premium.

Latest reading: 19.6% for the three months to March 2026, improved from 21.6% a year earlier. Revenue is growing faster than the stock-compensation expense. Below the 25% watch threshold.

  1. Revenue growth above 20% for two consecutive quarters. The thesis requires that the installed base's natural expansion — more cloud workloads, more products adopted per customer — sustains revenue growth above 20%. A sustained decline below that level would suggest either competitive displacement or a structural slowdown in cloud infrastructure investment.

Latest reading: 32% for the three months to March 2026. Above the threshold and accelerating.

  1. No single acquisition above $2 billion. Datadog has made its acquisitions small and targeted, typically paying between $10 million and $220 million to bring specific technologies in-house. A deal above $2 billion at a growth-company multiple would introduce integration risk and capital-allocation uncertainty that the current earnings base cannot absorb comfortably.

Latest reading: Total acquisitions in the twelve months to January 2026 were $118 million. No large deal disclosed in the March 2026 filing.

  1. No material regulatory enforcement on cross-border data or AI compliance. Datadog collects telemetry data from customer infrastructure that may include personally identifiable information flowing across borders. A material fine under European data-protection rules, a forced market exit from a significant revenue geography, or a compliance shutdown under emerging AI regulation affecting more than 5% of revenue would trigger this condition.

Latest reading: Extensive disclosure on applicable regulations — European data-protection rules, emerging AI regulation, national cybersecurity directives — with no enforcement actions, fines, or market-exit notices as of March 2026.

The Share Price

At $258, Datadog's share price sits above the trim line and well above fair value. The fair-value anchor used here is 2.5% — the level of annual earnings yield (post-compensation earnings as a share of market value, after deducting stock-based compensation) at which the stock would trade at what the analysis considers a neutral, watch-zone price. At current earnings, that corresponds to roughly $28 per share.

The current yield is 0.27%, meaning the market is pricing Datadog at approximately nine times the level at which a value-oriented framework would call the share price fair. The gap is large enough that calling it a precision measurement would be misleading — the more useful observation is that at $258, virtually every dollar paid represents a bet on future earnings growth materialising into permanent cash flow, rather than a payment for earnings already in hand.

The fair-value estimate derived from post-compensation earnings at a 2.5% yield is more conservative than the separate scenario analysis described in Gaps in the Picture, which produces a central estimate closer to $103 per share using a multi-method peer-comparison approach. Even against the more generous $103 figure, the current price represents a premium of roughly 150%.

What's Wobbling

One reading was mixed rather than clean: free-cash-flow margin compressed from 32% in the three months to March 2025 to 29% in the same period this year. The cause was $42 million in higher third-party cloud-hosting costs and higher headcount. The margin at 29% remains above the 25% watch threshold, and the compression pattern is consistent with first-quarter seasonality, when onboarding and infrastructure costs tend to be highest. No exit condition is triggered.

The more substantive wobble is not in the operating metrics but in the valuation signal. A 2.5% post-compensation earnings yield implies a fair-value price of about $28 per share — a figure that sits 89% below the current price. A separate scenario-based analysis using discounted cash flows and peer multiples produces a central estimate closer to $103 per share. Both anchors sit far below $258. The hyperscaler competition dynamic — Amazon Web Services, Azure Monitor, and Google Cloud's native monitoring tools offering similar functionality to single-cloud customers at near-zero incremental cost — has not produced a measurable impact on Datadog's retention or revenue growth yet, but it remains a structural ceiling risk that the filing's boilerplate competitive disclosures do not illuminate.

What Could Still Go Wrong

  • Net retention compresses to 110%. AI-native customers on large contracts are already disclosing spend optimisation as a risk in their own communications, and the filing flags that several of Datadog's largest AI-native customers may reduce usage or fail to renew. If retention falls to the high-100%s, the compound-growth engine slows materially — and at $258 per share, there is no valuation cushion to absorb a slowdown.
  • Cloud-hyperscaler substitution takes hold in the mid-market. Roughly 70% of customers running a single cloud provider can get adequate monitoring from their cloud provider's native tool at no additional cost. If Datadog's gross retention starts weakening in the sub-$100,000 customer segment, the enterprise-heavy cohort that currently drives 90% of revenue may not be large enough to sustain the current growth trajectory.
  • Gross margins continue to compress. The decline from 82% in early 2024 to 79% in early 2026 is modest, but the direction matters. Rising infrastructure costs that Datadog cannot fully pass through to customers would eventually squeeze the cash-flow margins that justify the platform's premium valuation.
  • A large acquisition disrupts the model. Datadog's disciplined track record of small, targeted deals is part of the thesis. A single transaction above $2 billion would introduce integration complexity and likely require issuing shares at a valuation that dilutes existing holders significantly.
  • Regulatory enforcement on cross-border data. The EU Artificial Intelligence Act entered force in 2024 and imposes significant obligations on AI systems; Datadog's LLM Observability product sits squarely in this regulatory perimeter. A material fine or a required product modification affecting a meaningful share of European revenue would alter the economics of the AI-observability wedge.

Gaps in the Picture

Two fair-value anchors coexist in the underlying analysis and diverge significantly. The first — derived from a post-compensation earnings yield threshold of 2.5%, using post-stock-compensation earnings of roughly $250 million — produces a fair-value price of approximately $28 per share. The second — derived from the scenario-based analysis using discounted cash flows and peer multiples — produces a central estimate of approximately $103 per share. The gap between them reflects the fact that the first method deducts stock-based compensation as a cash cost (which it economically is) while the discounted-cash-flow method uses the raw free-cash-flow figure before that deduction. A future schema revision will resolve the divergence. Until then, both anchors are noted: even the more generous $103 estimate implies a premium of roughly 150% at the current price.

Free-cash-flow margin for the trailing twelve months is approximately 25.5%, derived from $937 million in free cash flow on $3.67 billion of revenue — both figures from the market-data snapshot. The FY2025 annual margin was 29.2%. The difference reflects the period over which the calculation is taken; neither figure is wrong, but the trailing-twelve-month figure is more conservative.
